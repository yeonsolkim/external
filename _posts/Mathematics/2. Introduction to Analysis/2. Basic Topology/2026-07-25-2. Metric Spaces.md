**Definition 2.2.1.** Let $P$ and $R$ be a point sort and a scalar sort, respectively. Then, we let

$$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{met}} = \left\{ P,R,\, 0_R,+_R,<_{R},\, d \right\},$$

where $0_R$ is a constant of sort $R, +_R:R^2\to R, <_{R}\subseteq R^2,$ and $d:P^2\to R$ is a function symbol. An $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{met}}$-structure therefore has the form

$$\mathcal M = \left( M,S;\, 0^{\mathcal M},+^{\mathcal M},<^{\mathcal M},d^{\mathcal M} \right),$$

where $M$ is the set of points and $S$ is the set in which distances take their values. We henceforth suppose that the scalar sort is interpreted as the ordered additive structure of the real numbers. That is,

$$\mathcal M_{(M,d)} = (M,\mathbb R;\,0,+,<,d).$$

<br>

**Definition 2.2.2.** $\mathcal M_{(M,d)}$ is a *metric space* if it satisfies the following sentences, where $x,y,z$ range over the point sort $P\!:$

1. $\forall x,y\ (0\le d(x,y)),$
2. $\forall x,y\ (d(x,y)=0\leftrightarrow x=y),$
3. $\forall x,y\ (d(x,y)=d(y,x)),$
4. $\forall x,y,z\ \left(d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)\right).$
{:marker-prefix="M" }

In this case, $d$ is called the *metric* on $M.$<br>
<br>

**Definition 2.2.3.** A set $S\subseteq \mathbb R^k$ is *convex* if 

$$\forall x,y\in S\ \forall \lambda \in (0,1)\ \left( \lambda x + (1-\lambda)y\in S\right).$$

<br>

**Example 2.2.4.** Let $a_i<b_i$ for $i=1,\dots, k,$ and define $S$ to be the set of all points $x=(x_1,\dots,x_k)$ in $\mathbb R^k$ such that $x_i\in [a_i,b_i]$ for $i=1,\dots, k.$ The set $S$ is called a $k$-*cell*.<br>
&emsp;Let $x,y\in S, \lambda \in (0,1),$ and $z_i = \lambda x_i + (1-\lambda)y_i.$ Since 

$$ \lambda a_i\le \lambda x_i \le \lambda b_i\quad \text{and} \quad (1-\lambda)a_i\le (1-\lambda)y_i\le (1-\lambda)b_i,$$

we have $a_i\le z_i\le b_i,$ so that $z_i\in S.$ Thus $S$ is convex.<br>
&emsp;In general, $k$-cells are convex.<br>
<br>

**Definition 2.2.5.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space. For $a\in M$ and $r>0$, the *open ball of radius $r$ centered at* $a$ is the set $B_r(a)$ defined by

$$B_r(a)=\{x\in M:d(x,a)<r\}.$$

The corresponding *closed ball* and *punctured open ball* are

$$\overline B_r(a)=\{x\in M:d(x,a)\le r\} \quad \text{and} \quad B_r^{\ast}(a)=B_r(a)\setminus\{a\}.$$

<br>

**Definition 2.2.6.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space. For $a\in M,$ a *neighborhood of* $a$ is a subset $N$ of $M$ satisfying $B_r(a)\subseteq N$ for some $r>0.$<br>
<br>

**Definition 2.2.7.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space, let $A\subseteq M$, and let $a\in M$. The metric induces several local relations between the point $a$ and the set $A$.
1. $a\in \operatorname{int}A \iff \exists r>0 \ \left(B_r(a)\subseteq A\right).$ 
2. $a\in \partial A \iff \forall r>0\ \left( B_r(a)\cap A\neq\varnothing  \land  B_r(a)\cap A^c\neq\varnothing \right).$
3. $a\in\operatorname{ext}A \iff  \exists r>0\ \left(B_r(a)\cap A=\varnothing\right).$  
4. $a\in A' \iff \forall r>0 \ \left(B_r^{\ast}(a)\cap A\neq\varnothing\right),$
5. $a\in \overline A \iff \forall r>0 \ \left(B_r(a)\cap A\neq\varnothing\right).$

<br>

**Theorem 2.2.8.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M.$ Every point of $M$ is either an interior point of $A,$ an exterior point of $A,$ or a boundary point of $A,$ and hence

$$M = \operatorname{int}A \ \dot{\cup} \ \partial A\  \dot{\cup} \ \operatorname{ext}A.$$

*Proof.*

<br>

**Definition 2.2.9.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M$.
1. The set $A$ is *open* if $A\subseteq \operatorname {int}A.$
2. The set $A$ is *closed* if $A'\subseteq A.$
3. The set $A$ is *perfect* if it is closed and has no isolated points.
4. The set $A$ is *dense in* $M$ if $M\subseteq \overline A.$ 
5. The set $A$ is *bounded* if $A\subseteq B_r(x)$ for some $r>0$ and $x\in M.$

<br>

**Example 2.2.10.** Let $A=\lbrace 1/n : n\in \mathbb N_{>0}\rbrace \subseteq \mathbb R.$ Its interior relative to $\mathbb R$ is empty. Indeed, fix $a=1/n\in A,$ and let $r>0.$ Choose an irrational number $t$ with $0<|t|<r.$ Then $a+t\in B_r(a)$ but $a+t\notin A.$ Thus no point of $A$ is an interior point. Hence $a\in \operatorname {int} A$ means more than merely $a\in A.$ It means that membership in $A$ persists for all points sufficiently close to $a.$<br>
&emsp;The point $0$ is a limit point of $A.$ Indeed, for every $r>0,$ Theorem 1.1.9 gives $n\in \mathbb N_{>0}$ such that $1<nr.$ Consequently, 

$$ \frac 1 n \in B_r^{\ast}(0)\cap A,$$ 

so $0\in A'.$ On the other hand, each $1/n$ is isolated in $A.$ Moreover, any $x\notin A\cup \lbrace 0\rbrace$ has a sufficiently small ball disjoint from $A.$ Hence $A' = \lbrace 0\rbrace.$ <br>
<br>

**Example 2.2.11.** Let $\mathbb C$ with its usual metric $d(x,y) = |x-y|$ be a metric space, and let $A = \lbrace x\in \mathbb C : |x|<1\rbrace.$ Then the point $(1,0)$ is a limit point of $A,$ while $(1,0)\notin A.$ Thus $A$ does not contain all of its limit points, so $A$ is not closed. Since being closed is a necessary condition to be perfect, $A$ is also not perfect.<br>
On the other hand, $A$ is open. Indeed, if $x\in A$ then, for some $r>0,$ $B_r(x)\subseteq A.$ It follows that $x\in \operatorname {int} A.$ Thus $A\subseteq \operatorname {int} A,$ so that $A$ is open. Furthermore, since $A\subseteq B_1(0),$ $A$ is bounded.<br>
<br>

**Theorem 2.2.12.** Every open ball is open.<br><br>
*Proof.* Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space. Then let $a\in M$ and $r>0$ be arbitrary, and let $A=B_r(a).$ We show that $x\in A$ implies $x\in \operatorname {int} A.$ If $x\in A$ then $d(x,a)<r.$ We choose $s>0$ so that $s<r-d(x,a).$ Since, for any $y\in M,$ 

$$ d(y,x)<s \quad \Longrightarrow \quad d(y,a)\le d(y,x)+d(x,a)<s+d(x,a)<r,$$ 

we have $B_s(x)\subseteq A.$ Hence $x\in \operatorname {int} A.$ Therefore it is concluded that $A\subseteq \operatorname {int} A$ and $A$ is open. <span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>
<br>

**Proposition 2.2.13.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space, and let $A\subseteq M,$ then
1. $\overline A$ is closed,
2. $\overline A \subseteq X$ for every closed set $X\subseteq M$ such that $A\subseteq X.$

*Proof.* (1) Let $x\notin \overline A.$ Since $\overline A = A\cup A',$ there exists $r>0$ such that $B_r(x)\cap A = \varnothing.$  We show that there exists $s>0$ such that $B_s(x)\cap \overline A=\varnothing.$ Equivalently, we show that there exists $s>0$ such that 

$$ y\in B_s(x) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \exists t>0\ (B_t(y)\cap A = \varnothing).$$

Put $s=r/2$ and $t=r/2.$ Then, $d(z,x)\le d(y,x)+d(z,y)<r,$ for every $z\in B_t(y).$ Hence $B_t(y)\cap A = \varnothing$ so that $B_s(x)\cap \overline A=\varnothing.$ That is, $x$ is not a limit point of $\overline A.$ Consequently, every limit point of $\overline A$ is in $\overline A,$ and hence $\overline A$ is closed.<br>
&emsp;(2) Let $x$ be a limit point of $A.$ Since $A\subseteq X,$ that $B^{\ast}_r(x)$ meets $A$ for all $r>0$ implies that $B^{\ast}_r(x)$ meets $X$ for all $r>0.$ Hence $x$ is a limit point of $X.$ Since $X$ is closed, $x\in X$ so that $A'\subseteq X.$ Therefore $\overline A = A \cup A'\subseteq X.$<span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>
<br>

**Theorem 2.2.14.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space, and let $A\subseteq M$. If $a\in A'$, then, for every $r>0$, the set $B_r(a)\cap A$ is infinite.<br><br>
*Proof.* Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists $r>0$ such that $B_r(a)\cap A$ is finite. Then $B_r^{\ast}(a)\cap A$ is also finite. Since $a\in A'$, this set is nonempty. Thus, for some $n\in\mathbb N$, we may write

$$  
B_r^{\ast}(a)\cap A=\lbrace p_1,\ldots,p_n\rbrace .  
$$

Define

$$  
s=\min_{1\leq i\leq n} d(a,p_i).  
$$

Since $p_i\neq a$ for every $i$, we have $d(a,p_i)>0$, and hence $s>0$. Moreover, since every $p_i\in B_r(a)$, we have $s<r$.<br>
&emsp;We now claim that $B_s^{\ast}(a)\cap A=\varnothing.$ Indeed, if $x\in B_s^{\ast}(a)\cap A$ then we have $x\in B_r^{\ast}(a)\cap A,$ since $s<r.$ Thus $x=p_i$ for some $i,$ and

$$  
s \le d(a,p_i) = d(a,x),
$$

which contradicts $x\in B^{\ast}_s(a).$ Therefore $B_s^{\ast}(a)\cap A=\varnothing$, contradicting $a\in A'$. Hence it is concluded that $B_r(a)\cap A$ is infinite for every $r>0$. <span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>
<br>

**Theorem 2.2.15.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space. A set $A\subseteq M$ is open if and only if $A^c$ is closed.<br><br>
*Proof.* Suppose that $A$ is open and $x\in A.$ Then we can choose $r>0$ so that $B_r(x)\subseteq A.$ It follows that $B^{\ast}_r(x)\cap A^c = \varnothing,$ so $x$ is not a limit point of $A^c.$ Hence every limit point of $A^c$ is a member of $A^c,$ and thus $A^c$ is closed.<br>
&emsp;Conversely, suppose that $A^c$ is closed and let $x\in A.$ Then $x$ is not a limit point of $A^c.$ Hence we can choose $r>0$ so that $B^{\ast}_r(x)\cap A^c=\varnothing.$ It follows that $B_r(x)\subseteq A.$ Therefore $A$ is open.<span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>
<br>

**Theorem 2.2.16.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space, and let $A_i\subseteq M$ for every $i\in I.$ 
1. If $A_i$ is open for every $i\in I,$ then $\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i$ is open.
2. If $A_i$ is closed for every $i\in I,$ then $\bigcap_{i\in I } A_i$ is closed.

*Proof.* (1) Let $x\in \bigcup_{i\in I } A_i.$ Then we may let $x\in A_i$ for some $i\in I.$ Since $A_i$ is open, there exists $r>0$ such that $B_r(x)\subseteq A_i.$ Since $A_i\subseteq \bigcup_{i\in I } A_i,$ we have $B_r(x)\subseteq \bigcup_{i\in I } A_i.$ Therefore $\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i$ is open.<br>
&emsp;(2) By Theorem 2.2.15, $A_i^c$ is open for every $i\in I.$ Then $\bigcup_{i\in I } A^c_i$ is open by (1). Since 

$$\bigcup_{i\in I } A^c_i = \lowparen{\bigcap_{i\in I } A_i}^{\!\!c},$$

$\bigcap_{i\in I } A_i$ is closed by Theorem 2.2.15.<span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>
<br>

**Theorem 2.2.17.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space, and let $A_i\subseteq M$ for every $i\in I$ where $I$ is a finite set. 
1. If $A_i$ is open for every $i\in I,$ then $\bigcap_{i\in I } A_i$ is open.
2. If $A_i$ is closed for every $i\in I,$ then $\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i$ is closed.

*Proof.* (1) Let $x\in \bigcap_{i\in I } A_i.$ Then, for every $i\in I,$ we have $x\in A_i.$ Since $A_i$ is open, there exists $r_i>0$ such that $B_{r_i}(x)\subseteq A_i.$ We let 

$$ r = \min_{i\in I}r_i.$$

Then $B_r(x)\subseteq A_i$ for every $i\in I,$ so that $B_r(x)\subseteq \bigcap_{i\in I } A_i.$ Hence $\bigcap_{i\in I } A_i$ is open.<br>
&emsp;(2) By Theorem 2.2.15, $A_i^c$ is open for every $i\in I.$ Then $\bigcap_{i\in I } A^c_i$ is open by (1). Since 

$$\bigcap_{i\in I } A^c_i = \lowparen{\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i}^{\!\!c},$$

$\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i$ is closed by Theorem 2.2.15.<span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>
<br>

**Remark 2.2.18.** The necessity of $I$'s being finite in the preceding theorem can be illustrated by the following example. For $n\in \mathbb N_{>0},$ we let 

$$ A_n = \left( -\frac 1 n, \frac 1 n\right).$$

Then $A_n$ is open in $\mathbb R.$ However $\bigcap_{n\in \mathbb N_{>0}}A_n$ consists of a single point, namely, $x=0,$ and is therefore not open in $\mathbb R.$ Similarly, the union of an infinite set of closed sets need not be closed.<br>
<br>

**Theorem 2.2.19.** Let $A$ be a nonempty subset of $\mathbb R$ which is bounded above, and let $\alpha = \sup A.$ Then $\alpha \in \overline A,$ and thus $\alpha \in A$ if $A$ is closed.<br><br>
*Proof.* Since $\alpha$ is the supremum for $A,$ for every $r>0,$ there exists $x\in A$ such that $0\le \alpha -x <r.$ Hence $\alpha \in A$ or $\alpha\in A'.$ Hence $\alpha \in \overline A.$<span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>
<br>

**Definition 2.2.20.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq E \subseteq  M.$ $A$ is *open relative to* $E$ if

$$ \forall x \in A\ \exists r>0\ (B_r(x)\cap E \subseteq A).$$

<br>

**Theorem 2.2.21.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq E\subseteq M.$ Then, $A$ is open relative to $E$ if and only if there exists an open set $X\subseteq M$ such that $A = X\cap E.$<br><br>
*Proof.* Suppose that $A$ is open relative to $E.$ Let $x\in A$ and define $r_x$ to be a positive real such that $B_{r_x}(x)\cap E \subseteq A.$ Then we define

$$ X = \bigcup_{x\in A}B_{r_x}(x).$$

Since every open ball is open, $X$ is open by Theorem 2.2.16.<br>
&emsp;Since $x\in B_{r_x}(x)$ for all $x,$ we have $A\subseteq X \cap E.$ We also have $X\cap E\subseteq A$ since $B_{r_x}(x)\cap E \subseteq A$ for all $x.$ Thus $A=X\cap E.$<br>
&emsp;Conversely, suppose that a set $X$ is open and $X \cap E=A.$ Then, for every $x\in A,$ there exists $r>0$ such that $B_r(x)\subseteq X.$ It follows that $B_{r}(x)\cap E\subseteq A.$ Thus $A$ is open relative to $E.$<span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>
