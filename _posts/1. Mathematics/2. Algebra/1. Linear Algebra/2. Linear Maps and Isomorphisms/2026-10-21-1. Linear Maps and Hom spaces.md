We suppose that $V$ and $W$ are vector spaces over the same field $F$, and $\tau$ is a function from $V$ to $W$. In this situation, $\tau$ is said to preserve addition if $\tau(u)=s$ and $\tau(v)=t$ implies $\tau(u+v)=s+t$ for all $u,v\in V$ and $s,t\in W$. Similarly, $\tau$ is said to preserve scalar multiplication if $\tau(v)=w$ implies $\tau(av)=aw$ for all $a\in F$, $v\in V$, and $w\in W$. If $\tau$ preserves both operations, then it is said to be *linear*. More generally, functions that preserve the operations defining an algebraic structure are called *homomorphisms*.

**Definition 2.1.1.** Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over the same field $F$. A function $\tau\colon V\to W$ is called a *linear map* from $V$ to $W$ if for all $u,v\in V$ and $a\in F$ the following conditions hold: 
1. $\tau(u+v)=\tau(u) +\tau(v)$; 
2. $\tau(av) = a\tau(v)$.

The set of all linear maps from $V$ to $W$ is denoted by $\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$.
A linear map from $V$ to $V$ is called a *linear operator* on $V$. The *identity operator* $\iota_V$ on $V$ is a linear operator on $V$ such that $\iota_V(v)=v$ for all $v\in V$. The set of all linear operators on $V$ is denoted by $\operatorname{End}(V)$. A linear operator on a real vector space is called a *real operator*.

**Proposition 2.1.2.** Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $F$, and let $\tau\colon V \to W$ be a function.
1. If $\tau$ is linear, then $\tau(0_V)=0_W$.
2. If $\tau$ is linear, then $\tau(-v)=-\tau(v)$ for all $v\in V$.
3. The function $\tau$ is linear if and only if, for all $u,v\in V$ and $a\in F$, we have 
   
    $$\tau(au+v)=a\tau(u)+\tau(v).$$ 

*Proof.* (1) We have $v+0_V=v$ for all $v\in V$. Since $\tau$ is linear, $\tau(v) + \tau(0_V) = \tau(v)$. Since the zero vector in $W$ is unique, $\tau(0_V)=0_W$.
(2) Since $v+(-v)=0_V$, we have $\tau(v)+\tau(-v)=0_W$ by (1). Since the inverse of a vector is unique, $\tau(-v)=-\tau(v)$.
(3) If $\tau$ is linear, then $\tau(au+v)=\tau(au) + \tau(v)=a\tau(u) + \tau(v)$.
We now prove the converse. Setting $a=1$, we obtain $\tau(u+v)=\tau(u)+\tau(v)$. By (1), setting $v=0_V$ gives $\tau(au)=a\tau(u)$. Therefore $\tau$ is linear.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

We first look at some examples of real operators.

**Example 2.1.3.** For any $\theta\in \mathbb R$, we define $\tau_{\theta}\colon \mathbb R^2\to \mathbb R^2$ by 

$$ \tau_{\theta}(x,y) = (x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta, x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta).$$

The operator $\tau_{\theta}$ is linear. Indeed, we have 

$$ \begin{align}
\tau_{\theta}(a(x,y)+(z,w))&= \tau_{\theta}(ax+z,ay+w) \\
&= ((ax+z)\cos\theta - (ay+w)\sin\theta, (ax+z)\sin\theta + (ay+w)\cos\theta)\\
&= a(x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta, x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta) + (z\cos\theta - w\sin\theta, z\sin\theta + w\cos\theta) \\
&= a\tau_{\theta}(x,y) + \tau_{\theta}(z,w).
\end{align}
$$

The real operator $\tau_{\theta}$ is called the *rotation* by $\theta$ in dimension $2$.


**Example 2.1.4.** We define $\tau\colon \mathbb R^2\to \mathbb R^2$ by $\tau(x,y) = (x,-y)$. Then $\tau$ is a real operator, and it is called the *reflection* about the $x$-axis, the set $\{(x,y)\in \mathbb R^2 : y=0\}$.

**Example 2.1.5.** We define $\tau\colon \mathbb R^2\to \mathbb R^2$ by $\tau(x,y)=(x,0)$. Then $\tau$ is a real operator, and it is called the *projection* on the $x$-axis.

In real analysis, differentiation and integration provide us with two of the most important examples of linear map.

**Example 2.1.6.** Let $C^{\infty}$ denote the set of all real-valued functions defined on $\mathbb R$ that have derivatives of every order. Then $C^{\infty}$ is a vector space. 
Define $D\colon C^{\infty}\to C^{\infty}$ by $Df=f'$. Then, for $f,g\in C^{\infty}$, 

$$ D(af+g) = af'+g' = aDf + Dg.$$

Hence $D$ is linear. The linear operator $D$ is called a *differential operator*.

**Example 2.1.7.** Let $I$ be an interval, and define $\tau\colon C^1(I)\to \mathbb R$ by 

$$ \tau(f) =\int_I f(x)\,dx.$$

Then $\tau$ is a linear map.

**Theorem 2.1.8.** Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces, let $B$ be a basis for $V$, and let $\tau, \sigma\in \operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$. If $\tau(b)=\sigma(b)$ for all $b\in B$, then $\tau=\sigma$.

**Theorem 2.1.9.** Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces, and let $\tau\in \operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$. 
1. If $U$ is a subspace of $W$, then $\tau^{-1}(U)$ is a subspace of $V$.
2. If $U$ is a subspace of $V$, then $\tau(U)$ is a subspace of $W$.

*Proof.* (1) Since $\tau(0_V)=0_W$ and $0_W\in U$, $0_V\in \tau^{-1}(U)$. Let $u,v\in \tau^{-1}(U)$. Then we have $\tau(u)\in U$ and $\tau(v)\in U$. Since $U$ is a subspace, $\tau(u)+\tau(v)\in U$. Since linearity gives $\tau(u+v)=\tau(u)+\tau(v)$, $u+v\in \tau^{-1}(U)$. Similarly, closedness of $U$ under scalar multiplication and linearity of $\tau$ gives $av\in \tau^{-1}(U)$, where $a\in F$. By Theorem 1.3.2, $\tau^{-1}(U)$ is a subspace of $V$.
(2) Since $0_V\in U$, $0_W\in \tau(U)$ by Proposition 2.1.2(1). Let $u,v\in \tau(U)$ and let $s\in \tau^{-1}(\{u\})$ and $t\in \tau^{-1}(\{v\})$. Since $s+t\in U$, linearity gives $u+v=\tau(s+t)\in \tau(U)$. Similarly, since $as\in U$, we have $au=\tau(as)\in \tau(U)$, where $a\in F$. By Theorem 1.3.2, $\tau(U)$ is a subspace of $W$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>
