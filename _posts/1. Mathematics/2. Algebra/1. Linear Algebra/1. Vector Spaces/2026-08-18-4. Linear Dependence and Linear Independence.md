The span of a set $S$ records what the set generates, but it does not record how economically the generation is achieved. Some vectors of $S$ may be unnecessary to form the same span: removing one of them may leave the span unchanged. In this sense, a spanning set may contain redundant vectors.

**Example 1.4.1.** Let $\mathbf x=(1,0)$ and $\mathbf y=(0,1)$ in $\mathbb R^2$, and consider $S=\{\mathbf x,\mathbf y,\mathbf x+\mathbf y\}$. Then we have 

$$ \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf x,\mathbf y,\mathbf x+\mathbf y\}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf x,\mathbf y\}=\mathbb R^2. $$

Thus, removing $\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}$ from $S$ does not change the subspace generated by $S$. The vector $\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}$ is therefore redundant.


This observation motivates the following definition, which distinguishes generating sets containing redundant vectors from those whose spans cannot be generated by any proper subset.

**Definition 1.4.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $S\subseteq V$. We say that $S$ is *linearly dependent* if there exists $v\in S$ such that

$$ \operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})=\operatorname{span}(S). $$

Otherwise, the set $S$ is said to be *linearly independent*.

The same phenomenon can be described in terms of individual vectors: a vector of $S$ can be removed without changing the span precisely when it can be generated by the remaining vectors.

**Proposition 1.4.3.** Let $V$ be a vector space, and let $S\subseteq V$. The set $S$ is linearly dependent if and only if 

$$v\in\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})$$ 

for some $v\in S$.

*Proof.* Since $S\setminus\{v\}\subseteq S$, monotonicity gives

$$ \operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})\subseteq\operatorname{span}(S). $$

By Theorem 1.3.10, the reverse inclusion holds if and only if

$$ S\subseteq\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\}). $$

Since $S\setminus\{v\}$ is already contained in its span, this is equivalent to $v\in\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})$. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

This characterization immediately yields a basic property of linear dependence. 

**Proposition 1.4.4.** Let $V$ be a vector space, and let $S\subseteq T\subseteq V$. 
1. If $S$ is linearly dependent, then $T$ is linearly dependent.
2. If $T$ is linearly independent, then $S$ is linearly independent.

*Proof.* Suppose that $S$ is linearly dependent. By Proposition 1.4.3, there exists $v\in S$ such that $v\in\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})$. Since $S\setminus\{v\}\subseteq T\setminus\{v\}$, monotonicity gives

$$ v\in\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\}) \subseteq\operatorname{span}(T\setminus\{v\}). $$

Hence $T$ is linearly dependent by Proposition 1.4.3. Taking the contrapositive of (1) yields (2).<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Proposition 1.4.4(2) shows that linear independence is preserved when vectors are removed. Extending a linearly independent set, however, requires more care: adding a vector may introduce redundancy. The following theorem provides a precise criterion for when a linearly independent set can be extended by a new vector. This criterion will later help us understand the relationship between maximal linear independence and spanning.

**Theorem 1.4.5.** Let $S$ be a linearly independent subset of a vector space $V$, and let $v\in V\setminus S$. Then, $S\cup \{v\}$ is linearly independent if and only if $v\notin \operatorname{span}(S)$.

*Proof.* We show that $S\cup\{v\}$ is linearly dependent if and only if $v\in\operatorname{span}(S)$. Suppose that $S\cup\{v\}$ is linearly dependent. By Proposition 1.4.3, there exists $u\in S\cup\{v\}$ such that

$$ u\in\operatorname{span}((S\cup\{v\})\setminus\{u\}). $$

If $u=v$, then $v\in\operatorname{span}(S)$. If $u\in S$, then

$$ u\in\operatorname{span}((S\setminus\{u\})\cup\{v\}). $$

Since $S$ is linearly independent, Proposition 1.4.3 gives

$$ u\notin\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{u\}). $$

Thus, by the exchange property,

$$ v\in\operatorname{span}((S\setminus\{u\})\cup\{u\}) =\operatorname{span}(S). $$


Conversely, suppose that $v\in\operatorname{span}(S)$. Then

$$ \operatorname{span}(S\cup\{v\})=\operatorname{span}(S). $$

Since $S=(S\cup\{v\})\setminus\{v\}$, it follows from Definition 1.4.2 that $S\cup\{v\}$ is linearly dependent.
Taking negations yields the desired equivalence.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

In the language of algebra, the redundancy of a subset of a vector space can be expressed *without* referring to one particular element. For example, the redundancy of the set $S=\{\mathbf x,\mathbf y,\mathbf x+\mathbf y\}$ in Example 1.4.1 can be expressed as follows: 

$$\mathbf x+\mathbf y-(\mathbf x+\mathbf y)=\mathbf0.$$


That is, a redundant generator gives rise to a *nontrivial representation* of the zero vector as a linear combination of the generators. In this way, we can reformulate the concept of linear dependence and linear independence. 

**Theorem 1.4.6.** A subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$ is linearly dependent if and only if there exist a finite number of distinct vectors $v_1,\dots,v_n\in S$ and scalars $a_1,\dots,a_n\in F$, not all zero, such that

$$ a_1v_1 + a_2v_2 + \cdots + a_nv_n = 0_V.$$

*Proof.* Suppose $S$ is linearly dependent. By Proposition 1.4.3, there exists $v_0\in S$ such that $v_0\in \operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v_0\})$. If $S\setminus\{v_0\}=\varnothing$, then $v_0=0_V$. So we have the required nontrivial relation: $1v_0=0_V$. Otherwise, by combining the coefficients of repeated vectors, we have

$$ v_0 = a_1v_1 + \cdots + a_nv_n$$

where $v_1,\ldots,v_n\in S\setminus\{v_0\}$ are distinct and $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in F$. It follows that

$$ (-1)v_0 + a_1v_1 + \cdots + a_nv_n = 0_V.$$

Since $v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_n$ are distinct, and the coefficient of $v_0$ is nonzero, this relation is nontrivial.
Conversely, suppose

$$ a_1v_1 + \cdots + a_nv_n = 0_V$$

for distinct vectors $v_1,\dots,v_n\in S$ and scalars $a_1,\dots,a_n\in F$, not all zero. If $n=1$ then $v_1=0_V$. Since $0_V$ belongs to the span of every set, by Proposition 1.4.3, $S$ is linearly dependent. Otherwise, choose $i$ such that $a_i\ne 0$. Then we have

$$ v_i = \sum_{\substack{1\le j\le n\\j\ne i}}(-a_i^{-1}a_j)v_j.$$

Every vector $v_j$ with $j\ne i$ belongs to $S\setminus\{v_i\}$ because the vectors are distinct. Thus $v_i\in\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v_i\})$. By Proposition 1.4.3, $S$ is linearly dependent.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Proposition 1.4.7.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $S$ be a subset of $V$.
1. $S$ is linearly dependent if $0_V\in S$.
2. $\varnothing$ is linearly independent.
3. $S$ is linearly independent if it consists of a single nonzero vector.
4. $S$ is linearly independent if and only if every representation of $0_V$ as a linear combination of vectors in $S$ is a trivial representation.

*Proof.* (1) Since $S$ has a nontrivial representation of $0_V$, namely $1\cdot 0_V$, $S$ is linearly dependent.
(2) Since $\varnothing$ has no vectors, it does not have nontrivial representation of $0_V$.
(3) Let $S=\lbrace v\rbrace$. If $S$ is linearly dependent, then $av=0_V$ for some nonzero scalar $a\in F$. It follows that $v = a^{-1}(av) = a^{-1}0_V=0_V$, which contradicts the fact that $v$ is nonzero.
(4) If $S$ is linearly independent, then every representation of $0_V$ as a linear combination of vectors in $S$ is a trivial representation. Conversely, if every representation of $0_V$ as a linear combination of vectors in $S$ is a trivial representation, then $S=\varnothing$ or $S$ has only trivial representations of $0_V$. In both cases, $S$ is linearly independent.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Example 1.4.8.** Let $n\in  \mathbb Z_{\ge 0}$. For a nonnegative integer $k\le n$, define

$$ p_k(x) = x^n + x^{n-1} + \cdots + x^k.$$

Then the set

$$ S = \lbrace p_0(x),p_1(x),\dots,p_n(x)\rbrace$$

is linearly independent in $F_n[x]$. Indeed, suppose that 

$$ a_0p_0(x) + a_1p_1(x) + \cdots + a_np_n(x) = \mathbf 0$$

for some $a_0,\dots,a_n\in F$. Then

$$ (a_0 + \cdots + a_n)x^n + (a_0 + \cdots + a_{n-1})x^{n-1} + \cdots + (a_0 + a_1)x + a_0 = \mathbf 0.$$

It follows that $a_0 = \cdots =a_n = 0$. Hence, by Proposition 1.4.7(4), $S$ is linearly independent.
