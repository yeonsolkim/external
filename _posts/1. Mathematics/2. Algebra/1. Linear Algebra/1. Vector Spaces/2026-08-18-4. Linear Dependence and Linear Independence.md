The span of a set $S$ records what $S$ generates, but it does not record how economically the generation is achieved. Some proper subset of $S$ may have the same span, because some generators in $S$ may already lie in the span of the subset. 

**Example 1.4.1.** Let $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(0,1)$ in $\mathbb R^2$, and consider $S=\{e_1,e_2,e_1+e_2\}$. Then we have 

$$ \operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2,e_1+e_2\}=\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2\}=\mathbb R^2. $$

The vector $e_1+e_2\in S$ is therefore redundant: it already belongs to the closure of the remaining vectors,

$$e_1+e_2\in\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2\}.$$

**Definition 1.4.2.** A subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$ is said to be *linearly dependent* if there exists $v\in S$ such that 

$$v\in\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\}).$$

If $S$ is not linearly dependent, then it is said to be *linearly independent*.

**Proposition 1.4.3.** Let $V$ be a vector space, and let $S\subseteq T\subseteq V$. 
1. If $S$ is linearly dependent, then $T$ is linearly dependent.
2. If $T$ is linearly independent, then $S$ is linearly independent.

*Proof.* If $S$ is linearly dependent, then there exists $v\in S$ such that $v\in \operatorname{span}(S\setminus \{v\})$. Since $S\setminus \{v\}\subseteq T\setminus \{v\}$, monotonicity gives 

$$ v\in \operatorname{span}(S\setminus \{v\}) \subseteq \operatorname{span}(T\setminus \{v\}).$$

Therefore $T$ is linearly dependent.
Taking contraposition of statement (1) gives (2).<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

In the language of algebra, the redundancy of a subset of a vector space can be expressed *without* singling out a particular vector. For example, the same redundancy in Example 1.4.1 can be expressed as follows: 

$$e_1+e_2-(e_1+e_2)=0.$$


That is, a redundant generator gives rise to a *nontrivial representation* of $0_V$ as a linear combination of the generators. In this way, we can reformulate the concept of linear dependence and linear independence. 

**Theorem 1.4.4.** A subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$ is linearly dependent if and only if there exist a finite number of distinct vectors $v_1,\dots,v_n\in S$ and scalars $a_1,\dots,a_n\in F$, not all zero, such that

$$ a_1v_1 + a_2v_2 + \cdots + a_nv_n = 0_V.$$

**Proposition 1.4.4.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $S$ be a subset of $V$.
1. $S$ is linearly dependent if $0_V\in S$.
2. $\varnothing$ is linearly independent.
3. $S$ is linearly independent if it consists of a single nonzero vector.
4. $S$ is linearly independent if and only if every representation of $0_V$ as a linear combination of vectors in $S$ is a trivial representation.

*Proof.* (1) Since $S$ has a nontrivial representation of $0_V$, namely $1\cdot 0_V$, $S$ is linearly dependent.
(2) Since $\varnothing$ has no vectors, it does not have nontrivial representation of $0_V$.
(3) Let $S=\lbrace v\rbrace$. If $S$ is linearly dependent, then $av=0_V$ for some nonzero scalar $a\in F$. It follows that $v = a^{-1}(av) = a^{-1}0_V=0_V$, which contradicts the fact that $v$ is nonzero.
(4) If $S$ is linearly independent, then every representation of $0_V$ as a linear combination of vectors in $S$ is a trivial representation. Conversely, if every representation of $0_V$ as a linear combination of vectors in $S$ is a trivial representation, then $S=\varnothing$ or $S$ has only trivial representations of $0_V$. In both cases, $S$ is linearly independent.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Example 1.4.5.** Let $n\in  \mathbb Z_{\ge 0}$. For a nonnegative integer $k\le n$, define

$$ p_k(x) = x^n + x^{n-1} + \cdots + x^k.$$

Then the set

$$ S = \lbrace p_0(x),p_1(x),\dots,p_n(x)\rbrace$$

is linearly independent in $F_n[x]$. Indeed, suppose that 

$$ a_0p_0(x) + a_1p_1(x) + \cdots + a_np_n(x) = \mathbf 0$$

for some $a_0,\dots,a_n\in F$. Then

$$ (a_0 + \cdots + a_n)x^n + (a_0 + \cdots + a_{n-1})x^{n-1} + \cdots + (a_0 + a_1)x + a_0 = \mathbf 0.$$

It follows that $a_0 = \cdots =a_n = 0$. Hence, by Proposition 1.4.4(4), $S$ is linearly independent.
