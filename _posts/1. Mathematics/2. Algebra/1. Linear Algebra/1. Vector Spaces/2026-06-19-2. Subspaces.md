**Definition 1.2.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$. A subset $W$ of $V$ is called a *subspace* if $W$ is itself a vector space under the operations inherited from $V$.

**Theorem 1.2.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F,$ and $W$ a subset of $V.$ Then $W$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if $W$ has the following three properties.
1. $0_V\in W$.
2. $u+v\in W$ for all $u,v\in W$.
3. $av\in W$ for all $a\in F$ and $v\in W$.

*Proof*. Suppose that $W$ is a subspace of $V.$ By Definition 1.2.1, (2) and (3) hold. Since there exists $0_W\in W$ such that $v+0_W = v$ for all $v\in W$ and $0_W = 0_V$, (1) holds.
Conversely, suppose that (1), (2), and (3) hold. Then, $W$ is closed under scalar multiplication and vector addition. Since $-v=(-1)v\in W$ for all $v\in W$, each element of $W$ has its additive inverse in $W$. Therefore $W$ is a subspace of $V$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Example 1.2.3.** Let $A$ be an $m\times n$ matrix with entries from a field $F$. The *transpose* of $A$, denoted by $A^{\!\top}$, is the unique matrix whose $i$-th row is the $i$-th column of $A$, for each $i=1,\dots,n$, where a row and a column are identified with the same coordinate tuple. Then, $(A^{\!\top})_{ij}$ is the $j$-th entry of the $i$-th column of $A$, namely, $A_{ji}$. Consequently, one may think of $A^{\!\top}$ as being obtained from $A$ interchanging the rows with the columns.
If $A^{\!\top}=A$, then $A$ is called a *symmetric matrix*. In this case, $A$ is a square matrix with $A_{ij} = A_{ji}$.
The set $W$ of all symmetric matrix in ${F}^{n\times n}$ is a subspace of ${F}^{n\times n}$. Indeed, $O\in W$ since $O^{\!\top}=O$. Let $A,B\in W.$ We have $A_{ij}=A_{ji}$ and $B_{ij}=B_{ji},$ thus $(A+B)_{ij}=A_{ij}+B_{ij}=A_{ji}+B_{ji}=(A+B)_{ji}.$ Therefore $(A+B)\in W.$ Let $A\in W$ and $c\in F.$ We have $A_{ij}=A_{ji},$ thus $(cA)_{ij}=cA_{ij}=cA_{ji}=(cA)_{ij}.$ Therefore $cA\in W.$

**Example 1.2.4.** Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer, and let ${F}_n[x]$ consist of all polynomials in ${F}[x]$ having degree less than or equal to $n.$ Since the zero polynomial $\mathbf 0\in {F}[x]$ has degree $-\infty,$ it is in ${F}_n[x].$ Let $P, Q \in {F}[x].$ Since $\text{deg}(P+Q)\leq \max{\lbrace  \text{deg}(P),\text{deg}(Q)\rbrace }\leq n,\ (P+Q)\in {F}_n[x].$ Let $P \in {F}[x].$ Since $\text{deg}(cP)=\text{deg}(P)\leq n, \ cP\in {F}_n[x].$
It follows from Theorem 1.2.2 that ${F}_n[x]$ is a subspace of ${F}[x].$


**Example 1.2.5.** Let $C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R)$ denote the set of all continuous real-valued functions defined on $\mathbb R.$ Since constant functions are continuous functions, $\mathbf {0} \in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$ Let $f,g\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$ Since $f+g$ is continuous function, $(f+g)\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$ Let $f\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R)$ and $c\in \mathbb R.$ Since $cf$ is a continuous function, $cf\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$
Theorem 1.2.2 shows $C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R)$ is a subspace of ${F}[x].$


**Example 1.2.6.** An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is called *diagonal matrix* if $A_{ij}=0$ whenever $i\neq j$. Let $W$ be the set of all $n\times n$ diagonal matrices. Since $O_{ij}=0$ for all $i$ and $j,\ O\in W.$ Let $A,B\in W.$ If $i\neq j$ then $(A+B)_{ij}=A_{ij}+B_{ij}=0+0=0.$ Thus $(A+B)\in W.$ Let $A\in W$ and $c\in F.$ If $i\neq j$ then $(cA)_{ij}=cA_{ij}=c0=0c=0.$ Thus $cA\in W.$
Theorem 1.2.2 shows that $W$ is a subspace of ${F}^{n\times n}$.

**Example 1.2.7.** Let $A$ be a $n\times n$ matrix with the entries $A_{ij}\in F$. The $n$-tuple $(A_{11},\dots,A_{nn})$ is called the *main diagonal* of $A$. The *trace* of $A$ is the sum of all the entries of the main diagonal, denoted by $\operatorname{tr}(A)$. That is, 

$$ \operatorname{tr}(A) = A_{11} + A_{22} + \cdots + A_{nn}.$$

The set of $n\times n$ matrices having trace equal to zero is a subspace of ${F}^{n\times n}$.

**Theorem 1.2.8.** Any intersection of subspaces of a vector space $V$ is a subspace of $V.$

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal C$ be a collection of subspaces of $V,$ and let $W$ denote the intersection of $\mathcal C.$ Since every subspace contains the zero vector, $0_V\in W.$ Let $a\in F$ and $u,v\in W.$ Then $u$ and $v$ are elements of each subspace in $\mathcal C.$ Since each subspace in $\mathcal C$ is closed under the vector addition and scalar multiplication, $u+v$ and $av$ are elements of each subspace in $\mathcal C.$ Hence $u+v$ and $av$ are also elements of $W$, so that $W$ is a subspace of $V$ by Theorem 1.2.2.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.2.1.** Prove that

$$(aA+bB)^{\!\top} = aA^{\!\top} + bB^{\!\top}$$

for any $A,B\in F^{m\times n}$ and any $a,b\in F.$

*Proof.* Let $1\leq i \leq m$ and $1\leq j \leq n.$ Then we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left( (aA+bB)^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}
&= (aA+bB)_{ji}\\
&= (aA)_{ji} + (bB)_{ji}\\
&= aA_{ji} + bB_{ji}\\
&= a\left(  A^{\!\top}\right)_{ij} + b\left(  B^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}\\
&= \left( aA^{\!\top}\right)_{ij} + \left( bB^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}\\
&= \left( aA^{\!\top} + bB^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore $(aA+bB)^{\!\top} = aA^{\!\top} + bB^{\!\top}.$<span class="qed">$\square$</span>
