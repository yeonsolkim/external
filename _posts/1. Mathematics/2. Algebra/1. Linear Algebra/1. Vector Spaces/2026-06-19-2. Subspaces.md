Within a vector space, we often focus on elements that satisfy a particular condition. The important question is whether the condition survives the operations of the larger space: do addition and scalar multiplication keep us among elements satisfying that condition? If they do, and the zero vector also satisfies the condition, the collection itself forms a vector space within the original one. Such a collection is called a subspace.

**Definition 1.2.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$. A subset $W$ of $V$ is called a *subspace* if $W$ is itself a vector space under the operations inherited from $V$.
Note that $\{0_V\}$ is a subspace; it is called the *zero subspace* of $V$.

Since $W$ uses the operations of $V$, the algebraic laws governing those operations are inherited from $V$. Thus, to decide whether $W$ is a subspace, it is enough to check that $W$ contains $0_V$ and is *closed* under the two operations of addition and scalar multiplication. The following theorem makes this criterion precise.

**Theorem 1.2.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F,$ and $W$ a subset of $V.$ Then $W$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if $W$ has the following three properties.
1. $0_V\in W$.
2. $u+v\in W$ for all $u,v\in W$.
3. $av\in W$ for all $a\in F$ and $v\in W$.

*Proof*. Suppose that $W$ is a subspace of $V.$ By Definition 1.2.1, (2) and (3) hold. Since there exists $0_W\in W$ such that $v+0_W = v$ for all $v\in W$ and $0_W = 0_V$, (1) holds.
Conversely, suppose that (1), (2), and (3) hold. Then, $W$ is closed under scalar multiplication and vector addition. Since $-v=(-1)v\in W$ for all $v\in W$, each element of $W$ has its additive inverse in $W$. Therefore $W$ is a subspace of $V$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Example 1.2.3.** Let $A$ be an $m\times n$ matrix with entries from a field $F$. The *transpose* of $A$, denoted by $A^{\!\top}$, is the unique matrix whose $i$-th row is the $i$-th column of $A$, for each $i=1,\dots,n$, where a row and a column are identified with the same coordinate tuple. Then, $(A^{\!\top})_{ij}$ is the $j$-th entry of the $i$-th column of $A$, namely, $A_{ji}$. Consequently, one may think of $A^{\!\top}$ as being obtained from $A$ interchanging the rows with the columns.
If $A^{\!\top}=A$, then $A$ is called a *symmetric matrix*. In this case, $A$ is a square matrix with $A_{ij} = A_{ji}$.
The set $W$ of all symmetric matrix in ${F}^{n\times n}$ is a subspace of ${F}^{n\times n}$. Indeed, $O\in W$ since $O^{\!\top}=O$. Let $A,B\in W.$ We have $A_{ij}=A_{ji}$ and $B_{ij}=B_{ji},$ thus $(A+B)_{ij}=A_{ij}+B_{ij}=A_{ji}+B_{ji}=(A+B)_{ji}.$ Therefore $(A+B)\in W.$ Let $A\in W$ and $c\in F.$ We have $A_{ij}=A_{ji},$ thus $(cA)_{ij}=cA_{ij}=cA_{ji}=(cA)_{ji}.$ Therefore $cA\in W.$

**Example 1.2.4.** Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer, and let ${F}_n[x]$ consist of all polynomials in ${F}[x]$ having degree less than or equal to $n.$ Since the zero polynomial $\mathbf 0\in {F}[x]$ has degree $-\infty,$ it is in ${F}_n[x].$ Let $P, Q \in {F}_n[x].$ Since $\text{deg}(P+Q)\leq \max{\lbrace  \text{deg}(P),\text{deg}(Q)\rbrace }\leq n,\ (P+Q)\in {F}_n[x].$ Let $P \in {F}_n[x]$ and $c\in F.$ Since $\text{deg}(cP)\leq n$, we have $cP\in {F}_n[x].$
It follows from Theorem 1.2.2 that ${F}_n[x]$ is a subspace of ${F}[x].$


**Example 1.2.5.** Let $C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R)$ denote the set of all continuous real-valued functions defined on $\mathbb R.$ Since constant functions are continuous functions, $\mathbf {0} \in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$ Let $f,g\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$ Since $f+g$ is continuous function, $(f+g)\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$ Let $f\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R)$ and $c\in \mathbb R.$ Since $cf$ is a continuous function, $cf\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$
Theorem 1.2.2 shows $C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R)$ is a subspace of ${\mathbb R}^{\mathbb R}.$


**Example 1.2.6.** An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is called a *diagonal matrix* if $A_{ij}=0$ whenever $i\neq j$. Let $W$ be the set of all $n\times n$ diagonal matrices. Since $O_{ij}=0$ for all $i$ and $j,\ O\in W.$ Let $A,B\in W.$ If $i\neq j$ then $(A+B)_{ij}=A_{ij}+B_{ij}=0+0=0.$ Thus $(A+B)\in W.$ Let $A\in W$ and $c\in F.$ If $i\neq j$ then $(cA)_{ij}=cA_{ij}=c0=0c=0.$ Thus $cA\in W.$
Theorem 1.2.2 shows that $W$ is a subspace of ${F}^{n\times n}$.

**Example 1.2.7.** Let $A$ be a $n\times n$ matrix with the entries $A_{ij}\in F$. The *trace* of $A$ is the sum of all the entries of the main diagonal, denoted by $\operatorname{tr}(A)$. That is, 

$$ \operatorname{tr}(A) = A_{11} + A_{22} + \cdots + A_{nn}.$$

The set of $n\times n$ matrices with their traces equal to zero is a subspace of ${F}^{n\times n}$.

**Theorem 1.2.8.** Any intersection of subspaces of a vector space $V$ is a subspace of $V.$

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal C$ be a collection of subspaces of $V,$ and let $W$ denote the intersection of $\mathcal C.$ Since every subspace contains the zero vector, $0_V\in W.$ Let $a\in F$ and $u,v\in W.$ Then $u$ and $v$ are elements of each subspace in $\mathcal C.$ Since each subspace in $\mathcal C$ is closed under the vector addition and scalar multiplication, $u+v$ and $av$ are elements of each subspace in $\mathcal C.$ Hence $u+v$ and $av$ are also elements of $W$, so that $W$ is a subspace of $V$ by Theorem 1.2.2.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

We now ask whether the union of subspaces of $V$ is a subspace of $V$. It is easily seen that the union contains the zero vector and is closed under scalar multiplication. But the union need not be closed under addition in general. In fact, the union of two subspaces becomes a subspace if and only if one of the subspaces contains the other.

**Proposition 1.2.9.** Let $W_1$ and $W_2$ be subspaces of a vector space $V$. Then, $W_1\cup W_2$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if $W_1\subseteq W_2$ or $W_2\subseteq W_1$.

*Proof.* Suppose $W_1\cup W_2$ is a subspace of $V$, and suppose $x\in W_1\setminus W_2$ and $y\in W_2\setminus W_1$. Then $x+y\in W_1\cup W_2$ and, without loss of generality, $x+y\in W_1$. Since $W_1$ is a subspace of $V$, we have $y=(x+y)+(-x)\in W_1.$ This contradicts $y\in W_2\setminus W_1$. Therefore if $W_1\cup W_2$ is a subspace of $V$ then $W_1\subseteq W_2$ or $W_2\subseteq W_1$.
Since $W_1$ and $W_2$ are subspaces of $V$, the converse automatically holds.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

We have seen that the union itself is not an operation that generates a subspace. There is, however, a natural way to combine two subspaces $W_1$ and $W_2$ to obtain a subspace containing both $W_1$ and $W_2$. The key to finding such an operation is to assure that the result must be closed under addition.

**Definition 1.2.10.** Let $W_1$ and $W_2$ be subspaces of a vector space $V$. We define the *sum* $W_1+W_2$ of $W_1$ and $W_2$ by

$$ W_1 + W_2 = \{x+y: x\in W_1 \text{ and } y\in W_2\}.$$

**Theorem 1.2.11.** Let $W_1$ and $W_2$ be subspaces of a vector space $V$.
1. $W_1+W_2$ is a subspace of $V$ that contains both $W_1$ and $W_2$.
2. Every subspace of $V$ that contains both $W_1$ and $W_2$ contains $W_1+W_2$.

*Proof.* (1) Since $0_V\in W_1$ and $0_V\in W_2$, we have $W_1\subseteq W_1+W_2$ and $W_2\subseteq W_1+W_2$; we also have $0_V\in W_1 + W_2$.
If $v\in W_1 + W_2$, then there exist $x\in W_1$ and $y\in W_2$ such that $v=x+y$. Hence for any scalar $a$,

$$av = ax + ay\in W_1+W_2.$$

If $v_1,v_2\in W_1+W_2$, then there exist $x_1,x_2\in W_1$ and $y_1,y_2\in W_2$ such that $v_1=x_1+y_1$ and $v_2=x_2+y_2$. Hence,

$$v_1+v_2=(x_1+x_2)+(y_1+y_2)\in W_1+W_2.$$


(2) Let $U$ be a subspace of $V$ that contains both $W_1$ and $W_2$. If $v\in W_1+W_2$, then there exist $x\in W_1$ and $y\in W_2$ such that $v=x+y$. Since $x\in U$ and $y\in U$, closedness of $U$ under addition gives $x+y\in U$. Therefore $W_1 + W_2\subseteq U$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

The preceding theorem shows that $W_1+W_2$ is the smallest subspace containing both $W_1$ and $W_2$. Every vector in this sum can be written as $x+y$, where $x\in W_1$ and $y\in W_2$, but this representation need not be unique. For a nonzero vector $z\in W_1\cap W_2$, we have $x+y=(x+z)+(y-z)$: a common vector can be moved from one summand to the other. The following definition describes the case in which this ambiguity disappears.

**Definition 1.2.12.** Let $W_1$ and $W_2$ be subspaces of a vector space $V$. If $W_1\cap W_2 = \{0_V\}$, then the sum of $W_1$ and $W_2$ is called the *direct sum*, which is denoted by $W_1\oplus W_2$.

**Theorem 1.2.13.** Let $W_1$ and $W_2$ be subspaces of a vector space $V$. The sum $W_1+W_2$ is a direct sum if and only if every $v\in W_1+W_2$ has a unique expression $v=x+y$, where $x\in W_1$ and $y\in W_2$.

*Proof.* Suppose $W_1+W_2$ is a direct sum. Given $v\in W_1+W_2$, let $v = x_1 + y_1 =x_2 + y_2$ where $x_1,x_2\in W_1$ and $y_1,y_2\in W_2$. If $x_2 = x_1 + z$ for $z\in V$, then $y_2 = y_1-z$. It follows that $z\in W_1$ and $z\in W_2$. Since $W_1\cap W_2 = \{0_V\}$, the vector $z$ is the zero vector. Hence $x_1=x_2$ and $y_1=y_2$.
Conversely, suppose every $v\in W_1+W_2$ has a unique expression $v=x+y$, where $x\in W_1$ and $y\in W_2$. If $x=y$ then $v=x+y=2x + 0_V$. Our premise yields $x=2x$ and $y=0_V$, so $x=y=0_V$. Hence $W_1\cap W_2 = \{0_V\}$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

The sum and direct sum describe how subspaces combine inside $V$. A subspace $W$ can also be used to collect vectors of $V$ that differ by an element of $W$. Each such collection is a translate of $W$. These translates, called cosets, will become the elements of a new vector space, the quotient space $V/W$.

**Definition 1.2.14.** Let $W$ be a subspace of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$. Given a vector $v\in V$, the set

$$v+W=\{v+w:w\in W\}$$

is called the *coset* of $W$ containing $v$.


Different vectors may determine the same coset. The following criterion tells us exactly when this happens.

**Proposition 1.2.15.** Let $u,v\in V$. Then $u+W=v+W$ if and only if $u-v\in W$. Consequently, $0_V+W=W$, and any two cosets of $W$ are either equal or disjoint.

*Proof.* Suppose $u+W=v+W$. Since $u\in u+W$, $u\in v+W$. Hence there exists $w\in W$ such that $u=v+w$. It follows that $u-v=w\in W$. 
Conversely, suppose $u-v\in W$. If $x\in u+W$, then there exists $w\in W$ such that $x=u+w$. We have $(u-v) + w\in W$; let $w'=(u-v)+w$. It follows that $x=u+w=v+w'$. Hence $x\in v+W$, so that $u+W\subseteq v+W$. In the same way, we obtain $v+W\subseteq u+W$. Therefore $u+W=v+W$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

The cosets therefore partition $V$: each vector belongs to one coset, and vectors in the same coset differ by an element of $W$. We now treat each coset as a single vector.

**Definition 1.2.16.** Let $W$ be a subspace of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$. The set of all cosets of $W$ in $V$ is denoted by

$$V/W=\{v+W:v\in V\}$$

and is read "$V$ modulo $W$". For $u,v\in V$ and $a\in F$, define addition and scalar multiplication on $V/W$ by

$$(u+W)+(v+W)=(u+v)+W \quad\text{and}\quad a(v+W)=av+W.$$

Since a coset can have more than one representative, these operations must give the same result regardless of which representatives are chosen.

**Theorem 1.2.17.** The operations in Definition 1.2.16 are well-defined and make $V/W$ a vector space over $F$, called the *quotient space* of $V$ modulo $W$. Its zero vector is $W=0_V+W$, and the additive inverse of $v+W$ is $(-v)+W$.

**Example 1.2.18.** Let $V=\mathbb R^2$ and $W=\{(t,0):t\in\mathbb R\}$, the horizontal axis. For $(a,b)\in V$,

$$(a,b)+W=\{(a+t,b):t\in\mathbb R\}=\{(t,b):t\in\mathbb R\}.$$

Thus each coset is a horizontal line, determined by its height $b$. For example, $(2,3)+W=(0,3)+W$ is the line at height $3$. If $L_b$ denotes the line at height $b$, then the operations in $V/W$ become

$$L_b+L_c=L_{b+c} \quad\text{and}\quad \lambda L_b=L_{\lambda b}.$$


In particular, $L_0=W$ is the zero vector of $V/W$. If $U=\{(0,b):b\in\mathbb R\}$ is the vertical axis, then $V=W\oplus U$, and each coset has exactly one representative in $U$.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.2.1.** Prove that

$$(aA+bB)^{\!\top} = aA^{\!\top} + bB^{\!\top}$$

for any $A,B\in F^{m\times n}$ and any $a,b\in F.$

*Proof.* Let $1\leq i \leq n$ and $1\leq j \leq m.$ Then we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left( (aA+bB)^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}
&= (aA+bB)_{ji}\\
&= (aA)_{ji} + (bB)_{ji}\\
&= aA_{ji} + bB_{ji}\\
&= a\left(  A^{\!\top}\right)_{ij} + b\left(  B^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}\\
&= \left( aA^{\!\top}\right)_{ij} + \left( bB^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}\\
&= \left( aA^{\!\top} + bB^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore $(aA+bB)^{\!\top} = aA^{\!\top} + bB^{\!\top}.$<span class="qed">$\square$</span>
