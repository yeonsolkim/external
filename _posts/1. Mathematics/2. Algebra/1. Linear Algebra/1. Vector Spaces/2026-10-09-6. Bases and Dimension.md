**Bases for a vector space.** The concepts of generating sets and linearly independent sets were introduced in the preceding two sections. We now seek a generating set for an entire vector space that contains no redundancy. This leads to the concept of a basis for a vector space.

**Definition 1.6.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space. A subset $S$ of $V$ is called a *basis* for $V$ if $\operatorname{span}(S)=V$ and $S$ is linearly independent.

**Example 1.6.2.** The empty set $\varnothing$ is a basis for the zero vector space, because $\operatorname{span}(\varnothing)=\{0_V\}$ and $\varnothing$ is linearly independent.

**Example 1.6.3.** In the vector space $F_n[x]$, the set $\{x^k: 0\le k\le n\}$ is a basis, called the *standard basis* for $F_n[x]$. While $F_n[x]$ has a finite basis, the infinite set $\{x^k: k\in \mathbb Z_{\ge 0}\}$ is a basis for $F[x]$.


The definition of a basis requires a set to have two properties at the same time: generating and linear independence. The next theorem shows that extremeizing only one of the two conditions motivating the definition is sufficient to characterize a basis. Here, minimality and maximality are understood with respect to set inclusion.

**Theorem 1.6.4.** Let $B$ be a subset of a vector space $V$. The following are equivalent.

1. The set $B$ is a basis for $V$.
2. The set $B$ is a minimal generating set for $V$.
3. The set $B$ is a maximal linearly independent subset of $V$.

*Proof.* Definition 1.5.2 immediately shows that the conditions (1) and (2) are equivalent. 
We now show that the conditions (1) and (3) are equivalent. If $B$ is a basis for $V$, then $\operatorname{span}(B) = V$ and $B$ is linearly independent. By Theorem 1.5.5, every superset of $B$ not equal to $B$ is linearly dependent. Hence $B$ is maximal. 
Conversely, suppose that $B$ is a maximal linearly independent subset of $V$. Then, by Theorem 1.5.5, $v\in \operatorname{span}(B)$ for all $v\in V\setminus B$. Hence $V\setminus B\subseteq \operatorname{span}(B)$. Since we also have $B\subseteq \operatorname{span}(B)$, $\operatorname{span}(B)=V$. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>


A basis provides an unambiguous representation of every vector. Generating guarantees that a representation exists, while linear independence ensures the uniqueness of that representation.

**Theorem 1.6.5.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $B\subseteq V$. Then, $B$ is a basis for $V$ if and only if every vector $v\in V$ has a unique representation in terms of $B$.

*Proof.* Suppose that $B$ is a basis for $V$. Since $\operatorname{span}(B)=V$, $v\in V$ implies $v\in \operatorname{span}(B)$. By Proposition 1.3.14, $v$ has a representation in terms of $B$. Since $B$ is linearly independent, Corollary 1.5.7 guarantees that this representation is unique.
We now prove the converse. Since every $v\in V$ has a representation in terms of $B$, $v\in\operatorname{span}(B)$ by Proposition 1.3.14. Hence $\operatorname{span}(B)=V$. Since every vector has at most one representation in terms of $B$, Corollary 1.5.7 implies that $B$ is linearly independent. Therefore $B$ is a basis for $V$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Definition 1.6.6.** Let $B$ be a basis for a vector space $V$ over a field $F$. For each $v\in V$, the unique representation $c_v^B:B\to F$ of $v$ in terms of $B$ is called the *coordinate function* of $v$ with respect to $B$. 
The associated *coordinate map* is the function

$$ \Phi_B:V\longrightarrow F^{(B)}, \qquad v\longmapsto c_v^B, $$

where $F^{(B)}$ denotes the set of all finitely supported functions from $B$ to $F$.


**Theorem 1.6.7.** Let $V$ be a vector space, and let $S\subseteq V$ be a finite generating set for $V$. Then there exists a subset $B\subseteq S$ that is a basis for $V$. Hence $V$ has a finite basis.

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal C$ be the collection of all subsets of $S$ that generate $V$. Since $S$ is a finite generating set for $V$, $\mathcal C$ is nonempty and finite. Hence there exists a minimal element in $\mathcal C$; let $B$ be that element. By Theorem 1.6.4, $B$ is a basis for $V$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 1.6.8 (Steinitz replacement theorem).** Let $V$ be a vector space, let $S\subseteq V$ be a generating set for $V$, and let $L\subseteq V$ be a finite linearly independent set. Then there exists a subset $R\subseteq S$ such that

$$ |R|=|L| \quad \text{and} \quad \operatorname{span}((S\setminus R)\cup L)=V. $$

*Proof.* We proceed by induction on $n=|L|$. If $n=0$, take $R=\varnothing$. Then $|R|=|L|=0$ and $\operatorname{span}((S\setminus R)\cup L)=\operatorname{span}(S)=V$. Now suppose that the statement holds for $n=k$. Let $|L|=k+1$ and choose $\ell\in L$. Put $L'=L\setminus\{\ell\}$. By Proposition 1.5.4(2), $L'$ is linearly independent, so the induction hypothesis gives $R'\subseteq S$ such that

$$ |R'|=|L'|=k \quad \text{and} \quad \operatorname{span}((S\setminus R')\cup L')=V. $$

Put $A=S\setminus R'$. Since $L$ is linearly independent, Theorem 1.5.5 gives $\ell\notin\operatorname{span}(L')$. On the other hand, $\ell\in\operatorname{span}(A\cup L')$. By Proposition 1.4.11, there exists a finite subset $T\subseteq A$ such that $\ell\in\operatorname{span}(T \cup L')$. Among the subsets of $T$ satisfying this condition, choose a minimal one; denote it by $T_0$. Since $\ell\notin\operatorname{span}(L')$, the set $T_0$ is nonempty. Choose $t\in T_0$ and put $C=(T_0\setminus\{t\})\cup L'$. By the minimality of $T_0$,

$$ \ell\in\operatorname{span}(C\cup\{t\})\setminus\operatorname{span}(C). $$

The exchange property therefore gives

$$ \operatorname{span}(C\cup\{t\})=\operatorname{span}(C\cup\{\ell\}). $$


In particular, since $C\cup\{\ell\}\subseteq(A\setminus\{t\})\cup L$, we have

$$ t\in\operatorname{span}(C\cup\{\ell\})\subseteq\operatorname{span}((A\setminus\{t\})\cup L). $$

All other vectors of $A\cup L'$ already belong to $(A\setminus\{t\})\cup L$. It therefore follows that

$$ \operatorname{span}((A\setminus\{t\})\cup L)=V. $$


Now put $R=R'\cup\{t\}$. Since $t\in A=S\setminus R'$, we have $R\subseteq S$ and 

$$|R|=|R'|+1 = k+1 = |L|.$$ 

Moreover, $S\setminus R=A\setminus\{t\}$, so 

$$\operatorname{span}((S\setminus R)\cup L)=V.\tag*{\(\square\)}$$ 


**Corollary 1.6.9.** Let $V$ be a vector space that admits a finite basis. Then every basis for $V$ is finite, and has the same cardinality.

*Proof.* Let $B$ be a finite basis for $V$, and let $C$ be a basis for $V$. By the Steinitz replacement theorem, there exists $S\subseteq C$ that has the same cardinality as $B$. Since $B$ generates $V$ and $S$ is linearly independent, the set $S$ also generates $V$ by the Steinitz replacement theorem. Since $C$ is a minimal generating set for $V$, $S=C$. Therefore $|C|=|B|$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**The dimension of a vector space.** Although the choice of a basis is generally not unique, its cardinality is determined by the vector space itself. This allows us to assign a well-defined numerical invariant to every finitely generated vector space, measuring the number of independent generators needed to describe it.

**Definition 1.6.10.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$. We say that $V$ is *finite-dimensional* if it admits a finite basis. In this case, the *dimension* of $V$, denoted by $\dim_F V$ or simply $\dim V$, is defined by

$$ \dim V=|B|, $$

where $B$ is any basis for $V$. If $V$ admits no finite basis, then $V$ is said to be *infinite-dimensional*.

**Theorem 1.6.11.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space with $\dim V=n$.

1. If $S\subseteq V$ is linearly independent, then $S$ is finite and $|S|\le n$.
2. If $S\subseteq V$ generates $V$, then $|S|\ge n$.
