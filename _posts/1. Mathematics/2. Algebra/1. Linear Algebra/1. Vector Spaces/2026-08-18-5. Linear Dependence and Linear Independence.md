Some vectors of a generating set may be unnecessary to form the same span: removing one of them may leave the span unchanged. In this sense, a generating set may contain *redundant* vectors. 

**Example 1.5.1.** Let $\mathbf a=(1,0)$ and $\mathbf b=(0,1)$ in $\mathbb R^2$, and consider $S=\{\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf a+\mathbf b\}$. Then we have 

$$ \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf a+\mathbf b\}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf a,\mathbf b\}=\mathbb R^2. $$

Thus, removing $\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}$ from $S$ does not change the subspace generated by $S$. The vector $\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}$ is therefore redundant.


This observation motivates the following definition, which distinguishes generating sets containing such redundancy from those that do not. 

**Definition 1.5.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $S\subseteq V$. We say that $S$ is *linearly dependent* if there exists $v\in S$ such that

$$ \operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})=\operatorname{span}(S). $$

Otherwise, the set $S$ is said to be *linearly independent*.

The same phenomenon can be described in terms of individual vectors: a vector of $S$ can be removed without changing the span precisely when it can be generated by the remaining vectors.

**Proposition 1.5.3.** Let $V$ be a vector space, and let $S\subseteq V$. The set $S$ is linearly dependent if and only if 

$$v\in\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})$$ 

for some $v\in S$.

*Proof.* Since $S\setminus\{v\}\subseteq S$, monotonicity gives

$$ \operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})\subseteq\operatorname{span}(S). $$

By Theorem 1.4.4, the reverse inclusion holds if and only if

$$ S\subseteq\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\}). $$

Since $S\setminus\{v\}$ is already contained in its span, this is equivalent to $v\in\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})$. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

This characterization immediately yields a basic property of linear dependence: linear dependence is monotone with respect to set inclusion.

**Proposition 1.5.4.** Let $V$ be a vector space, and let $S\subseteq T\subseteq V$. 
1. If $S$ is linearly dependent, then $T$ is linearly dependent.
2. If $T$ is linearly independent, then $S$ is linearly independent.

*Proof.* Suppose that $S$ is linearly dependent. By Proposition 1.5.3, there exists $v\in S$ such that $v\in\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})$. Since $S\setminus\{v\}\subseteq T\setminus\{v\}$, monotonicity gives

$$ v\in\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\}) \subseteq\operatorname{span}(T\setminus\{v\}). $$

Hence $T$ is linearly dependent by Proposition 1.5.3. Taking the contrapositive of (1) yields (2).<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Proposition 1.5.4(2) shows that linear independence is preserved when vectors are removed. Extending a linearly independent set, however, requires more care: adding a vector may introduce redundancy. The following theorem provides a precise criterion for when a linearly independent set can be extended by a new vector. This criterion will later help us understand the relationship between maximal linear independence and spanning.

**Theorem 1.5.5.** Let $S$ be a linearly independent subset of a vector space $V$, and let $v\in V\setminus S$. Then, $S\cup \{v\}$ is linearly independent if and only if $v\notin \operatorname{span}(S)$.

*Proof.* We show that $S\cup\{v\}$ is linearly dependent if and only if $v\in\operatorname{span}(S)$. Suppose that $S\cup\{v\}$ is linearly dependent. By Proposition 1.5.3, there exists $u\in S\cup\{v\}$ such that

$$ u\in\operatorname{span}((S\cup\{v\})\setminus\{u\}). $$

If $u=v$, then $v\in\operatorname{span}(S)$. If $u\in S$, then

$$ u\in\operatorname{span}((S\setminus\{u\})\cup\{v\}). $$

Since $S$ is linearly independent, Proposition 1.5.3 gives

$$ u\notin\operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{u\}). $$

Thus, by the Theorem 1.4.13(1),

$$ v\in\operatorname{span}((S\setminus\{u\})\cup\{u\}) =\operatorname{span}(S). $$


Conversely, suppose that $v\in\operatorname{span}(S)$. Then

$$ \operatorname{span}(S\cup\{v\})=\operatorname{span}(S). $$

Since $S=(S\cup\{v\})\setminus\{v\}$, it follows from Definition 1.5.2 that $S\cup\{v\}$ is linearly dependent.
Taking negations yields the desired equivalence.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

In the language of algebra, the redundancy of a subset of a vector space can be expressed *without* referring to one particular element. For example, the redundancy of the set $S$ in Example 1.5.1 can be expressed as follows: 

$$\sum_{\mathbf x\in S}a_{\mathbf x}\mathbf x=\mathbf0.$$


That is, a redundant generator gives rise to a *nontrivial representation* of the zero vector as a linear combination of the generators. This gives an algebraic formulation of linear dependence.

**Theorem 1.5.6.** A subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$ is linearly dependent if and only if there exists a pair $(I,a)$, where $I\subseteq S$ is finite and $a:I\to F$ is a function, such that

$$ \sum_{s\in I}a(s)s=0_V $$

and $a(s)\ne0$ for some $s\in I$.

*Proof.* Suppose that $S$ is linearly dependent. Then there exists $s\in S$ such that $s\in \operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{s\})$. By Definition 1.3.8, there exist a finite set $J$ and indexed families $\{t_j\}_{j\in J}$ in $S\setminus\{s\}$ and $\{a_j\}_{j\in J}$ in $F$ such that

$$ s=\sum_{j\in J}a_jt_j. $$

For each $t\in \{t_j: j\in J\}$, define $J_t$ to be the set of all $j\in J$ such that $t_j=t$. Then $J_t\subseteq J$. Let $I = \{t_j: j\in J\} \cup \{s\}$ and define $b:I\to F$ by

$$ b(t) =
\begin{cases}
\sum_{j\in J_t}a_j, &\text{if } t\in \{t_j:j\in J\},\\
-1, &\text{if } t=s.
\end{cases}$$

Then we have

$$ \sum_{t\in I}b(t)t = 0_V,$$

and $b(s)=-1\ne0$.
We now prove the converse. Let $I\subseteq S$ be finite and let $a:I\to F$ satisfy the stated conditions. Choose $s\in I$ such that $a(s)\ne0$. By Definition 1.3.1, we have

$$ a(s)s + \sum_{t\in I\setminus\{s\}}a(t)t = 0_V.$$

Since $a(s)\ne 0$,

$$ \begin{align}
s &= (-a(s))^{-1}\sum_{t\in I\setminus\{s\}}a(t)t \\
&= \sum_{t\in I\setminus\{s\}}(-a(s)^{-1}a(t))t.
\end{align}$$

Therefore $s\in \operatorname{span}(I\setminus\{s\})$, so $I$ is linearly dependent by Proposition 1.5.3. It follows from Proposition 1.5.4(1) that $S$ is linearly dependent.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


**Proposition 1.5.7.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $S$ be a subset of $V$.
1. The set $S$ is linearly dependent if $0_V\in S$.
2. The empty set is linearly independent.
3. The set $S$ is linearly independent if it consists of a single nonzero vector.

*Proof.* (1) Let $I=\{0_V\}$ and let $a_{0_V} = 1$. Then, by Definition 1.3.1, 

$$ \sum_{v\in I} a_v v = 1\cdot 0_V = 0_V.$$

By Theorem 1.5.6, $S$ is linearly dependent.
(2) The empty set does not have nontrivial representation of $0_V$. By Theorem 1.5.6, $S$ is linearly independent.
(3) Let $S=\lbrace v\rbrace$ and $I\subseteq S$. Since $a_v \ne 0$ for some $v\in I$, we must choose $I=\{v\}$. Then, by Definition 1.3.1, 

$$ \sum_{v\in I} a_v v = a_v v \ne 0_V.$$

By Theorem 1.5.6, $S$ is linearly independent.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Example 1.5.8.** Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer and define

$$ p_k(x)=\sum_{i=k}^{n}x^i \qquad (0\le k\le n). $$

Then the set $S = \{ p_k(x) : 0\le k\le n\}$ is linearly independent in $F_n[x]$. Indeed, by Proposition 1.3.7(2) and Corollary 1.3.6,

$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k=0}^{n}a_kp_k(x) &=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_k\left(\sum_{i=k}^{n}x^i\right)\\ 
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{i=k}^{n}a_kx^i\\
&=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{i}a_kx^i\\
&=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{i}a_k\right)x^i \end{aligned} $$

for any scalars $a_0,\dots,a_n\in F$. Therefore, if 

$$ \sum_{k=0}^{n}a_kp_k(x) = \mathbf 0$$

for some $a_0,\dots,a_n\in F$, then $a_0 = \cdots =a_n = 0$. Hence $S$ does not have nontrivial representation of $0_V$. By Theorem 1.5.6, $S$ is linearly independent.

The notion of linear independence, as well as linear dependence, can be formulated in the language of linear algebra, as the following corollary shows.

**Corollary 1.5.9.** A subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$ is linearly independent if and only if for every $v\in V$, there exists at most one pair $(I,a)$, where $I\subseteq S$ is finite and $a:I\to F\setminus\{0\}$ is a function, such that

$$ v=\sum_{s\in I}a(s)s. $$

*Proof.* Suppose that $S$ is linearly independent. Let $v\in V$ and suppose that

$$ v=\sum_{s\in I}a(s)s=\sum_{s\in J}b(s)s $$

for finite subsets $I,J\subseteq S$ and functions $a:I\to F\setminus\{0\}$ and $b:J\to F\setminus\{0\}$. Put $K=I\cup J$, and extend $a$ and $b$ to functions $\widetilde a,\widetilde b:K\to F$ by setting them equal to $0$ outside $I$ and $J$, respectively. By Proposition 1.3.3 and Proposition 1.3.7(1), (2),

$$ \begin{aligned}
\sum_{s\in K}(\widetilde a(s)-\widetilde b(s))s
&=\sum_{s\in I}a(s)s-\sum_{s\in J}b(s)s\\
&=0_V.
\end{aligned} $$

Since $K\subseteq S$, Theorem 1.5.6 gives $\widetilde a(s)=\widetilde b(s)$ for every $s\in K$. Since $a$ and $b$ take only nonzero values,

$$ I=\{s\in K:\widetilde a(s)\ne0\}=\{s\in K:\widetilde b(s)\ne0\}=J. $$

Restricting $\widetilde a=\widetilde b$ to this common domain gives $a=b$.
Conversely, suppose that the stated uniqueness condition holds. If $S$ were linearly dependent, then Theorem 1.5.6, after discarding terms with zero coefficients, would give a nonempty finite subset $I\subseteq S$ and a function $a:I\to F\setminus\{0\}$ such that

$$ 0_V=\sum_{s\in I}a(s)s. $$

Since the empty sum is also a representation of $0_V$, this contradicts the uniqueness condition. Hence $S$ is linearly independent.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

## Exercises
**1.** Let $u$ and $v$ be distinct vectors in a vector space $V$. Show that $\{u,v\}$ is linearly independent if and only if $u\notin \operatorname{span}\{v\}$ and $v\notin \operatorname{span}\{u\}$.

*Proof.* By Proposition 1.5.3, $\{u,v\}$ is linearly independent if and only if there are no redundant vectors in $\{u,v\}$, equivalently, if and only if $u\notin \operatorname{span}\{v\}$ and $v\notin \operatorname{span}\{u\}$.

**2.** The *characteristic* of a field $F$, denoted by $\operatorname{ch}(F)$, is defined to be the least positive integer $n$ such that

$$ \underbrace{1+1+\cdots + 1}_{n\text{ times}}=0$$

if such an $n$ exists; otherwise, the characteristic is defined to be $0$.
Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$ with $\operatorname{ch}(F)\ne 2$. 
1. Let $u,v\in V$ be distinct. Prove that $\{u,v\}$ is linearly independent if and only if $\{u+v,u-v\}$ is linearly independent. 
2. Let $u,v,w\in V$ be distinct. Prove that $\{u,v,w\}$ is linearly independent if and only if $\{u+v,u+w,v+w\}$ is linearly independent. 

**3.** Prove that a subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ is linearly independent if and only if every finite subset of $S$ is linearly independent.

*Proof.* If $S$ is linearly independent, then every subset of $S$ is linearly independent by Proposition 1.5.4(2). 
We now prove the converse. Since $\operatorname{span}$ is finitary, for any $v\in V$, if  $v\notin \operatorname{span}(S_0\setminus\{v\})$ for all finite subset $S_0\subseteq S$, then $v\notin \operatorname{span}(S\setminus\{v\})$. By Proposition 1.5.3, $S$ is linearly independent.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**4.** Prove that if $\{A_1,\ldots,A_n\}\subseteq \operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}(F)$ is linearly independent, then $\{A_1^{\!\top},\ldots,A_n^{\!\top}\}$ is linearly independent.

**5.** Let $S=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ be a finite subset of a vector space $V$. Prove that $S$ is linearly independent if and only if $v_1\ne 0_V$ and $v_{k+1}\notin \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots, v_k\}$ for all $k=1,\ldots,n-1$.

**6.** Let $S$ and $T$ be disjoint linearly independent subsets of a vector space $V$. Prove that $S\cup T$ is linearly independent if and only if $\operatorname{span}(S)\cap \operatorname{span}(T) = \{0_V\}$.
