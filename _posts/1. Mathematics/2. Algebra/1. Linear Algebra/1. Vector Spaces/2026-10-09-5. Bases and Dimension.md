The preceding sections reveal two competing requirements on a set of vectors. To generate an entire vector space $V$, the set must contain enough vectors; to avoid redundancy, it must not contain any unnecessary ones. These requirements suggest two complementary extremal problems.
Starting with a spanning set, we seek one that ceases to span $V$ whenever a vector is removed. Starting with a linearly independent set, we seek one that ceases to be linearly independent whenever a new vector from $V$ is added. In terms of set inclusion, the first seeks a _minimal spanning set_, while the second seeks a _maximal linearly independent set_.
As we shall see, these two seemingly different conditions characterize precisely the same sets. Such sets are called bases for $V$.

**Definition 1.5.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space. A subset $S$ of $V$ is called a *basis* for $V$ if $\operatorname{span}(S) = V$ and $S$ is linearly independent.

**Example 1.5.2.** In the vector space $F_n[x]$, the set $\{1,x,x^2,\ldots,x^n\}$ is a basis—the *standard basis* for $F_n[x]$. While $F_n[x]$ has a finite basis, in $F[x]$, the infinite set $\{1,x,x^2,\ldots\}$ is a basis.

**Theorem 1.5.3.** Let $V$ be a vector space and $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ be distinct vectors in $V$. Then, $B=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ is a basis for $V$ if and only if each vector in $V$ can be uniquely expressed as a linear combination of vectors in $B$.
