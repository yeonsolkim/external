**Definition 1.3.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $S$ be a nonempty subset of $V$. A vector $v \in V$ is called a *linear combination* of the vectors in $S$ if there exist a finite number of vectors $u_1,\ldots,u_n\in S$ and scalars $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in F$ such that

$$ v = a_1u_1 + \cdots + a_nu_n.$$

In this case, we also say that $v$ is a linear combination of $u_1, \dots, u_n$, and we call $a_1, \dots, a_n$ the *coefficients* of the linear combination.

**Definition 1.3.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $S$ be a subset of $V$. If $S$ is nonempty, the *span* of $S$ is the set $\operatorname{span}(S)$ defined by

$$
\operatorname{span}(S)
=
\left\lbrace
v\in V : v\text{ is a linear combination of the vectors in } S
\right\rbrace.
$$

In addition, we define $\operatorname{span}(\varnothing) = \{0_V\}$.
If $W$ is a subspace of $V$ and $\operatorname{span} (S) = W$, then we say that $S$ *generates* $W$.

**Example 1.3.3.** For a single vector $v\in V$,

$$\operatorname{span}(\{v\})=\{av:a\in F\}.$$

If $v=0_V$, this is the zero subspace. In Euclidean space $\mathbb R^n$, the span of a nonzero vector is the line through the origin in the direction of that vector.

**Example 1.3.5.** The matrices

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1& 0\end{pmatrix},
\quad \text{and} \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix},
$$

generate $\mathbb R^{2\times 2}$ since for any $2\times 2$ matrix the following holds:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12}\\ a_{21}& a_{22}\end{pmatrix} 
= a_{11} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix}
+ a_{12}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix} 
+ a_{21}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1& 0\end{pmatrix}
+ a_{22}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix}.
$$


On the other hand, consider the matrices

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\quad \text{and} \quad
C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Their linear combinations have the form

$$
\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C
=\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha+\beta+\gamma&\beta\\
\gamma&\alpha+\beta+\gamma
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The two diagonal entries are always equal, so these matrices do not generate $\mathbb R^{2\times2}$. Their span is exactly

$$
\operatorname{span}(\{A,B,C\})
=\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}t&u\\v&t\end{pmatrix}
:t,u,v\in\mathbb R
\right\}.
$$

Thus the same subspace can be described by a condition on its elements or by a set of vectors that generates it. The condition identifies which matrices belong to the subspace; the generating set identifies the matrices from which its elements can be built.

**Example 1.3.8.** Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer. The polynomial subspace $F_n[x]$ from Example 1.2.4 has the generating set

$$F_n[x]=\operatorname{span}(\{1,x,\ldots,x^n\}).$$

The space of all polynomials has the infinite generating set

$$F[x]=\operatorname{span}(\{x^k:k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\}).$$

A polynomial $p(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_mx^m$ uses only finitely many of these generators. Even when a generating set is infinite, each vector in its span is a finite linear combination; no infinite sums are included in the definition of span. The space $F[x]$ cannot be generated by any finite subset.

**Proposition 1.3.3.** A subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if $\operatorname{span}(S)=S$.


The notion of span has another important meaning: the span of a subset $S$ is the smallest subspace containing $S$.

**Theorem 1.3.3.** Let $S$ be a subset of a vector space $V$.

1. $\operatorname{span} (S)$ is a subspace of $V$ that contains $S.$
2. Every subspace of $V$ that contains $S$ contains $\operatorname{span} (S).$



A subspace already contains every linear combination of its elements. Enlarging a generating set can enlarge its span, but cannot make the span smaller. Once a subspace has been generated, taking its span again does not add any new vectors.

**Corollary 1.3.4.** Let $S$ and $T$ be subsets of a vector space $V$.

1. If $S\subseteq T$, then $\operatorname{span}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(T)$.
2. $\operatorname{span}(\operatorname{span}(S))=\operatorname{span}(S)$.



We can now connect span with the sum of subspaces introduced in the preceding section. Combining generating sets corresponds to adding the subspaces they generate.

**Theorem 1.3.7.** For any subsets $S_1,S_2$ of a vector space $V$,

$$
\operatorname{span}(S_1\cup S_2)
=\operatorname{span}(S_1)+\operatorname{span}(S_2).
$$



By means of the notion of span, now we can restate Theorem 1.2.11 more clearly. 

**Corollary 1.3.8.** If $W_1$ and $W_2$ are subspaces of $V$, then

$$W_1+W_2=\operatorname{span}(W_1\cup W_2)$$



## Exercises

**Exercise 1.3.1.** Let $S_1$ and $S_2$ be subsets of a vector space $V$. 

1. Prove that
   
   $$
	\operatorname{span}(S_1\cap S_2)
	\subseteq
	\operatorname{span}(S_1)\cap\operatorname{span}(S_2).
	$$
1. Give an example in $\mathbb R^2$ for which the inclusion is strict. 


**Exercise 1.3.2.** Let $W$ be a subspace of a vector space $V$ over $F$, and let $S\subseteq V$. 

1. Prove that $\{s+W:s\in S\}$ generates $W$ if and only if 
   
   $$ W+\operatorname{span}(S)=V. $$

1. Apply this criterion to the quotient space in Example 1.2.18, with $S=\{(0,1)\}$.
2. Determine whether $\{s+W:s\in S\}$ generates $V/W$ and whether $S$ generates $V$. 
3. Explain how the two questions in (3) differ when changes by elements of $W$ are regarded as insignificant.
