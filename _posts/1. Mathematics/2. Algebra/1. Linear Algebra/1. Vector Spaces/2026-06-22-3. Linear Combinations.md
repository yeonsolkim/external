**Definition 1.3.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $S$ be a nonempty subset of $V$. A vector $v \in V$ is called a *linear combination* of the vectors in $S$ if there exist a finite number of vectors $u_1,\ldots,u_n\in S$ and scalars $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in F$ such that 

$$ v = a_1u_1 + \cdots + a_nu_n.$$

In this case, we also say that $v$ is a linear combination of $u_1, \dots, u_n$, and we call $a_1, \dots, a_n$ the *coefficients* of the linear combination.

**Definition 1.3.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $S$ be a subset of $V$. If $S$ is nonempty, the *span* of $S$ is the set $\operatorname{span}(S)$ defined by

$$
\operatorname{span}(S)
=
\left\lbrace
v\in V : v\text{ is a linear combination of the vectors in } S
\right\rbrace.
$$

If $S$ is empty, we define $\operatorname{span}(S) = \{0_V\}$.


The notion of span has another important meaning: the span of $S$ is the smallest subspace containing $S$.


**Theorem 1.3.3.** Let $S$ be a subset of a vector space $V$.
1. $\operatorname{span} (S)$ is a subspace of $V$ that contains $S.$
2. Every subspace of $V$ that contains $S$ contains $\operatorname{span} (S).$



So far, we have started with a subset $S$ and considered the subspace generated by it. We now reverse this point of view: given a vector space $V$, we ask which subsets of $V$ are sufficient to produce every vector in $V$.


**Definition 1.3.4.** A subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ is said to *generate* $V$ if

$$\operatorname{span} (S) = V.$$


**Example 1.3.5.** The matrices 

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1& 0\end{pmatrix},
\quad \text{and} \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix},
$$

generate $\mathbb R^{2\times 2}$ since for any $2\times 2$ matrix the following holds:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12}\\ a_{21}& a_{22}\end{pmatrix} 
= a_{11} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix}
+ a_{12}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix} 
+ a_{21}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1& 0\end{pmatrix}
+ a_{22}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix}.
$$


On the other hand, consider the matrices 

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix},
\quad \text{and} \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 1& 1\end{pmatrix},
$$

each of these having the same main diagonal. Then, they do not generate $\mathbb R^{2\times 2}$. It is because any matrix with the main diagonal of distinct entries cannot be expressed as a linear combination of the three given matrices.
