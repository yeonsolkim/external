**Definitions of linear combinations and spans.** We begin with the vectors that can be built from a given set of vectors by addition and scalar multiplication.

**Definition 1.3.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $S$ be a nonempty subset of $V$. A vector $v \in V$ is called a *linear combination* of the vectors in $S$ if there exist a finite number of vectors $u_1,\ldots,u_n\in S$ and scalars $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in F$ such that

$$ v = a_1u_1 + \cdots + a_nu_n.$$

In this case, we also say that $v$ is a linear combination of $u_1, \dots, u_n$, and we call $a_1, \dots, a_n$ the *coefficients* of the linear combination.

**Definition 1.3.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $S$ be a subset of $V$. If $S$ is nonempty, the *span* of $S$ is the set $\operatorname{span}(S)$ defined by

$$
\operatorname{span}(S)
=
\left\lbrace
v\in V : v\text{ is a linear combination of the vectors in } S
\right\rbrace.
$$

In addition, we define $\operatorname{span}(\varnothing) = \{0_V\}$.
If $W$ is a subspace of $V$ and $\operatorname{span} (S) = W$, then we say that $S$ *generates* $W$.


A subspace is closed under addition and scalar multiplication, so it contains every linear combination of its vectors; that is, every subspace generates itself. This property in fact characterizes subspaces: a subspace is precisely a subset that already contains every vector it generates.

**Proposition 1.3.3.** A subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if $\operatorname{span}(S)=S$.

*Proof.* If $S$ is a subspace of $V$, then $\operatorname{span}(S)\subseteq S$. Since we have $S\subseteq \operatorname{span}(S)$, it follows that $\operatorname{span}(S)=S$.
Conversely, if $\operatorname{span}(S)=S$, then $S\ne \varnothing$ and $\operatorname{span}(S)\subseteq S$. Since there is $v\in S$, $0_V\in S$. In addition, $u,v\in S$ implies $u+v\in S$ and $av\in S$ for all $u,v\in S$ and $a\in F$. Theorem 1.2.2 completes the proof.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Example 1.3.4.** For a single vector $v\in V$,

$$\operatorname{span}\{v\}=\{av:a\in F\}.$$

If $v=0_V$, this is the zero subspace. In Euclidean space $\mathbb R^n$, the span of a nonzero vector is the line through the origin in the direction of that vector.

**Example 1.3.5.** The matrices

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1& 0\end{pmatrix},
\quad \text{and} \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix},
$$

generate $\mathbb R^{2\times 2}$ since for any $2\times 2$ matrix the following holds:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12}\\ a_{21}& a_{22}\end{pmatrix} 
= a_{11} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix}
+ a_{12}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix} 
+ a_{21}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1& 0\end{pmatrix}
+ a_{22}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix}.
$$


On the other hand, consider the matrices

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\quad \text{and} \quad
C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Their linear combinations have the form

$$
\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C
=\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha+\beta+\gamma&\beta\\
\gamma&\alpha+\beta+\gamma
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The two diagonal entries are always equal, so these matrices do not generate $\mathbb R^{2\times2}$. It follows that 

$$
\operatorname{span}\{A,B,C\}
=\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}t&u\\v&t\end{pmatrix}
:t,u,v\in\mathbb R
\right\}.
$$


Thus the same subspace can be described by a condition on its elements or by a set of vectors that generates it. The condition identifies which vectors belong to the subspace; the generating set identifies the vectors from which the elements of the subspace can be built.

**Example 1.3.6.** Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer. The polynomial subspace $F_n[x]$ from Example 1.2.4 has $\{1,x,\ldots,x^n\}$ as a generating set:

$$F_n[x]=\operatorname{span}\{1,x,\ldots,x^n\}.$$



**Span as a closure operator.** A function maps each input to the output. Viewing it as an operator emphasizes how it *transforms* mathematical objects. When an operator maps a collection of objects to itself, we can compose it with itself and apply it repeatedly. This leads us to study how an object changes under one application, what happens under successive applications, and which objects remain unchanged.
Taking the span of a set can be viewed as an operator, specifically as a closure operator on the power set of a vector space. We begin by studying a closure operator through its relation to inclusion and its behavior under iteration, and then use the general properties of this transformation to describe the span operator.

**Definition 1.3.7.** An operator $\operatorname{cl}$ on the collection of subsets of a set $X$ is called a *closure operator* on $X$ if it satisfies the following axioms: For all $S,T\subseteq X$,
1. $S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$ (*extensive*);
2. if $S\subseteq T$, then $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(T)$ (*monotone*);
3. $\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(S))=\operatorname{cl}(S)$ (*idempotent*).

**Definition 1.3.8.** Let $\operatorname{cl}$ be a closure operator on a set $X$, and let $S\subseteq X$. The set $\operatorname{cl}(S)$ is called the *closure* of $S$. If $S=\operatorname{cl}(S)$, then $S$ is called a *closed set*.

**Proposition 1.3.9.** Let $\operatorname{cl}$ be a closure operator on a set $X$, and let $S,T\subseteq X$. Then, 

1. $\operatorname{cl}(S)\cup \operatorname{cl}(T) \subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S\cup T)$;
2. $\operatorname{cl}(S\cup T) = \operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(S)\cup \operatorname{cl}(T))$.

*Proof.* (1) Since $S,T\subseteq S\cup T$, monotonicity gives $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S\cup T)$ and $\operatorname{cl}(T)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S\cup T)$. Hence $\operatorname{cl}(S)\cup \operatorname{cl}(T) \subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S\cup T)$.
(2) Since $S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$ and $T\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T)$, we have $S\cup T\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)\cup \operatorname{cl}(T)$. The inclusion $\operatorname{cl}(S\cup T) \subseteq \operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(S)\cup \operatorname{cl}(T))$ follows from monotonicity. Since $\operatorname{cl}(S) \cup \operatorname{cl}(T) \subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S\cup T)$ by (1), monotonicity and idempotence give the reverse inclusion.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

The closure axioms can be expressed as a single inclusion criterion: replacing a set by its closure does not change whether it lies inside a closed set.

**Theorem 1.3.10.** Let $X$ be a set. An operator $\operatorname{cl}:\mathcal P(X)\to \mathcal P(X)$ is a closure operator on $X$ if and only if

$$ S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T) \iff \operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T) \tag{$\ast$}$$

for any $S,T\subseteq X$.

*Proof.* Suppose $\operatorname{cl}$ is a closure operator on $X$. Since $\operatorname{cl}$ is monotone and idempotent, if $S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T)$, then $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(T))=\operatorname{cl}(T)$. Since $\operatorname{cl}$ is extensive, if $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T)$, then $S\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T)$.
Conversely, suppose $(\ast)$ holds for any $S,T\subseteq X$. Since $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$, we have $S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$; hence $\operatorname{cl}$ is extensive. Since $\operatorname{cl}$ is extensive, if $S\subseteq T$, then $S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T)$. Then $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T)$ follows from $(\ast)$; $\operatorname{cl}$ is monotone. The inclusion $\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(S))\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$ follows from $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$ by $(\ast)$. Since extensivity gives the reverse inclusion, $\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(S))=\operatorname{cl}(S)$; $\operatorname{cl}$ is idempotent.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>



We now turn from the behavior of the operator to the property of the collection $\mathcal C$ of all its closed sets. A union of closed sets need not be closed; applying the closure operator produces a closed set containing that union, as in Proposition 1.3.9(2). An arbitrary intersection of closed sets, however, is already closed. The following theorem establishes this intersection property of $\mathcal C$. 


**Theorem 1.3.11.** Let $\operatorname{cl}$ be a closure operator on a set $X$. The collection $\mathcal C$ of all closed subsets of $X$ is a Moore collection.

*Proof.* Let $\{S_i\}_{i\in I}$ be any collection of elements of $\mathcal C$. If $I=\varnothing$, its intersection is $X$. Since $\operatorname{cl}(X)\subseteq X$ and extensivity gives the reverse inclusion, $\operatorname{cl}(X)=X$. Thus the empty intersection belongs to $\mathcal C$.
Now assume $I\ne\varnothing$, and put $S=\bigcap_{i\in I}S_i$. For every $i\in I$, we have $S\subseteq S_i$. By monotonicity and the fact that $S_i$ is closed, $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(S_i)=S_i$. It follows that $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq S$. Since $\operatorname{cl}$ is extensive, $S\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(S)$, so $\operatorname{cl}(S)=S$. Hence $S$ is closed and belongs to $\mathcal C$. Therefore $\mathcal C$ is closed under arbitrary intersections, including the empty intersection, and is a Moore collection by Definition 1.2.8.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

We now reverse the point of view: once $\mathcal C$ is known, the value $\operatorname{cl}(S)$ can be recovered for every $S\subseteq X$ by intersecting all members of $\mathcal C$ that contain $S$. Thus the collection of closed sets completely determines the closure operator.

**Theorem 1.3.12.** Let $\operatorname{cl}$ be a closure operator on a set $X$, and let $\mathcal C$ be the collection of all closed subsets of $X$. Then, for all $S\subseteq X$,

   $$\operatorname{cl}(S) = \bigcap \{C\in \mathcal C: C\supseteq S\}$$

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal C_{S}=\{C\in \mathcal C: C\supseteq S\}$. Since $\operatorname{cl}(S)\in \mathcal C_S$, we have the first inclusion $\bigcap \mathcal C_S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$. By Theorem 1.3.10, for all $C\in \mathcal C_S$, $S\subseteq C$ implies $\operatorname{cl}(S) \subseteq C$. Therefore we have the second inclusion $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \bigcap \mathcal C_S$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


For a fixed $S\subseteq X$, Theorem 1.3.12 also has a pointwise interpretation. The closure $\operatorname{cl}(S)$ is itself closed, contains $S$, and is contained in every closed set containing $S$. Hence $\operatorname{cl}(S)$ is the smallest closed set containing $S$.
We now show that span is a closure operator on a vector space.

**Theorem 1.3.13.** Let $V$ be a vector space. The self-map

$$\operatorname{span}:\mathcal P(V)\to \mathcal P(V), \qquad S\mapsto \operatorname{span}(S)$$ 

is a closure operator. In addition, a subset $S$ of $V$ is closed if and only if $S$ is a subspace of $V$.

*Proof.* Since Definition 1.3.2 guarantees that $\operatorname{span}(S)\in \mathcal P(V)$ for all $S\in \mathcal P(V)$, the operation is well-defined.
Firstly, if $S=\varnothing$, then $S\subseteq \operatorname{span}(S)=\{0_V\}$. Suppose $S\ne\varnothing$. Since $v=1v\in \operatorname{span}(S)$ for every $v\in S$, we have $S\subseteq \operatorname{span}(S)$. Hence $\operatorname{span}$ is extensive.
Secondly, suppose $S,T\in \mathcal P(V)$ and $S\subseteq T$. If $S=T=\varnothing$, then $\operatorname{span}(S)=\{0_V\}=\operatorname{span}(T)$. If $S=\varnothing$ and $T\ne \varnothing$, then there exists $v\in T$ and $0_V=0v\in \operatorname{span}(T)$. Hence $\operatorname{span}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{span}(T)$. If $S\ne \varnothing$ and $v\in \operatorname{span}(S)$, then there exist $v_1,\ldots, v_n\in S$ and $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in F$ such that $v=a_1v_1 + \cdots + a_nv_n$. Since $S\subseteq T$, we have $v_1,\ldots, v_n\in T$. So $v\in \operatorname{span}(T)$. Hence $\operatorname{span}$ is monotone.
Lastly, we check idempotence. If $S=\varnothing$, then $\operatorname{span}(S)=\{0_V\}$ is the zero subspace, so $\operatorname{span}(\operatorname{span}(S))=\operatorname{span}(S)$ by Proposition 1.3.3. Now assume $S\ne\varnothing$. If $v\in\operatorname{span}(\operatorname{span}(S))$, then there exist a positive integer $n$, vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_n\in\operatorname{span}(S)$, and scalars $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in F$ such that $v=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i v_i$. For each $i$, there exist a positive integer $m_i$, vectors $u_{ij}\in S$, and scalars $b_{ij}\in F$ such that $v_i=\sum_{j=1}^{m_i}b_{ij}u_{ij}$. Thus

$$v=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^{m_i}(a_i b_{ij})u_{ij}.$$

This is a finite linear combination of vectors in $S$, so $v\in\operatorname{span}(S)$. Therefore $\operatorname{span}(\operatorname{span}(S))\subseteq\operatorname{span}(S)$. Extensivity gives the reverse inclusion, so $\operatorname{span}(\operatorname{span}(S))=\operatorname{span}(S)$; $\operatorname{span}$ is idempotent.
By Proposition 1.3.3, a subset $S$ of $V$ is closed if and only if $S$ is a subspace of $V$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


Since span is a closure operator whose closed sets are precisely the subspaces, Theorem 1.3.12 specializes immediately to the following description.

**Corollary 1.3.14.** Let $S$ be a subset of a vector space $V$. Then,

$$
\operatorname{span}(S)
=\bigcap\{W\subseteq V: W\text{ is a subspace containing }S\}.
$$



In general closure-operator theory, to combine two closed sets is to take the closure of the union of the two sets. In the setting of vector spaces, this construction corresponds to taking the span of two subspaces $U$ and $W$. Theorem 1.2.12 and Corollary 1.3.14 characterize $\operatorname{span}(U\cup W)$ and $U+W$ by the same universal property: each is the smallest subspace containing both $U$ and $W$. Therefore, 

$$  
\operatorname{span}(U\cup W)=U+W.  
$$

Thus, the sum of subspaces is the specific realization of the general construction of combining two closed sets. The next corollary extends this connection from subspaces to arbitrary subsets $S$ and $T$.

**Corollary 1.3.15.** For any subsets $S$ and $T$ of a vector space $V$,

$$
\operatorname{span}(S\cup T)=\operatorname{span}(S)+\operatorname{span}(T).
$$

*Proof.* By Theorem 1.3.13, $\operatorname{span}(S\cup T)$ is a subspace. It contains both $\operatorname{span}(S)$ and $\operatorname{span}(T)$ by Proposition 1.3.9(1). Theorem 1.2.12(2) then gives the inclusion $\operatorname{span}(S)+\operatorname{span}(T) \subseteq \operatorname{span}(S\cup T)$. 
On the other hand, $\operatorname{span}(S)+\operatorname{span}(T)$ is a subspace by Theorem 1.2.12(1). It contains $S\cup T$ by the same theorem and extensivity. Corollary 1.3.14 therefore gives the reverse inclusion.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Span has two further properties that distinguish it from an arbitrary closure operator. Both will be especially important in the study of linear dependence, bases, and dimension developed in the following sections.
The first is *finite character*: although a generating set may be infinite, membership in its span is always witnessed by finitely many generators.

**Proposition 1.3.16.** Let $S$ be a subset of a vector space $V$, and let $v\in V$. Then $v\in\operatorname{span}(S)$ if and only if there exists a finite subset $S_0\subseteq S$ such that $v\in\operatorname{span}(S_0)$.

**Example 1.3.17.** The vector space $F[x]$ of all polynomials has an infinite generating set:

$$F[x]=\operatorname{span}\{x^k:k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\}.$$

Any polynomial $p(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n$ in $F[x]$, however, uses only finitely many of these generators: 

$$ p(x) \in \operatorname{span}\{1,x,\ldots,x^n\}.$$



The second is the *exchange property*. Suppose that adjoining $y$ to a set $S$ makes it possible to generate a vector $x$ that could not be generated from $S$ alone. Then $x$ can take the role of $y$: after adjoining $x$ instead, the vector $y$ can be recovered.

**Theorem 1.3.18 (Exchange property).** Let $S$ be a subset of a vector space $V$, and let $u,v\in V$. 
1. If $u\in\operatorname{span}(S\cup\{v\})\setminus\operatorname{span}(S)$, then $v\in\operatorname{span}(S\cup\{u\})$.
2. Under the same hypothesis, $\operatorname{span}(S\cup\{u\}) = \operatorname{span}(S\cup\{v\})$.

*Proof.* Suppose 

$$u\in\operatorname{span}(S\cup\{v\})\setminus\operatorname{span}(S).$$

By Corollary 1.3.15, there exist $s\in \operatorname{span}(S)$ and $a\in F$ such that 

$$u = s + av.$$ 

Since $u\notin\operatorname{span}(S)$, we must have $a\ne0$. It follows that

$$v=a^{-1}u - a^{-1}s \in \operatorname{span}(S\cup \{u\}).$$


We now prove the equality. Since $u\in\operatorname{span}(S\cup\{v\})$, we have 

$$S\cup \{u\}\subseteq \operatorname{span}(S\cup \{v\}).$$

Since the right hand side is closed, Theorem 1.3.10 gives the inclusion

$$\operatorname{span}(S\cup\{u\}) \subseteq \operatorname{span}(S\cup\{v\}).$$

The reverse inclusion follows similarly from $v\in\operatorname{span}(S\cup\{u\})$. Therefore the equality holds. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.3.1.** Let $S$ and $T$ be subsets of a vector space $V$. 

1. Prove that
   $$
	\operatorname{span}(S\cap T)
	\subseteq
	\operatorname{span}(S)\cap\operatorname{span}(T).
	$$
2. Give an example in $\mathbb R^2$ for which the inclusion is strict.


**Exercise 1.3.2.** Let $W$ be a subspace of a vector space $V$ over $F$, and let $S\subseteq V$. 

1. Prove that $\{s+W:s\in S\}$ generates $V/W$ if and only if
   
   $$ W+\operatorname{span}(S)=V. $$

2. Apply this criterion to the quotient space in Example 1.2.19, with $S=\{(0,1)\}$.
3. Determine whether $\{s+W:s\in S\}$ generates $V/W$ and whether $S$ generates $V$.
4. Explain how the two questions in (3) differ when changes by elements of $W$ are regarded as insignificant.
