**Definitions of linear combinations and spans.** We begin with the vectors that can be built from a given set of vectors by addition and scalar multiplication.

**Definition 1.3.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $S$ be a nonempty subset of $V$. A vector $v \in V$ is called a *linear combination* of the vectors in $S$ if there exist a finite number of vectors $u_1,\ldots,u_n\in S$ and scalars $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in F$ such that

$$ v = a_1u_1 + \cdots + a_nu_n.$$

In this case, we also say that $v$ is a linear combination of $u_1, \dots, u_n$, and we call $a_1, \dots, a_n$ the *coefficients* of the linear combination.

**Definition 1.3.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $S$ be a subset of $V$. If $S$ is nonempty, the *span* of $S$ is the set $\operatorname{span}(S)$ defined by

$$
\operatorname{span}(S)
=
\left\lbrace
v\in V : v\text{ is a linear combination of the vectors in } S
\right\rbrace.
$$

In addition, we define $\operatorname{span}(\varnothing) = \{0_V\}$.
If $W$ is a subspace of $V$ and $\operatorname{span} (S) = W$, then we say that $S$ *generates* $W$.


A subspace is closed under addition and scalar multiplication, so it contains every linear combination of its vectors; that is, every subspace generates itself. This property in fact characterizes subspaces: a subspace is precisely a subset that already contains every vector it generates.

**Proposition 1.3.3.** A subset $S$ of a vector space $V$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if $\operatorname{span}(S)=S$.

*Proof.* If $S$ is a subspace of $V$, then $\operatorname{span}(S)\subseteq S$. Since we have $S\subseteq \operatorname{span}(S)$, it follows that $\operatorname{span}(S)=S$.
Conversely, if $\operatorname{span}(S)=S$, then $S\ne \varnothing$ and $\operatorname{span}(S)\subseteq S$. Since there is $v\in S$, $0_V\in S$. In addition, $u,v\in S$ implies $u+v\in S$ and $av\in S$ for all $u,v\in S$ and $a\in F$. Theorem 1.2.2 completes the proof.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Example 1.3.4.** For a single vector $v\in V$,

$$\operatorname{span}(\{v\})=\{av:a\in F\}.$$

If $v=0_V$, this is the zero subspace. In Euclidean space $\mathbb R^n$, the span of a nonzero vector is the line through the origin in the direction of that vector.

**Example 1.3.5.** The matrices

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1& 0\end{pmatrix},
\quad \text{and} \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix},
$$

generate $\mathbb R^{2\times 2}$ since for any $2\times 2$ matrix the following holds:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12}\\ a_{21}& a_{22}\end{pmatrix} 
= a_{11} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix}
+ a_{12}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix} 
+ a_{21}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1& 0\end{pmatrix}
+ a_{22}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix}.
$$


On the other hand, consider the matrices

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\quad \text{and} \quad
C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Their linear combinations have the form

$$
\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C
=\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha+\beta+\gamma&\beta\\
\gamma&\alpha+\beta+\gamma
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The two diagonal entries are always equal, so these matrices do not generate $\mathbb R^{2\times2}$. It follows that 

$$
\operatorname{span}(\{A,B,C\})
=\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}t&u\\v&t\end{pmatrix}
:t,u,v\in\mathbb R
\right\}.
$$


Thus the same subspace can be described by a condition on its elements or by a set of vectors that generates it. The condition identifies which vectors belong to the subspace; the generating set identifies the vectors from which the elements of the subspace can be built.

**Example 1.3.6.** Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer. The polynomial subspace $F_n[x]$ from Example 1.2.4 has the finite generating set

$$F_n[x]=\operatorname{span}(\{1,x,\ldots,x^n\}).$$


The space of all polynomials has the infinite generating set

$$F[x]=\operatorname{span}(\{x^k:k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\}).$$

A polynomial $p(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_mx^m$, however, uses only finitely many of these generators. Even when a generating set is infinite, each vector in its span is a finite linear combination; no infinite sums are included in the definition of span. 


**Characterization of span: The smallest containing subspace.** Definition 1.3.2 describes $\operatorname{span}(S)$ from below, as the set of vectors built from $S$, the set of ingredients. We can also characterize it from above: The span of a subset $S$ is the smallest subspace containing $S$.

**Theorem 1.3.7.** Let $S$ be a subset of a vector space $V$.

1. The span of $S$ is a subspace of $V$ that contains $S.$
2. Every subspace of $V$ that contains $S$ contains $\operatorname{span} (S).$

*Proof.* (1) If $S=\varnothing$, then $\operatorname{span}(S)=\{0_V\}$ is a subspace containing $S$ and is contained in every subspace of $V$, so both assertions hold. 
Assume $S\ne\varnothing$. Since $v=1v\in\operatorname{span}(S)$ for every $v\in S$, we have $S\subseteq\operatorname{span}(S)$. Let $v\in S$. Then $0_V=0v\in\operatorname{span}(S)$. If $s,t\in \operatorname{span}(S)$, then there exist $m,n\in \mathbb Z^+$, $u_i,v_j\in S$, and $a_i,b_j\in F$ such that 

$$
s=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_i u_i
\quad\text{and}\quad
t=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_j v_j.
$$

For any $c\in F$, we have

$$
s+t=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_i u_i+\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_j v_j\quad \text{and} \quad
cs=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(ca_i)u_i.
$$

Both expressions are finite linear combinations of vectors in $S$, so $s+t$ and $cs$ belong to $\operatorname{span}(S)$. Therefore $\operatorname{span}(S)$ is a subspace of $V$ by Theorem 1.2.2.
(2) Let $W$ be a subspace of $V$ containing $S$. If $v\in \operatorname{span}(S)$, then there exist a finite number of $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in F$ and $u_1,\ldots,u_n\in S$ such that $v = a_1u_1 + \cdots + a_nu_n$. Since $u_1,\ldots,u_n\in W$ and $W$ is closed, $v = a_1u_1 + \cdots + a_nu_n\in W$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Span: A closure operator.** A function maps each input to the output. Viewing it as an operator emphasizes how it *transforms* mathematical objects. When an operator maps a collection of objects to itself, we can compose it with itself and apply it repeatedly. This leads us to study how an object changes under one application, what happens under successive applications, and which objects remain unchanged. 
Span acts on subsets of a vector space $V$, transforming each subset into the smallest subspace containing it. We can study this transformation through its relation to inclusion, its behavior under iteration, and its fixed points. 
We begin by introducing the general notion of a closure operator. 

**Definition 1.3.8.** An operator $\operatorname{cl}$ on the collection of subsets of a set $X$ is called a *closure operator* on $X$ if it satisfies the following axioms: For all $S,T\subseteq X$, 
1. $S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$ ($\operatorname{cl}$ is *extensive*);
2. if $S\subseteq T$, then $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(T)$ ($\operatorname{cl}$ is *monotone*);
3. $\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(S))=\operatorname{cl}(S)$ ($\operatorname{cl}$ is *idempotent*).

**Definition 1.3.9.** Let $\operatorname{cl}$ be a closure operator on a set $X$, and let $S\subseteq X$. The set $\operatorname{cl}(S)$ is called the *closure* of $S$. If $S=\operatorname{cl}(S)$, then $S$ is called a *closed set*.

**Theorem 1.3.10.** An operator $\operatorname{cl}$ on the collection of subsets of a set $X$ is a closure operator on $X$ if and only if 

$$ S\subseteq C \iff \operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq C\tag{\(\ast\)}$$

for any closed set $C$ and any subset $S$ of $X$.

*Proof.* Suppose $\operatorname{cl}$ is a closure operator on $X$. Let $C$ be any closed set. Since $\operatorname{cl}$ is monotone and idempotent, if $S\subseteq C$, then $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(C)=C$. Since $\operatorname{cl}$ is extensive, if $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq C$, then $S\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq C$.
Conversely, suppose $(\ast)$ holds. Since $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$, we have $S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$; hence $\operatorname{cl}$ is extensive. It follows that if $S\subseteq T$, then $S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T)$. Since $\operatorname{cl}(T)$ is closed, $S\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T)$ implies $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(T)$; $\operatorname{cl}$ is monotone. Since $\operatorname{cl}(S)$ is closed, $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$ implies $\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(S))\subseteq \operatorname{cl}(S)$ by $(\ast)$. Extensivity now gives $\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(S))=\operatorname{cl}(S)$; $\operatorname{cl}$ is idempotent.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


**Theorem 1.3.11.** Let $\operatorname{cl}$ be a closure operator on a set $X$. 
1. The collection $\mathcal C$ of all closed subsets of $X$ is a Moore collection.
2. $\operatorname{cl}(S) = \bigcap \{C\subseteq \mathcal C: C\supseteq S\}$ for any $S\subseteq X$.

*Proof.* (1) Let $\{S_i\}_{i\in I}$ be any collection of elements of $\mathcal C$. If $I=\varnothing$, its intersection is $X$. Since $\operatorname{cl}(X)\subseteq X$ and extensivity gives the reverse inclusion, $\operatorname{cl}(X)=X$. Thus the empty intersection belongs to $\mathcal C$.
Now assume $I\ne\varnothing$, and put $S=\bigcap_{i\in I}S_i$. For every $i\in I$, we have $S\subseteq S_i$. By monotonicity and the fact that $S_i$ is closed, $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(S_i)=S_i$. It follows that $\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq S$. Since $\operatorname{cl}$ is extensive, $S\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(S)$, so $\operatorname{cl}(S)=S$. Hence $S$ is closed and belongs to $\mathcal C$. Therefore $\mathcal C$ is closed under arbitrary intersections, including the empty intersection, and is a Moore collection by Definition 1.2.8.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 1.3.12.** Let $V$ be a vector space. The self-map 

$$\operatorname{span}:\mathcal P(V)\to \mathcal P(V), \qquad S\mapsto \operatorname{span}(S)$$ 

is a closure operator. In addition, a subset $S$ of $V$ is closed if and only if $S$ is a subspace of $V$.

*Proof.* Extensivity follows from Theorem 1.3.7(1). Since $\operatorname{span}(T)$ is a subspace containing $T$, $\operatorname{span}(T)$ is a subspace also containing $S$. Since $\operatorname{span}(S)$ is the smallest subspace containing $S$, $\operatorname{span}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(T)$; $\operatorname{span}$ is monotone. Let $W=\operatorname{span}(S)$. Then $W$ is a subspace of $V$, which contains $W$. It therefore follows that $\operatorname{span}(W)\subseteq W$ by Theorem 1.3.7(2). Since $\operatorname{span}(W)$ contains $W$ by Theorem 1.3.7(1), we have $\operatorname{span}(W)=W$; $\operatorname{span}$ is idempotent. Therefore $\operatorname{span}$ is a closure operator.
Proposition 1.3.3 shows that a subset $S$ of $V$ is closed if and only if $S$ is a subspace of $V$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Proposition 1.3.13.** For any subsets $S$ and $T$ of a vector space $V$,

$$
\operatorname{span}(S\cup T)
=\operatorname{span}\left(\operatorname{span}(S)\cup
\operatorname{span}(T)\right)
=\operatorname{span}(S)+\operatorname{span}(T).
$$




## Exercises

**Exercise 1.3.1.** Let $S$ and $T$ be subsets of a vector space $V$. 

1. Prove that
   $$
	\operatorname{span}(S\cap T)
	\subseteq
	\operatorname{span}(S)\cap\operatorname{span}(T).
	$$
2. Give an example in $\mathbb R^2$ for which the inclusion is strict.


**Exercise 1.3.2.** Let $W$ be a subspace of a vector space $V$ over $F$, and let $S\subseteq V$. 

1. Prove that $\{s+W:s\in S\}$ generates $V/W$ if and only if
   
   $$ W+\operatorname{span}(S)=V. $$

2. Apply this criterion to the quotient space in Example 1.2.19, with $S=\{(0,1)\}$.
3. Determine whether $\{s+W:s\in S\}$ generates $V/W$ and whether $S$ generates $V$.
4. Explain how the two questions in (3) differ when changes by elements of $W$ are regarded as insignificant.
