**The finite sum of an indexed family.** Linear combinations are finite sums of scalar multiples of vectors. To define them precisely, we first formalize finite sums without imposing an order on their terms. 

**Definition 1.3.1.** Let $(M,+,0_M)$ be a commutative monoid, and let $\{x_i\}_{i\in I}$ be a family of elements of $M$ indexed by a finite set $I$. The *sum* of this family is defined recursively by

$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i\in\varnothing}x_i &=0_M,\\ \sum_{i\in I}x_i &=x_j+\sum_{ i\in I\setminus\{j\}}x_i \quad \text{if } j\in I. \end{aligned} $$

The sum of a family indexed by the empty set is called the _empty sum_. When $I=\{1,\ldots,n\}$, we also write the sum as $\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i$.


**Proposition 1.3.2.** Let $(M,+,0_M)$ be a commutative monoid, and let $\{x_i\}_{i\in I}$ be a family in $M$ indexed by a finite set $I$. Then the recursive rules in Definition 1.3.1 determine a unique element of $M$. In particular, the resulting sum is independent of the choices of indices made during the recursion.

**Proposition 1.3.3.** Let $(M,+,0_M)$ be a commutative monoid, and let $I$ be a finite set. If $I=A\mathbin{\dot{\cup}} B$, then for every indexed family $\{x_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $M$,

   $$
   \sum_{i\in I}x_i
   =
   \sum_{i\in A}x_i
   +
   \sum_{i\in B}x_i.
   $$

*Proof.* We proceed by induction on $n=|B|$. If $n=0$, then $B=\varnothing$ and $I=A$. By Definition 1.3.1, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i\in I}x_i
&=\sum_{i\in A}x_i\\
&=\sum_{i\in A}x_i+0_M\\
&=\sum_{i\in A}x_i+\sum_{i\in B}x_i.
\end{aligned}
$$


Now suppose that the statement holds for $n=k$. Let $I=A\mathbin{\dot{\cup}} B$, and $n=k+1$. Choose $b\in B$, and define $B'=B\setminus\{b\}$. Since $I\setminus\{b\}=A\mathbin{\dot{\cup}} B'$ and $|B'|=k$, the induction hypothesis gives

$$
\sum_{i\in I\setminus\{b\}}x_i
=
\sum_{i\in A}x_i+\sum_{i\in B'}x_i.
$$

Therefore, by Definition 1.3.1, the induction hypothesis, and the associativity and commutativity of addition in $M$,

$$
\begin{align}
\sum_{i\in I}x_i
&=x_b+\sum_{i\in I\setminus\{b\}}x_i\\
&=x_b+\lowparen{\sum_{i\in A}x_i+\sum_{i\in B'}x_i}\\
&=\sum_{i\in A}x_i+\lowparen{x_b+\sum_{i\in B'}x_i}\\
&=\sum_{i\in A}x_i+\sum_{i\in B}x_i.\tag*{\(\square\)}
\end{align}
$$

**Theorem 1.3.4 (Grouping of finite sums).** Let $(M,+,0_M)$ be a commutative monoid, let $I$ and $J$ be finite sets, and let $f:I\to J$ be a function. Then, for every family $\{x_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $M$,

$$\sum_{i\in I}x_i = \sum_{j\in J} \lowparen{\sum_{i\in f^{-1}(j)}x_i} . $$

*Proof.* We proceed by induction on $n=|J|$. If $n=0$, then $I=J=\varnothing$, so both sides equal $0_M$. Now suppose that the statement holds for $n=k$. Let $|J|=k+1$ and choose $j'\in J$. Define $J'=J\setminus\{j'\}$. Then, by Proposition 1.3.3, 

$$\sum_{i\in I}x_i = \sum_{i\in f^{-1}(j')}x_i + \sum_{i\in I\setminus f^{-1}(j')}x_i.$$

Since $|J'|=k$, by the induction hypothesis and Definition 1.3.1, 

$$ \begin{align}
\sum_{i\in I}x_i 
&= \sum_{i\in f^{-1}(j')}x_i + \sum_{j\in J'}\lowparen{\sum_{i\in f^{-1}(j)} x_i}\\
&= \sum_{j\in J} \lowparen{\sum_{i\in f^{-1}(j)}x_i}.\tag*{\(\square\)}
\end{align}
$$


**Corollary 1.3.5.** Let $(M,+,0_M)$ be a commutative monoid, let $I$ and $J$ be finite sets, and let $\varphi:J\to I$ be a bijection. Then, for every family $\{x_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $M$,

$$
\sum_{i\in I}x_i
=
\sum_{j\in J}x_{\varphi(j)}.
$$

*Proof.* By Theorem 3.1.4, we have

$$ \sum_{i\in I}x_i = \sum_{j\in J}\lowparen{\sum_{i\in \varphi(j)}x_i}.$$

Since $\varphi$ is bijective, the fiber of $\varphi$ over $j$ is the singleton $\{\varphi(j)\}$. It follows from Definition 1.3.1 that 

$$ 
\begin{align}
\sum_{i\in \varphi(j)}x_i 
&= x_{\varphi(j)} + 0_M\\
&= x_{\varphi(j)}.\tag*{\(\square\)}
\end{align}
$$

**Corollary 1.3.6.** Let $(M,+,0_M)$ be a commutative monoid, and let $n$ be a nonnegative integer. Define $K=\{(i,j):0\le i\le j\le n\}$. Then, for every family $\{x_{ij}\}_{(i,j)\in K}$ in $M$,

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=i}^{n}x_{ij}
=
\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{j}x_{ij}.
$$


**Proposition 1.3.7.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $I$ be a finite set. Then the following hold.

1. For any families $\{u_i\}_{i\in I}$ and $\{v_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $V$,

   $$
   \sum_{i\in I}(u_i+v_i)
   =
   \sum_{i\in I}u_i
   +
   \sum_{i\in I}v_i.
   $$

2. For any family $\{v_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $V$ and any $a\in F$,

   $$
   \sum_{i\in I}av_i
   =
   a\sum_{i\in I}v_i.
   $$


**Definitions of linear combinations and spans.** Now we move on to our main topic, linear combinations and spans. 

**Definition 1.3.8.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$. Given a finite set $I$ and indexed families $\{u_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $V$ and $\{a_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $F$, the sum

$$ \sum_{i\in I}a_iu_i $$

is called a *linear combination* of $\{u_i\}_{i\in I}$. The scalars $a_i$ are called the *coefficients* of the linear combination.
For a subset $S\subseteq V$, a vector $v\in V$ is called a linear combination of vectors in $S$ if there exist a finite set $I$ and indexed families $\{u_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $S$ and $\{a_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $F$ such that

$$ v=\sum_{i\in I}a_iu_i. $$

**Definition 1.3.9.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $S\subseteq V$. The span of $S$ is the set

$$
\operatorname{span}(S)
=
\left\lbrace
v\in V : v\text{ is a linear combination of vectors in } S
\right\rbrace.
$$

If $W$ is a subspace of $V$ and $\operatorname{span} (S) = W$, then the set $S$ is called a *generating set* for $W$.


**Example 1.3.10.** For a single vector $v\in V$,

$$\operatorname{span}\{v\}=\{av:a\in F\}.$$

If $v=0_V$, this is the zero subspace. In Euclidean space $\mathbb R^n$, the span of a nonzero vector is the line through the origin in the direction of that vector.

**Example 1.3.11.** The matrices

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1& 0\end{pmatrix},
\quad \text{and} \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix},
$$

generate $\operatorname{Mat}_{2\times 2}(\mathbb R)$ since for any $2\times 2$ matrix the following holds:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12}\\ a_{21}& a_{22}\end{pmatrix} 
= a_{11} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix}
+ a_{12}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0& 0\end{pmatrix} 
+ a_{21}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 1& 0\end{pmatrix}
+ a_{22}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0& 1\end{pmatrix}.
$$


On the other hand, consider the matrices

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\quad \text{and} \quad
C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Their linear combinations have the form

$$
\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C
=\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha+\beta+\gamma&\beta\\
\gamma&\alpha+\beta+\gamma
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The two diagonal entries are always equal, so these matrices do not generate $\operatorname{Mat}_{2\times 2}(\mathbb R)$. It follows that

$$
\operatorname{span}\{A,B,C\}
=\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}t&u\\v&t\end{pmatrix}
:t,u,v\in\mathbb R
\right\}.
$$


Thus the same subspace can be described by a condition on its elements or by a set of vectors that generates it. The condition identifies which vectors belong to the subspace; the generating set identifies the vectors from which the elements of the subspace can be built.

**Example 1.3.12.** Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer. The polynomial subspace $F_n[x]$ from Example 1.2.4 has $\{1,x,\ldots,x^n\}$ as a generating set:

$$F_n[x]=\operatorname{span}\{1,x,\ldots,x^n\}.$$
