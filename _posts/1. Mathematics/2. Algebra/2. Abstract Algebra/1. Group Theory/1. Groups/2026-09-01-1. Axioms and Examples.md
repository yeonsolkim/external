**Definition 1.1.1.** 
1. A *binary operation* $\whitestar$ on a set $A$ is a function $\whitestar:A\times A\to A$. We shall write $x\whitestar y$ for $\whitestar(x,y)$.
2. A binary operation $\whitestar$ on $A$ is said to be *associative* if 
   
   $$x\whitestar (y\whitestar z) = (x\whitestar y)\whitestar z$$ 
   
   for all $x,y,z\in A$.
1. Let $\whitestar$ be a binary operation on $A$. The elements $x$ and $y$ of $A$ *commute* if 
   
   $$x\whitestar y = y\whitestar x.$$ 
   
   The binary operation $\whitestar$ is said to be *commutative* if $x$ and $y$ commute for all $x,y\in A$.

**Definition 1.1.2.** Suppose that $\whitestar$ is a binary operation on a set $A$, and $B$ is a subset of $A$. If the restriction of $\whitestar$ to $B$ is a binary operation on $B$, that is, $x\whitestar y\in B$ for all $x,y\in B$, then $B$ is said to be *closed* under $\whitestar$.

Note that if $\whitestar$ is associative (respectively, commutative) on $A$, and if $B$ is closed under $\whitestar$, then $\whitestar$ is also associative (respectively, commutative) on $B$. 


**Definition 1.1.3.** A *group* is an ordered pair $(G,\whitestar)$ where $G$ is a set and $\whitestar$ is a binary operation on $G$ satisfying the following axioms: 

1. $\whitestar$ is associative;
2. there exists $e\in G$, called an *identity* of $G$, such that 
   
   $$ x\whitestar e = e \whitestar x = x$$
   
   for all $x\in G$;
1. for each $x\in G$ there exists $x^{-1}\in G$, called an *inverse* of $x$, such that 
   
   $$ x\whitestar x^{-1} = x^{-1} \whitestar x = e.$$
{:marker-prefix="G" }

Less formally, we shall often say that $G$ is a group under $\whitestar$. We also say the group $G$ is *commutative* if $\whitestar$ is commutative, and say $G$ is *finite* if the set $G$ is finite.


A group $G$ under $\whitestar$ may be viewed as a closed system of composable, reversible operations. Its binary operation $\whitestar$ specifies how two elements are composed, while associativity ensures that successive compositions are unambiguous. The identity axiom guarantees the existence of an element that is neutral under composition on either side: composing it with any element leaves that element unchanged. The inverse axiom, in turn, guarantees that every element has a reverse element whose composition with it yields the identity.

**Example 1.1.4.** 
1. $\mathbb Z, \mathbb Q, \mathbb R$, and $\mathbb C$ are groups under $+$ with $e=0$ and $x^{-1} = -x$.
2. $\mathbb Q\setminus \{0\}, \mathbb Q^+, \mathbb R\setminus \{0\}, \mathbb R^+$, and $\mathbb C\setminus \{0\}$ are groups under $\times$ with $e=1$ and $x^{-1} = 1/x$.

**Definition 1.1.5.** If $(G,\whitestar)$ and $(H,\diamond)$ are groups, we may form a new group $G\times H$, called their *direct product*, whose elements are those in the Cartesian product $G\times H$

$$ G \times H = \{(x,y) : x\in G, y\in H\}$$

and whose operation $\circ$ is defined componentwise: 

$$ (x_1,y_1) \circ (x_2,y_2) = (x_1\whitestar x_2, y_1\diamond y_2).$$



We prove the uniqueness of the identity and the inverse of an element.

**Proposition 1.1.6.** If $G$ is a group under $\whitestar$, then 
1. the identity of $G$ is unique;
2. for each $x\in G$, the inverse of $x$ is unique;
3. $(x^{-1})^{-1} = x$ for all $x\in G$;
4. $(x\whitestar y)^{-1} = y^{-1}\whitestar x^{-1}$ for all $x,y\in G$;
5. for any $x_1,\ldots,x_n\in G$, the value of $x_1\whitestar \cdots \whitestar x_n$ is independent of how the expression is bracketed.

*Proof.* (1) Let $e,e'\in G$ be identities. Since $e$ is an identity, $e' = e\whitestar e'$. Since $e'$ is an identity, we also have $e = e\whitestar e'$. Therefore $e=e'$.
(2) Let $y,z\in G$ be inverses of $x$. Since $e = x\whitestar z$, we have 

$$y = y\whitestar e = y\whitestar (x\whitestar z).$$

Associativity of $\whitestar$ gives 

$$ y\whitestar (x\whitestar z) = (y\whitestar x) \whitestar z.$$

Since $y\whitestar x= e$, 

$$ (y\whitestar x) \whitestar z = e\whitestar z = z.$$

Therefore $y=z$.
(3) Let $y=x^{-1}$. Then $x\whitestar y = e$. Thus $x=y^{-1}$.
(4) Let $z = (x\whitestar y)^{-1}$. Then $(x\whitestar y)\whitestar z = e$. Hence $x^{-1} = y\whitestar z$. Therefore

$$y^{-1}\whitestar x^{-1} = y^{-1}\whitestar (y\whitestar z) = (y^{-1}\whitestar y)\whitestar z = e\whitestar z = z.$$


(5) <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Henceforth, except when necessary, the product $x\whitestar y$ will be written as $xy$. Also, for any $n\in \mathbb Z^+$, we write $x^{n}$ and $x^{-n}$ in place of 

$$ \underbrace{x x \cdots  x}_{n\text{ times}}\quad \text{and} \quad \underbrace{x^{-1} x^{-1} \cdots  x^{-1}}_{n\text{ times}}.$$

Of course, when we are dealing with specific groups, we shall use the natural notations.

**Proposition 1.1.7.** Let $G$ be a group and let $a,b\in G$. The equation $ax=b$ and $ya=b$ have unique solutions for $x,y\in G$. In particular, the left and right cancellation law hold in $G$, that is, if $au=av$ then $u=v$, and if $ub =vb$ then $u=v$.

*Proof.* If $ax = b$ then $x = a^{-1}b$. Since $a^{-1}$ is unique, the solution $x$ is unique. Similarly, if $ya=b$ then $y=ba^{-1}$.
If $au=av$ then $u = a^{-1}av = ev = v$. Similarly, the right cancelation law holds.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

The successive powers $e,x,x^2,\ldots$ record what happens when an element $x$ is repeatedly composed with itself. The first time this sequence returns to the identity leads to the notion of the order of an element.

**Definition 1.1.8.** An element $x$ of a group $G$ has *finite order* if 

$$ x^n = e$$

for some positive integer $n$. In this case the *order* $|x|$ is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $x^n = e$. If there is no such $n$, the order of $x$ is defined to be infinity, and we write $|x| = \infty$.

**Definition 1.1.9.** Let $G = \{ x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\}$ be a finite group with $x_1 = e$. The *group table* of $G$ is the $n\times n$ matrix whose $i,j$ entry is the element $x_ix_j$.
 


## Exercises
**Exercise 1.1.1** Let $G = \{x\in \mathbb R : 0\le x<1\}$ and let $x\whitestar y = x + y - \lfloor x+y\rfloor$ for $x,y\in G$. Prove that $\whitestar$ is a binary operation on $G$ and that $G$ is an commutative group under $\whitestar$. ($G$ is called the *real numbers modulo* $1$.)

*Proof.* Since $a-1< \lfloor a \rfloor \le a$ for all $a\in \mathbb R$, we have 

$$ 0\le x\whitestar y = x + y - \lfloor x+y\rfloor < 1.$$

So $\whitestar$ is a binary operation on $G$. $\whitestar$ may be understood as a procedural operation adding two elements and then removing the natural part of the sum. 
Let $x,y,z\in G$ and let $\lfloor x+y\rfloor=n$. Then,

$$ \begin{align}
(x\whitestar y)\whitestar z &= (x+y-n)\whitestar z\\
&= ((x+y-n)+z) - \lfloor (x+y-n)+z\rfloor \\
&= (x+y+z) - \lfloor x+y+z\rfloor.
\end{align}
$$

Similarly, 

$$ x\whitestar (y\whitestar z) = (x+y+z) - \lfloor x+y+z\rfloor.$$

Therefore $\whitestar$ is associative.
Since 

$$ \begin{align}
x\whitestar 0 &= (x+0) -\lfloor x+0\rfloor\\
&= x -\lfloor x\rfloor \\
&= x
\end{align}
$$ 

and $0\whitestar x = x$ as well, $0$ is the identity.
The inverse of $x$ is $-x$, because 

$$ \begin{align}
x\whitestar (-x) &= (x+(-x)) - \lfloor x+(-x)\rfloor \\
&= 0 -0 \\
&=0.
\end{align}$$

Therefore $G$ is a group under $\whitestar$.
Furthermore, we have 

$$ \begin{align}
x\whitestar y &= (x+y) -\lfloor x+y\rfloor\\
&= (y+x) -\lfloor y+x\rfloor\\
&= y\whitestar x.
\end{align}
$$

Consequently, the group $G$ is commutative.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Exercise 1.1.2.** Let $G = \{z\in \mathbb C : z^n = 1\text{ for some } n\in \mathbb Z^+\}$.
1. Prove that $G$ is a group under multiplication. ($G$ is called the group of *roots of unity* in $\mathbb C$.)
2. Prove that $G$ is not a group under addition.

*Proof.* (1) Let $w,x,y,z\in G$. If $z^n=1$ and $w^m=1$, then $(zw)^{mn} = (z^n)^m(w^m)^n = 1$. 
Let $x = (x_1,x_2), y=(y_1,y_2)$, and $z=(z_1,z_2)$. Since we have

$$ \begin{align}
(xy)z &= (x_1y_1-x_2y_2,x_1y_2+x_2y_1)(z_1,z_2)\\
&= ((x_1y_1-x_2y_2)z_1-(x_1y_2 + x_2y_1)z_2, (x_1y_1-x_2y_2)z_2+ (x_1y_2 + x_2y_1)z_1)\\
&= (x_1y_1z_1-x_1y_2z_2-x_2y_1z_2-x_2y_2z_1, x_1y_1z_2 + x_1y_2z_1 +x_2y_1z_1 -x_2y_2z_2)\\
&= (x_1(y_1z_1 -y_2z_2) -x_2(y_1z_2 + y_2z_1),x_1(y_1z_2 + y_2z_1) + x_2(y_1z_1 -y_2z_2))\\
&= x(yz),
\end{align}
$$

multiplication is associative.
Also, $z1 = (z_1,z_2)(1,0) = (z_1,z_2) =z_1$ and $1z = (1,0)(z_1,z_2) = (z_1,z_2) = z$. Hence $1$ is the identity.
For any $z\in G$, we may let $z^n = 1$. Then $zz^{n-1} = 1$. Hence $z^{n-1}$ is the inverse of $z$.
Therefore, $G$ is a group under multiplication.
(2) Since $1^2=1$, $1$ is an element of $G$. However, $1+1=2\notin G$, because there is no $n\in \mathbb Z^+$ such that $2^n=1$. Thus, $G$ is not closed under addition.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Exercise 1.1.3.** Let $x$ be an element of a group $G$ having finite order. Prove that if $x^n = e$ for some positive integer $n$, then $|x|$ is a divisor of $n$.

*Proof.* Let $|x| = m$ and let $n = mk + r$ where $k,r\in \mathbb Z$ with $k\ge 0$ and $1\le r \le m$. Since $x^m = e$, we have $x^r = e$. By Definition 1.1.8, $r\ge m$. Hence $r = m$ and $n = m(k+1)$. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Exercise 1.1.4.** Let $G$ be a group and $x\in G$. Prove that $x$ and $x^{-1}$ have the same order. 

*Proof.* Let $|x|=n$ and $|x^{-1}|=m$. Since $x^{-n} = x^{-n}e = x^{-n}x^n=e$, $m$ is a divisor of $n$. Similarly, $n$ is a divisor of $m$. Thus $m=n$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Exercise 1.1.5.** Let $G$ be a group and let $x\in G$ be of order $n$. Prove that if $n$ is odd, then $x = x^{2k}$ for some $k\in \mathbb Z^+$.

*Proof.* Let $n=2k-1$ for some $k\in \mathbb Z^+$. Since $x^n = e$, we have $x^{n+1} = x$. Thus $x^{2k} = x$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Exercise 1.1.6.** Let $G$ be a group and $x,y\in G$. Prove that $|x| = |y^{-1}xy|$, and deduce $|ab|=|ba|$ for all $a,b\in G$.

*Proof.* Let $z=y^{-1}xy$. If $z^n=y^{-1}x^ny$ for some $n\in \mathbb Z^+$, then $z^{n+1} = (y^{-1}x^ny)(y^{-1}xy) = y^{-1}x^{n+1}y$. By mathematical induction, we have $z^n=y^{-1}x^ny$ for all $n\in \mathbb Z^+$. Hence, $|x|$ divides $|z|$. Similarly, since $x=yzy^{-1}$, we also have $x^k=yz^ky^{-1}$ for all $k\in \mathbb Z^+$. So $|z|$ divides $|x|$. Therefore $|x| = |z|$. 
Taking $xy=a$ and $y^{-1}=b$, we obtain $|ab|=|ba|$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>
