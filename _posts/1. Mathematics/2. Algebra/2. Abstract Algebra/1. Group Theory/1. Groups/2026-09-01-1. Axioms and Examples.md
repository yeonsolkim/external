**Definition 1.1.1.** 
1. A *binary operation* $\ast$ on a set $A$ is a function $\ast:A\times A\to A$. We shall write $x\ast y$ for $\ast(x,y)$.
2. A binary operation $\ast$ on $A$ is said to be *associative* if 
   
   $$x\ast (y\ast z) = (x\ast y)\ast z$$ 
   
   for all $x,y,z\in A$.
1. Let $\ast$ be a binary operation on $A$. The elements $x$ and $y$ of $A$ *commute* if 
   
   $$x\ast y = y\ast x.$$ 
   
   The binary operation $\ast$ is said to be *commutative* if $x$ and $y$ commute for all $x,y\in A$.

**Definition 1.1.2.** Suppose that $\ast$ is a binary operation on a set $A$, and $B$ is a subset of $A$. If the restriction of $\ast$ to $B$ is a binary operation on $B$, that is, $x\ast y\in B$ for all $x,y\in B$, then $B$ is said to be *closed* under $\ast$.

Note that if $\ast$ is associative (respectively, commutative) on $A$, and if $B$ is closed under $\ast$, then $\ast$ is also associative (respectively, commutative) on $B$. 


**Definition 1.1.3.** A *group* is an ordered pair $(G,\ast)$ where $G$ is a set and $\ast$ is a binary operation on $G$ satisfying the following axioms: 

1. $\ast$ is associative;
2. there exists $e\in G$, called an *identity* of $G$, such that 
   
   $$ x\ast e = e \ast x = x$$
   
   for all $x\in G$;
1. for each $x\in G$ there exists $x^{-1}\in G$, called an *inverse* of $x$, such that 
   
   $$ x\ast x^{-1} = x^{-1} \ast x = e.$$
{:marker-prefix="G" }

Less formally, we shall often say that $G$ is a group under $\ast$. We also say the group $G$ is *abelian* if $\ast$ is commutative, and say $G$ is *finite* if the set $G$ is finite.



A group may be viewed as a closed system of composable, reversible operations. The binary operation specifies how two operations are composed, and associativity of the operation ensures that successive compositions are unambiguous. The identity axiom guarantees the existence of an element doing nothing, while the inverse axiom provides an element for canceling each operation. Consequently, left and right multiplication by any element of the group are bijections; equivalently, equations of the form $a\ast x=b$ and $x\ast a=b$ have unique solutions in $G$.

**Example 1.1.4.** 
1. $\mathbb Z, \mathbb Q, \mathbb R$, and $\mathbb C$ are groups under $+$ with $e=0$ and $x^{-1} = -x$.
2. $\mathbb Q\setminus \{0\}, \mathbb Q^+, \mathbb R\setminus \{0\}, \mathbb R^+$, and $\mathbb C\setminus \{0\}$ are groups under $\times$ with $e=1$ and $x^{-1} = 1/x$.

**Definition 1.1.5.** If $(G,\ast)$ and $(H,\diamond)$ are groups, we may form a new group $G\times H$, called their *direct product*, whose elements are those in the Cartesian product $G\times H$

$$ G \times H = \{(x,y) : x\in G, y\in H\}$$

and whose operation $\star$ is defined componentwise: 

$$ (x_1,y_1)\star (x_2,y_2) = (x_1\ast x_2, y_1\diamond y_2).$$



We prove the uniqueness of the identity and the inverse of an element.

**Proposition 1.1.6.** If $G$ is a group under $\ast$, then 
1. the identity of $G$ is unique;
2. for each $x\in G$, the inverse of $x$ is unique;
3. $(x^{-1})^{-1} = x$ for all $x\in G$;
4. $(x\ast y)^{-1} = (x^{-1})\ast (y^{-1})$ for all $x,y\in G$;
5. for any $x_1,\ldots,x_n\in G$, the value of the expression $x_1\ast\cdots\ast x_n$ is independent of how it is bracketed.

*Proof.* (1) Suppose $e_1$ and $e_2$ are identities. Since $e_1$ is an identity, $e_1 \ast e_2 = e_2$. Similarly, since $e_2$ is an identity, $e_1\ast e_2 = e_1$. Since $e_1\ast e_2$ is a unique element in $G$, $e_1 = e_2$.
(2) Let $e$ be the identity.
