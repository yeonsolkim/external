**Definition 1.4.1.** A relation $\le$ on a set $S$ is called a *partial order* on $S$ if the following hold:
1. $\forall x\in S\ (x\le x)$ (*reflexivity*);
2. $\forall x,y\in S\ ((x\le y\land y\le x)\rightarrow x=y)$ (*antisymmetry*);
3. $\forall x,y,z\in S\ ((x\le y \land y\le z) \rightarrow x\le z)$ (*transitivity*).

A set equipped with a partial order is called a *partially ordered set* or *poset*.


**Definition 1.4.2.** A partial order $\le$ on a set $S$ is said to be a *total order* on $S$ if it has *comparability*:

 $$\forall x,y\in S\ (x\le y\lor y\le x).$$

A set equipped with a total order is called a *totally ordered set* or *toset*.

**Definition 1.4.3.** Let $P$ be a poset. An element $a$ of $P$ is a *least element* of $P$ if

$$ \forall x\in P\ (a\le x).$$

An element $a\in P$ is a *minimal element* of $P$ if

$$ \forall x\in P\ (x\le a \rightarrow x=a).$$



So, "least" means smaller than everything else, while "minimal" means nothing else is smaller.

**Proposition 1.4.4.** Let $P$ be a poset.
1. If there exists a least element of $P,$ then it is minimal and it is unique.
2. If $\le$ is a total order, then any minimal element is the least element.

*Proof.* (1) Let $a$ be a least element if $P.$ If $x\le a,$ then $x=a,$ since $\le$ is antisymmetric. Hence $a$ is minimal.
Suppose that $b\in P$ is a least element. Then we have $a\le b$ and $b\le a,$ so $a=b.$ Hence such $a$ is unique.
(2) Let $a$ be a minimal element. If $x\le a$ then $x=a$, so that $a\le x$. Hence we have $a\le x$ for all $x\in P$ by totality.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Definition 1.4.5.** An element $a$ of a poset $P$ is a *greatest element* of $P$ if

$$ \forall x\in P\ (x\le a),$$

and $a$ is a *maximal element* of $P$ if

$$ \forall x\in P\ (a\le x \rightarrow x=a).$$


**Definition 1.4.6.** Let $P$ and $Q$ be two posets with order relations $\le_P$ and $\le_Q$ respectively. The posets $P$ and $Q$ have the same *order type* if there exists a bijective function $f\colon P\to Q$ such that

$$ x\le_P y \iff f(x)\le_Q f(y).$$

**Definition 1.4.7.** Let $P$ be a poset, and $S\subseteq P.$ If there is an element $a\in P$ such that $x\leq a$ for all $x\in S,$ $S$ is *bounded above* and $a$ is an *upper bound* for $S.$ If $a\in S,$ then $a$ is the greatest element of $S.$
Similarly, if there is an element $a\in P$ such that $a\leq x$ for all $x\in S,$ $S$ is *bounded below* and $a$ is a *lower bound* for $S.$ If $a\in S,$ then $a$ is the least element of $S.$

**Definition 1.4.8.** Let $P$ be a poset and $S\subseteq P.$ Suppose that $S$ is bounded above and $a$ is an upper bound for $S.$ If $a\le b$ for every upper bound $b$ for $S,$ then $a$  is the *least upper bound* or the *supremum* for $S.$ In this case we write

$$ a = \sup {S}.$$

We now suppose that $S$ is bounded below and $a$ is a lower bound for $S.$ If $b\le a$ for every lower bound $b$ for $S,$ then $a$ is the *greatest lower bound* or the *infimum* for $S.$ In this case we write

$$ a = \inf {S}.$$

**Definition 1.4.9.** A poset $P$ has the *least-upper-bound property* if, for every nonempty subset $S$ that is bounded above, there exists $a\in P$ such that $a = \sup {S}.$
Similarly, $P$ has the *greatest-lower-bound property* if, for every nonempty subset $S$ that is bounded below, there exists $a\in P$ such that $a = \inf {S}.$

**Theorem 1.4.10.** Suppose that $P$ is a poset with the least-upper-bound property, $S$ is a nonempty subset of $P,$ and $S$ is bounded below. Let $T$ be the set of all lower bounds for $S.$ Then

$$ \exists a\in P \ (a = \sup T \ \land\ a = \inf S).$$

*Proof.* Since $S$ is bounded below, $T$ is not empty. Since every $x\in S$ is an upper bound for $T,$ $T$ is bounded above. Given that $P$ has the least-upper-bound property, there exists $a\in P$ such that $a=\sup T.$
Since every $x\in S$ is an upper bound for $T$ and $a$ is the least upper bound for $T,$ $a$ is a lower bound for $S.$
Let $y$ be any lower bound of $S.$ By the definition of $T,$ $y\in T.$ Since $a=\sup T,$ $y\le a.$ Consequently, $a$ is the greatest lower bound for $S.$ <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

We define another important relation defined on a poset.

**Definition 1.4.11.** Let $P$ be a partially ordered set. For $x,y\in P$, we say that $y$ *covers* $x$, written $x\lessdot y$, if $x<y$ and there is no $z\in P$ such that $x<z<y$.


**Definition 1.4.12.** Let $P$ be a partially ordered set. A *chain* $C$ in $P$ is a totally ordered subset of $P$. If the chain $C$ is finite and nonempty, then $C$ is called a *finite chain* and its *length* $\ell(C)$ is defined by

$$ \ell(C) = |C|-1,$$

which corresponds to the number of pairs in covering relation in $Q$.

**Definition 1.4.13.** Let $P$ be a poset. For $x,y\in P$ with $x\leq y$, the *interval* from $x$ to $y$ is the set

$$ [x,y]=\{z\in P:x\leq z\leq y\}. $$

An interval is regarded as a poset under the order inherited from $P$.
The *length* of the interval $[x,y]$, denoted by $\ell([x,y])$, is the supremum of the lengths of all finite chains in $[x,y]$.

**Definition 1.4.14.** A partially ordered set $P$ is said to have *finite length* if there exists a nonnegative integer $n$ such that every chain in $P$ is finite and has length at most $n$. The least such $n$ is called the *length* of $P$, denoted by $\ell(P)$.

**Example 1.4.15.** Consider the power set $\mathcal P$ of $\{x,y,z\}$, partially ordered by set inclusion. We can graphically express the covering structure of $\mathcal P$ as follows:

$$
\begin{tikzpicture}[hasse]
  \node (xyz) at (0,3) {$\{x,y,z\}$};
  \node (xy) at (-2,2) {$\{x,y\}$};
  \node (xz) at (0,2) {$\{x,z\}$};
  \node (yz) at (2,2) {$\{y,z\}$};
  \node (x) at (-2,1) {$\{x\}$};
  \node (y) at (0,1) {$\{y\}$};
  \node (z) at (2,1) {$\{z\}$};
  \node (e) at (0,0) {$\varnothing$};
  \draw (xyz) -- (xy) (xyz) -- (xz) (xyz) -- (yz)
        (xy) -- (x) (xy) -- (y) (xz) -- (x) (xz) -- (z) (yz) -- (y) (yz) -- (z)
        (x) -- (e) (y) -- (e) (z) -- (e);
\end{tikzpicture}
$$

This type of diagram is called a *Hasse diagram*.
By means of the Hasse diagram, we can easily identify the interval $I = [\varnothing, \{x,z\}]$: there are six finite chains and the length of $I$ is $2$. Furthermore, it is straightforward to see that the length of $\mathcal P$ is $3$.
