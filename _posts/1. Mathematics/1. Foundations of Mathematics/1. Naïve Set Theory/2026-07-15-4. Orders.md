**Definition 1.4.1.** A relation $\le$ on a set $P$ is called a *partial order* on $P$ if the following hold:
1. $\forall x\in P\ (x\le x)$ (*reflexivity*);
2. $\forall x,y\in P\ ((x\le y\land y\le x)\rightarrow x=y)$ (*antisymmetry*);
3. $\forall x,y,z\in P\ ((x\le y \land y\le z) \rightarrow x\le z)$ (*transitivity*).

The set $P$ equipped with the partial order is called a *partially ordered set* (or *poset*).

It is worth observing that although a partial order distinguishes two directions, neither direction is intrinsically privileged, nor need either carry any particular interpretation. 
An element $x$ of a poset $P$ is related to itself by the order relation. In other words, $x$ both precedes and follows itself. Two elements of $P$ can precede and follow each other in this way only when they are the same element. This is what the first two axioms express. The third axiom says that whenever two order relations are linked by a common intermediate element, the two elements at the ends are also related by the order relation. 

**Definition 1.4.2.** Let $P$ be a poset and let $a,b\in P$.
1. If $a\le x$ for all $x\in P$, then $a$ is called a *least element*.
2. If $x\le a$ for all $x\in P$, then $a$ is called a *greatest element*.
3. If $x\le a$ implies $x=a$ for all $x\in P$, then $a$ is called a *minimal element*.
4. If $a\le x$ implies $x=a$ for all $x\in P$, then $a$ is called a *maximal element*.



Consider, for example, the concept of a least element. We may ask which aspects of this concept are essential and which depend on the direction of the order. The essential feature is that the element lies at one extreme of the entire poset; the direction-dependent feature is which extreme it occupies. By preserving the former and reversing the latter, we arrive at the concept of a greatest element. In this way, concepts in order theory naturally occur in pairs that differ only in the direction of the order. Each such concept is called the *dual* of the other.

**Definition 1.4.4.** Let $P$ be a poset, and $S\subseteq P$. If there is an element $a\in P$ such that $a\leq x$ for all $x\in S,$ $S$ is *bounded below* and $a$ is a *lower bound* for $S.$ If $a\in S,$ then $a$ is the least element of $S.$
Dually, if there is an element $b\in P$ such that $x\leq b$ for all $x\in S,$ $S$ is *bounded above* and $b$ is an *upper bound* for $S.$ If $b\in S,$ then $b$ is the greatest element of $S$.

**Definition 1.4.5.** Let $P$ be a poset and $S\subseteq P$. Suppose that $S$ is bounded below and $\alpha$ is a lower bound for $S$. If $a\le \alpha$ for every lower bound $a$ for $S$, then $\alpha$ is called the *greatest lower bound* (or *infimum*) for $S$. In this case we write

$$ \alpha = \inf {S}.$$


Dually, suppose that $S$ is bounded above and $\beta$ is an upper bound for $S$. If $\beta\le b$ for every upper bound $b$ for $S,$ then $\beta$  is called the *least upper bound* (or *supremum*) for $S.$ In this case we write

$$ \beta = \sup {S}.$$

**Definition 1.4.6.** A poset $P$ is said to have the *greatest-lower-bound property* if, for every nonempty subset $S$ that is bounded below, there exists $\alpha\in P$ such that $\alpha = \inf {S}.$
Dually, a poset $P$ is said to have the *least-upper-bound property* if, for every nonempty subset $S$ that is bounded above, there exists $\beta\in P$ such that $\beta = \sup {S}.$

**Proposition 1.4.7.** Suppose that $P$ is a poset with the least-upper-bound property, $S$ is a nonempty subset of $P,$ and $S$ is bounded below. Let $T$ be the set of all lower bounds for $S.$ Then there exists $\alpha\in P$ such that

$$\alpha = \inf S\quad \text{and} \quad \alpha = \sup T.$$

*Proof.* Since $S$ is bounded below, $T$ is not empty. Since every $x\in S$ is an upper bound for $T,$ $T$ is bounded above. Given that $P$ has the least-upper-bound property, there exists $\alpha\in P$ such that $\alpha=\sup T.$
Since every $x\in S$ is an upper bound for $T$ and $\alpha$ is the least upper bound for $T,$ $\alpha$ is a lower bound for $S.$
Let $y$ be any lower bound of $S.$ By the definition of $T,$ $y\in T.$ Since $\alpha=\sup T,$ $y\le \alpha.$ Consequently, $\alpha$ is the greatest lower bound for $S.$ <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**The duality principle.** We may now ask a question: can we reverse not only the concepts themselves but also the theorems concerning them? Consider, for instance, the following two statements: “A poset has at most one least element” and “A poset has at most one greatest element.” Having proved the first statement, we are struck by the fact that the second appears to express essentially the same mathematical content, but with the direction reversed. 
Once we recognize that neither direction is privileged under the axioms of a partial order, we see that such a dual statement has the same truth value as the original statement; no separate proof is indeed necessary. The mathematical justification for this observation is provided by the *duality principle*.

**Proposition 1.4.8.** Let $T$ be the set of axioms defining a partial order, and let $T^{\mathrm{op}}$ be the set of their dual statements. Then $T^{\mathrm{op}}$ is logically equivalent to $T$. 

**Theorem 1.4.9 (Duality principle).** Let $\varphi$ be a statement in the language of posets. If $\varphi$ holds in every poset, then so does $\varphi^{\mathrm{op}}$.

**Maps between posets.**

**Definition 1.4.10.** Let $(P,\le_P)$ and $(Q,\le_Q)$ be two posets. 
1. A map $f\colon P\to Q$ is called *monotone* (or *order-preserving*) if, for all $x, y\in P$, 
   
   $$ x\le_P y \implies f(x)\le_Q f(y).$$

2. A map $f\colon P\to Q$ is called *antitone* (or *order-reversing*) if, for all $x, y\in P$,
   
   $$ x\le_P y \implies f(y)\le_Q f(x).$$

3. A bijection $f\colon P\to Q$ is called an *order isomorphism* if, for all $x, y\in P$,
   
   $$ x\le_P y \iff f(x)\le_Q f(y).$$
   
    If such a map exists, the two posets are called *order isomorphic*.
4. A bijection $f\colon P\to Q$ is called an *order anti-isomorphism* if, for all $x, y\in P$,
   
   $$ x\le_P y \iff f(y)\le_Q f(x).$$
   
   If such a map exists, the two posets are called *order anti-isomorphic*.

**Definition 1.4.11.** Let $(P,\leq_P)$ be a poset. The *dual poset* of $(P,\leq_P)$ is an ordered pair $(P^{\mathrm{op}},\leq_{P^{\mathrm{op}}})$, where $P^{\mathrm{op}}$ is the set $P$ and $\leq_{P^{\mathrm{op}}}$ is a relation such that, for all $x,y\in P^{\mathrm{op}}$,

$$ x\leq_{P^{\mathrm{op}}}y \iff y\leq_P x. $$


**Theorem 1.4.12.** Let $P$ and $Q$ be posets, and let $f\colon P\to Q$ be a map. Then the following are equivalent:
1. $f\colon P\to Q$ is antitone;
2. $f\colon P^{\mathrm{op}}\to Q$ is monotone;
3. $f\colon P\to Q^{\mathrm{op}}$ is monotone.


**Theorem 1.4.13.** Let $P$ and $Q$ be posets, and let $f\colon P\to Q$ be a bijection. Then the following are equivalent:
1. $f\colon P\to Q$ is an order anti-isomorphism;
2. both $f\colon P\to Q$ and $f^{-1}\colon Q\to P$ are antitone;
3. $f\colon P^{\mathrm{op}}\to Q$ is an order isomorphism;
4. $f\colon P\to Q^{\mathrm{op}}$ is an order isomorphism.
