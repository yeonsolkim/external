**Definition 1.2.1.** A subset $W$ of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$ is called a *subspace* of $V$ if $W$ is a vector space over $F$ with the operations of addition and scalar multiplication defined on $V.$<br>
<br>

**Remark 1.2.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F,$ and $W$ a subset of $V.$ Because (VS1), (VS2), (VS5), (VS6), (VS7), and (VS8) hold for all vectors in $V,$ these automatically hold for the vectors in $W.$ Thus $W$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if the following four properties are true in $W.$ 
1. $\forall u,v\in W \ (u+v\in W).$
2. $\forall c \in F\  \forall u\in W \ (cu\in W).$
3. $\exists u\in W \ \forall v\in W \ (v+u=v).$ 
4. $\forall u\in W \ (-u\in W).$

<br>

**Theorem 1.2.3.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $F,$ and $W$ a subset of $V.$ Then $W$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if the following three properties are true in $W.$
1. $0_V\in W.$
2. $\forall u,v\in W \ (u+v\in W).$
3. $\forall c \in F\  \forall u\in W \ (cu\in W).$

*Proof*. Suppose that $W$ is a subspace of $V.$ By Definition 1.2.1, (2) and (3) hold, and there exists $u\in W$ such that $\forall v\in W \ (v+u=v).$ But in $V,$  we have $v+0_V= v,$ and thus, by Theorem 1.1.8, $u=0_V.$ Therefore (1) holds.<br>
&emsp;Conversely, suppose that (1), (2), and (3) hold. Then $(-1)u\in W.$ We also have $0u=0_V,$ because $u+0u=1u+0u=(1+0)u=1u=u.$ Thus $u+(-1)u=1u+(-1)u=(1+(-1))u=0u=0_V.$ Therefore $-u=(-1)u\in W.$ From Remark 1.2.2, we can see that $W$ is a subspace of $V.$ <span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>
<br>

**Example 1.2.4.** The *transpose* $A^{{\!\top}}$ of an $m\times n$ matrix $A$ is the $n\times m$ matrix defined by $(A^{\!\top})_{ij}=A_{ji}.$ A *symmetric matrix* is a matrix $A$ such that $A^{\!\top}=A.$ It is clear that a symmetric matrix must be square. It is because that $A$ is a $m\times n$ matrix with $m<n$ implies that there are no entries in $A^{\!\top}$ corresponding to the entries $A_{in}.$<br>
&emsp;The set $W$ of all symmetric matrix in ${F}^{n\times n}$ is a subspace of ${F}^{n\times n}$ since Theorem 1.2.3 hold: 
1. Since $O^{\!\top}=O,$ $O\in W.$
2. Let $A,B\in W.$ We have $A_{ij}=A_{ji}$ and $B_{ij}=B_{ji},$ thus $(A+B)_{ij}=A_{ij}+B_{ij}=A_{ji}+B_{ji}=(A+B)_{ji}.$ Therefore $(A+B)\in W.$
3. Let $A\in W$ and $c\in F.$ We have $A_{ij}=A_{ji},$ thus $(cA)_{ij}=cA_{ij}=cA_{ji}=(cA)_{ij}.$ Therefore $cA\in W.$

<br>

**Example 1.2.5.** Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer, and let ${F}_n[x]$ consist of all polynomials in ${F}[x]$ having degree less than or equal to $n.$ 
1. Since the zero polynomial $0_V\in {F}[x]$ has degree $-\infty,$ it is in ${F}_n[x].$
2. Let $P, Q \in {F}[x].$ Since $\text{deg}(P+Q)\leq \max{\lbrace  \text{deg}(P),\text{deg}(Q)\rbrace }\leq n,\ (P+Q)\in {F}_n[x].$ 
3. Let $P \in {F}[x].$ Since $\text{deg}(cP)=\text{deg}(P)\leq n, \ cP\in {F}_n[x].$

It therefore follows from Theorem 1.2.3 that ${F}_n[x]$ is a subspace of ${F}[x].$<br>
<br>

**Example 1.2.6.** Let $C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R)$ denote the set of all continuous real-valued functions defined on $\mathbb R.$ 
1. Since constant functions are continuous functions, $0_V \in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$
2. Let $f,g\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$ Since $f+g$ is continuous function, $(f+g)\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$
3. Let $f\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R)$ and $c\in \mathbb R.$ Since $cf$ is a continuous function, $cf\in C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R).$

It therefore follows from Theorem 1.2.3 that $C(\mathbb R, \mathbb R)$ is a subspace of ${F}[x].$<br>
<br>

**Example 1.2.7.** An $n\times n$ matrix $M$ is called *diagonal matrix* if $M_{ij}=0$ whenever $i\neq j,$ that is, if all its non-diagonal entries are zero. Let $W$ denote the set of all $n\times n$ diagonal matrices.
1. Since $O_{ij}=0$ for all $i$ and $j,\ O\in W.$
2. Let $A,B\in W.$ If $i\neq j$ then $(A+B)_{ij}=A_{ij}+B_{ij}=0+0=0.$ Thus $(A+B)\in W.$
3. Let $A\in W$ and $c\in F.$ If $i\neq j$ then $(cA)_{ij}=cA_{ij}=c0=0c=0.$ Thus $cA\in W.$

It therefore follows from Theorem 1.2.3 that $W$ is a subspace of ${F}^{n\times n}.$<br>
<br>

**Theorem 1.2.8.** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$. Then any intersection of subspaces of $V$ is a subspace of $V.$<br><br>
*Proof.* Let $\mathcal C$ be a set whose elements are subspaces of $V,$ and let $W$ denote the intersection of $\mathcal C.$ Since every subspace contains the zero vector, $0_V\in W.$ Let $a\in F$ and $u,v\in W.$ Then $u$ and $v$ are elements of each subspace in $\mathcal C.$ Since each subspace in $\mathcal C$ is closed under the addition and scalar multiplication, $u+v$ and $cu$ are elements of each subspace in $\mathcal C.$ Hence $u+v$ and $cu$ are also elements of $W,$ so that $W$ is a subspace of $V$ by Theorem 1.2.3.<span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>

## Exercises

**Exercise 1.** Prove that 

$$(aA+bB)^{\!\top} = aA^{\!\top} + bB^{\!\top}$$

for any $A,B\in F^{m\times n}$ and any $a,b\in F.$<br><br>
*Proof.* Let $1\leq i \leq m$ and $1\leq j \leq n.$ Then we have

$$ 
\begin{aligned}
\left( (aA+bB)^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}
&= (aA+bB)_{ji}\\
&= (aA)_{ji} + (bB)_{ji}\\
&= aA_{ji} + bB_{ji}\\
&= a\left(  A^{\!\top}\right)_{ij} + b\left(  B^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}\\
&= \left( aA^{\!\top}\right)_{ij} + \left( bB^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}\\
&= \left( aA^{\!\top} + bB^{\!\top}\right)_{ij}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore $(aA+bB)^{\!\top} = aA^{\!\top} + bB^{\!\top}.$<span class="qed">$\square$</span><br>
