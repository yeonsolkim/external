**Definition 1.1.1.** A *topological space* is a set $X$ together with a collection of subsets of $X$, called *open sets*, such that $\varnothing$ and $X$ are open, and such that arbitrary unions and finite intersections of open sets are open. The collection of open sets is called a *topology* on $X$.

**Definition 1.1.2.** Let $(X,\mathcal T)$ be a topological space and let $x\in X$. A set $N\subseteq X$ is called a *neighborhood* of $x$ if there exists $U\in\mathcal T$ such that

$$ x\in U\subseteq N. $$

In particular, if $N$ is itself open, it is called an *open neighborhood* of $x$.


**Definition 1.1.3.** Let $(X, \mathcal{T})$ be a topological space. A *basis* for the topology $\mathcal{T}$ is a collection $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$, whose elements are called the *basis elements*, such that every $U \in \mathcal{T}$ can be represented as the union of some basis elements.

**Definition 1.1.4.** Let $(X,\mathcal T)$ be a topological space and $x\in X$. A collection $\mathcal B_x$ of open neighborhoods of $x$ is called a *local basis* at $x$ if, for every neighborhood $N$ of $x$, there exists $B\in\mathcal B_x$ such that $B\subseteq N$.

**Theorem 1.1.5.** Let $(X, \mathcal{T})$ be a topological space and let $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$. For $x \in X$, define $\mathcal{B}_x = \{B \in \mathcal{B} : x \in B\}$. Then, $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$ if and only if $\mathcal{B}_x$ is a local basis at $x$ for every $x \in X$.

If we select an arbitrary collection $\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal P(X)$ and take all possible unions of its elements, will a topology necessarily be formed? The answer is no. The two necessary conditions in the following proposition relate to this very problem.

**Proposition 1.1.6** A basis $\mathcal{B}$ for a topology on $X$ satisfies the following properties: 
1. $\mathcal{B}$ covers $X$, that is, for every $x \in X$, there exists $B \in \mathcal{B}$ that contains $x$;
2. if $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ contain $x \in X$, then there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$.
{:marker-prefix="B"}

*Proof.* Since $X \in \mathcal{T}$, by Theorem 1.1.5, $\mathcal{B}$ covers $X$. Similarly, since $B_1 \cap B_2 \in \mathcal{T}$, by Theorem 1.1.5, there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

(B1) is the condition that the basis elements must cover the entire of $X$. On the other hand, (B2) is a necessary condition regarding finite intersections. 
Indeed, together (B1) and (B2) are sufficient to determine a unique topology on $X$.

**Proposition 1.1.7.** Suppose that $\mathcal{B}$ is a collection of subsets of $X$ satisfying (B1) and (B2), and that $\mathcal{T}$ is the collection of all unions of the elements of $\mathcal{B}$. Then $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology on $X$ and $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$.

*Proof.* It is clear that $\varnothing \in \mathcal{T}$. By (B1), we also have $X \in \mathcal{T}$. Let $\{U_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a family in $\mathcal{T}$. For each $i \in I$, there exists a family $\{B_{j}\}_{j \in J_i}$ in $\mathcal{B}$ such that $\bigcup_{j \in J_i} B_{j} = U_i$. It follows that 

$$ \bigcup_{i \in I}U_i = \bigcup_{i \in I}\lowparen{\bigcup_{j \in J_i} B_{j}} \in \mathcal{T}.$$


Now let $U, V \in \mathcal{T}$. For every $x \in U \cap V$, there exist $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_1 \subseteq U$ and $x \in B_2 \subseteq V$. By (B2), there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2 \subseteq U \cap V$. Hence

$$U \cap V = \bigcup\{B \in \mathcal{B} : B \subseteq U \cap V\} \in \mathcal{T}.$$

Thus $\mathcal{T}$ is closed under finite intersections, and so it is a topology on $X$. 
Finally, each $B \in \mathcal{B}$ is itself a union of elements of $\mathcal{B}$, so $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$. Since every member of $\mathcal{T}$ is a union of elements of $\mathcal{B}$ by construction, $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$ by Definition 1.1.3.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Definition 1.1.8.** Suppose that $\mathcal{T}$ and $\mathcal{T}'$ are topologies on a given set $X$. If $\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{T}'$, then $\mathcal{T}'$ is called *finer* than $\mathcal T$, and $\mathcal T$ is called *coarser* than $\mathcal T'$. In particular, if $\mathcal{T} \subset \mathcal T'$, then $\mathcal T'$ is called *strictly finer* than $\mathcal T$, and $\mathcal T$ is called *strictly coarser* than $\mathcal T'$. We say that $\mathcal T'$ is *comparable* with $\mathcal T$ if either $\mathcal T \subseteq \mathcal T'$ or $\mathcal T' \subseteq \mathcal T$.

**Proposition 1.1.9.** Let $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{B}'$ be bases for the topologies $\mathcal{T}$ and $\mathcal{T}'$, respectively, on $X$. Then, $\mathcal{T}'$ is finer than $\mathcal{T}$ if and only if, for each $x \in X$ and each $B \in \mathcal{B}$ containing $x$, there exists $B' \in \mathcal{B}'$ such that $x \in B' \subseteq B$.

*Proof.* Suppose that $\mathcal{T}'$ is finer than $\mathcal{T}$. Then $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{T}'$, so every $B \in \mathcal{B}$ is open in $(X,\mathcal{T}')$. Hence, for each $x \in B$, there exists $B' \in \mathcal{B}'$ such that $x \in B' \subseteq B$ by Theorem 1.1.5.
Conversely, suppose $U \in \mathcal{T}$. By Theorem 1.1.5, for each $x \in U$, there exists $B \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B \subseteq U$. Since there exists $B' \in \mathcal{B}'$ such that $x \in B' \subseteq B$, we have $x \in B' \subseteq U$ for each $x \in U$. Hence $U \in \mathcal{T}'$ by Definition 1.1.3, so that $\mathcal{T}'$ is finer than $\mathcal{T}$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Definition 1.1.10.** 
1. The topology generated by the collection of all open intervals in $\mathbb{R}$ is called the *standard topology* on $\mathbb{R}$.
2. The topology generated by the collection of all half-open intervals of the form $[a, b)$, where $a < b$, is called the *lower limit topology* on $\mathbb{R}$. The real line $\mathbb{R}$ given with the lower limit topology is denoted by $\mathbb{R}_{\ell}$.
3. Let $K = \{1/n : n \in \mathbb{Z}^+\}$ and let $\mathcal{B}$ be the collection of all open intervals  together with all sets of the form $(a,b) \setminus K$. The topology generated by $\mathcal{B}$ is called the *K-topology* on $\mathbb{R}$. The real line $\mathbb{R}$ given with the K-topology is denoted by $\mathbb{R}_K$.

**Proposition 1.1.11.** The lower limit topology and the K-topology on $\mathbb{R}$ are strictly finer than the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$, but are not comparable with one another.
