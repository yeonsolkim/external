**Definition 1.1.1.** A *topological space* is a set $X$ together with a collection of subsets of $X$, called *open sets*, such that $\varnothing$ and $X$ are open, and such that arbitrary unions and finite intersections of open sets are open. The collection of open sets is called a *topology* on $X$.

**Definition 1.1.2.** Let $(X, \mathcal{T})$ be a topological space. A *basis* for the topology $\mathcal{T}$ is a collection $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$, whose elements are called the *basis elements*, such that every $U \in \mathcal{T}$ can be represented as the union of some basis elements.

**Proposition 1.1.3.** Let $(X, \mathcal{T})$ be a topological space. A collection $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$ if and only if for every $U \in \mathcal{T}$ and every $x \in U$ there exists $B \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B \subseteq U$.

*Proof.* Suppose that $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$. By Definition 1.1.2, there is $\{B_i\}_{i \in I}$ such that $B_i \in \mathcal{B}$ and $\bigcup_{i \in I} B_i = U$. Hence there exists $i \in I$ such that $x \in B_i \subseteq U$.
Conversely, suppose that, for every $U \in \mathcal{T}$ and every $x \in U$, there exists $B_x \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_x \subseteq U$. Then we have $\bigcup_{x \in U} B_x = U$, so $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Proposition 1.1.4.** A basis $\mathcal{B}$ for a topology on $X$ satisfies the following conditions: 
1. $\mathcal{B}$ covers $X$, that is, for every $x \in X$, there exists $B \in \mathcal{B}$ that contains $x$;
2. if $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ contain $x \in X$, then there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$.
{:marker-prefix="B"}

*Proof.* Since $X \in \mathcal{T}$, by Proposition 1.1.3, $\mathcal{B}$ covers $X$. Similarly, since $B_1 \cap B_2 \in \mathcal{T}$, by Proposition 1.1.3, there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


**Proposition 1.1.5.** Suppose that $\mathcal{B}$ is a collection of subsets of $X$ satisfying (B1) and (B2), and $\mathcal{T}$ is the collection of all unions of the elements of $\mathcal{B}$. Then $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology on $X$ and $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$.

*Proof.* It is clear that $\varnothing \in \mathcal{T}$. By (B1), we also have $X \in \mathcal{T}$. Let $\{U_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a family in $\mathcal{T}$. For each $i \in I$, there exists a family $\{B_{j}\}_{j \in J_i}$ in $\mathcal{B}$ such that $\bigcup_{j \in J_i} B_{j} = U_i$. It follows that 

$$ \bigcup_{i \in I}U_i = \bigcup_{i \in I}\lowparen{\bigcup_{j \in J_i} B_{j}} \in \mathcal{T}.$$

Now let $U, V \in \mathcal{T}$. For every $x \in U \cap V$, there exist $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_1 \subseteq U$ and $x \in B_2 \subseteq V$. By (B2), there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2 \subseteq U \cap V$. Hence

$$U \cap V = \bigcup\{B \in \mathcal{B} : B \subseteq U \cap V\} \in \mathcal{T}.$$

Thus $\mathcal{T}$ is closed under finite intersections, and so it is a topology on $X$. Finally, each $B \in \mathcal{B}$ is itself a union of elements of $\mathcal{B}$, so $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$. Since every member of $\mathcal{T}$ is a union of elements of $\mathcal{B}$ by construction, $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$ by Definition 1.1.2.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Definition 1.1.6.** Suppose that $\mathcal{T}$ and $\mathcal{T}'$ are topologies on a given set $X$. If $\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{T}'$, then $\mathcal{T}'$ is called *finer* than $\mathcal T$, and $\mathcal T$ is called *coarser* than $\mathcal T'$. 
If $\mathcal{T} \subset \mathcal T'$, then $\mathcal T'$ is called *strictly finer* than $\mathcal T$, and $\mathcal T$ is called *strictly coarser* than $\mathcal T'$. 
We say that $\mathcal T'$ is *comparable* with $\mathcal T$ if either $\mathcal T \subseteq \mathcal T'$ or $\mathcal T' \subseteq \mathcal T$.
