**Topologies and bases.** Let $X$ be a set of points. A topology $\mathcal{T}$ on $X$ is a collection of *admissible regions* in $X$. These regions, called open sets, include the empty set and the whole space and are closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections.

**Definition 1.1.1.** A *topology* on a set $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ satisfying the following properties: 
1. $\varnothing, X \in \mathcal{T}$;
2. if $U_i \in \mathcal{T}$ for all $i \in I$, then $\bigcup_{i \in I} U_i \in \mathcal{T}$;
3. if $I$ is finite and $U_i \in \mathcal{T}$ for all $i \in I$, then $\bigcap_{i \in I} U_i \in \mathcal{T}$.

The ordered pair $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is called a *topological space*. In this case, a subset $U$ of $X$ is called an *open set* if $U \in \mathcal{T}$. 


**Definition 1.1.2.** Let $(X, \mathcal{T})$ be a topological space. A *basis* for the topology $\mathcal{T}$ is a subcollection $\mathcal{B}$ of $\mathcal{T}$, whose elements are called the *basis elements*, such that every $U \in \mathcal{T}$ can be represented as the union of some basis elements.

If we select an arbitrary collection $\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal P(X)$ and take all possible unions of its elements, will a topology necessarily be formed? The answer is no. The two necessary conditions in the following proposition relate to this very problem.

**Proposition 1.1.3.** A basis $\mathcal{B}$ for a topology on $X$ satisfies the following properties: 
1. $\mathcal{B}$ covers $X$, that is, for every $x \in X$, there exists $B \in \mathcal{B}$ that contains $x$;
2. if $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ contain $x \in X$, then there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$.
{:marker-prefix="B"}

*Proof.* Since $X$ is open, by Definition 1.1.2, $\mathcal{B}$ covers $X$. Similarly, since $B_1 \cap B_2$ is open and $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$, there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Condition (B1) ensures that the basis elements collectively cover the whole space. Condition (B2), on the other hand, requires that finite intersections can be locally refined by basis elements. Together, these conditions are sufficient for a collection of subsets of $X$ to generate a unique topology by taking all possible unions of its elements.

**Proposition 1.1.4.** Suppose that $\mathcal{B}$ is a collection of subsets of $X$ satisfying (B1) and (B2), and that $\mathcal{T}$ is the collection of all unions of the elements of $\mathcal{B}$. Then $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology on $X$ and $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$.

*Proof.* It is clear that $\varnothing \in \mathcal{T}$. We also have $X \in \mathcal{T}$ by (B1).
Let $\{U_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a family in $\mathcal{T}$. For each $i \in I$, there exists a family $\{B_{j}\}_{j \in J_i}$ in $\mathcal{B}$ such that $\bigcup_{j \in J_i} B_{j} = U_i$. It follows that 

$$ \bigcup_{i \in I}U_i = \bigcup_{i \in I}\lowparen{\bigcup_{j \in J_i} B_{j}} \in \mathcal{T}.$$


Now let $U, V \in \mathcal{T}$. For every $x \in U \cap V$, there exist $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_1 \subseteq U$ and $x \in B_2 \subseteq V$. By (B2), there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2 \subseteq U \cap V$. Hence

$$U \cap V = \bigcup\{B \in \mathcal{B} : B \subseteq U \cap V\} \in \mathcal{T}.$$

Thus $\mathcal{T}$ is closed under finite intersections, and so it is a topology on $X$. 
Finally, each $B \in \mathcal{B}$ is itself a union of elements of $\mathcal{B}$, so $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$. Since every member of $\mathcal{T}$ is a union of elements of $\mathcal{B}$ by construction, $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$ by Definition 1.1.2.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Examples of topologies.** Proposition 1.1.4 provides a method for constructing topologies without explicitly specifying all their open sets. By varying the collection of subsets chosen as a basis, we can equip the same underlying set with different topological structures. The following constructions on the real line illustrate this possibility. In each case, the specified collection satisfies conditions (B1) and (B2).

**Definition 1.1.5.** 
1. The topology generated by the collection of all open intervals in $\mathbb{R}$ is called the *standard topology* on $\mathbb{R}$.
2. The topology generated by the collection of all half-open intervals of the form $[a, b)$, where $a < b$, is called the *lower limit topology* on $\mathbb{R}$. The real line $\mathbb{R}$ given with the lower limit topology is denoted by $\mathbb{R}_{\ell}$.
3. Let $K = \{1/n : n \in \mathbb{Z}^+\}$ and let $\mathcal{B}$ be the collection of all open intervals  together with all sets of the form $(a,b) \setminus K$. The topology generated by $\mathcal{B}$ is called the *K-topology* on $\mathbb{R}$. The real line $\mathbb{R}$ given with the K-topology is denoted by $\mathbb{R}_K$.

**Neighborhoods and local bases.** Openness is a global property of a set, involving the topological structure at every point of the set. When describing the local structure near a particular point $x\in X$, however, we need not be able to identify all open sets containing $x$. What matters instead is the information about which sets represent the openness exactly at the point $x$. This consideration leads to the notion of a *neighborhood*, which captures the pointwise aspect of openness. The collection of all neighborhoods of $x$ describes the local structure near $x$.

**Definition 1.1.6.** Let $(X,\mathcal T)$ be a topological space and let $x\in X$. A set $N\subseteq X$ is called a *neighborhood* of $x$ if there exists $U\in\mathcal T$ such that $x\in U\subseteq N$. In particular, if $N$ is itself open, it is called an *open neighborhood* of $x$.
The collection of all neighborhoods of $x$ is called the *neighborhood system* for $x$ and is denoted by $\mathcal{N}(x)$.

Just as a basis for a topology allows us to describe the entire topology in terms of a distinguished collection of open sets, a *local basis* at a point allows us to describe the neighborhood system at that point in terms of a distinguished collection of open neighborhoods. The two notions differ, however, in how they generate their respective structures: a basis generates the topology by forming arbitrary unions of its elements, whereas a local basis generates the neighborhood system by taking all supersets of its elements.

**Definition 1.1.7.** Let $(X,\mathcal T)$ be a topological space. Fix $x\in X$ and define $\mathcal{T}_x = \{ U \in \mathcal{T} : x \in U\}$. A subcollection $\mathcal B_x$ of $\mathcal{T}_x$ is called a *local basis* at $x$ if, for every $N \in \mathcal{N}(x)$, there exists $B\in\mathcal B_x$ such that $B\subseteq N$.

**Proposition 1.1.8.** Let $(X,\mathcal T)$ be a topological space. Then, $U \in \mathcal{T}$ if and only if $U \in \mathcal{N}(x)$ for all $x \in U$.

*Proof.* The forward implication is trivial. Conversely, suppose that $U \in \mathcal{N}(x)$ for all $x \in U$. By Definition 1.1.6, there exists $V \in \mathcal{T}$ such that $x \in V \subseteq U$ for each $x \in U$. The collection of all such $V$ covers $U$, so $U \in \mathcal{T}$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Although the neighborhood system for a single point generally contains insufficient information to recover the topology, the neighborhood systems for all points collectively determine it. Since each neighborhood system can, in turn, be recovered from a local basis, it is natural to expect a close relationship between a basis for the entire topology and local bases at individual points. 

**Theorem 1.1.9.** Let $(X, \mathcal{T})$ be a topological space and let $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$. For each $x \in X$, define $\mathcal{B}_x = \{B \in \mathcal{B} : x \in B\}$. Then, $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$ if and only if $\mathcal{B}_x$ is a local basis at $x$ for every $x \in X$.

*Proof.* Suppose that $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$. Let $x \in X$ and $N \in \mathcal{N}(x)$. By Definition 1.1.6, there exists $U \in \mathcal{T}$ such that $x \in U \subseteq N$. Since $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis, there exists $B \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B \subseteq U$. Hence we have $B \in \mathcal{B}_x$ and $x \in B \subseteq N$, proving that $\mathcal{B}_x$ is a local basis at $x$.
Conversely, suppose that $\mathcal{B}_x$ is a local basis at $x$ for every $x \in X$. Let $U \in \mathcal{T}$ and $x \in U$. Since $U \in \mathcal{N}(x)$, there exists $B \in \mathcal{B}_x$ such that $x \in B \subseteq U$. Since $\mathcal{B}_x \subseteq \mathcal{B}$, we have $B \in \mathcal{B}$, proving that $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**An overview.** So far, we have developed both global and local descriptions of a topological space. At the global level, a topology specifies the open sets of the space and can be generated from a basis by taking arbitrary unions. At the local level, a neighborhood system describes the topological structure near a particular point and can be generated from a local basis by taking all supersets of its elements. **Comparison of topologies.** Until now, we have been concerned with the structure of an individual topology. We now fix the underlying set $X$ and regard the topologies on $X$ themselves as objects of study. Since each topology on $X$ is a subcollection of $\mathcal{P}(X)$, a natural way to compare topologies is by set inclusion. 

**Definition 1.1.10.** Suppose that $\mathcal{T}$ and $\mathcal{T}'$ are topologies on a given set $X$. If $\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{T}'$, then $\mathcal{T}'$ is called *finer* than $\mathcal T$, and $\mathcal T$ is called *coarser* than $\mathcal T'$. In particular, if $\mathcal{T} \subset \mathcal T'$, then $\mathcal T'$ is called *strictly finer* than $\mathcal T$, and $\mathcal T$ is called *strictly coarser* than $\mathcal T'$. We say that $\mathcal T'$ is *comparable* with $\mathcal T$ if either $\mathcal T \subseteq \mathcal T'$ or $\mathcal T' \subseteq \mathcal T$.

**Example 1.1.11.** The lower limit topology and the K-topology on $\mathbb{R}$ are strictly finer than the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$, but are not comparable with one another.

When topologies are specified by their bases rather than by their entire collections of open sets, it is desirable to express the inclusion relation directly in terms of those bases. The following proposition shows that the comparison of topologies can be reduced to a pointwise comparison of their basis elements.

**Proposition 1.1.12.** Let $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{B}'$ be bases for the topologies $\mathcal{T}$ and $\mathcal{T}'$, respectively, on $X$. Then, $\mathcal{T}'$ is finer than $\mathcal{T}$ if and only if, for each $B \in \mathcal{B}$ and each $x \in B$, there exists $B' \in \mathcal{B}'$ such that $x \in B' \subseteq B$.

*Proof.* Suppose that $\mathcal{T}'$ is finer than $\mathcal{T}$. Since $B \in \mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{T}'$, $B$ is open in $(X, \mathcal{T}')$. Therefore, by Definition 1.1.2, there exists $B' \in \mathcal{B}'$ such that $x \in B' \subseteq B$. 
Conversely, suppose that, for each $B \in \mathcal{B}$ and each $x \in B$, there exists $B' \in \mathcal{B}'$ such that $x \in B' \subseteq B$. Let $U \in \mathcal{T}$ and $x \in U$. Then there exists $B \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B \subseteq U$. Hence, there exists $B' \in \mathcal{B}'$ such that $x \in B' \subseteq B \subseteq U$. Therefore we have $x \in B' \subseteq U$ for every $x \in U$, so $U \in \mathcal{T}'$, proving that $\mathcal{T}'$ is finer than $\mathcal{T}$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Set inclusion imposes a partial order on the set of all topologies on $X$. Having introduced this order, we can proceed to investigate its order-theoretic structure. We begin by identifying its least and greatest elements.

**Definition 1.1.13.** The *discrete topology* on $X$ is $\mathcal{P}(X)$, and the *indiscrete topology* on $X$ is $\{\varnothing, X\}$.

**Theorem 1.1.14.** Let $\operatorname{Top}(X)$ denote the set of all topologies on a given set $X$. The set $\operatorname{Top}(X)$, partially ordered by inclusion, is a complete lattice, with join and meet given by 

$$
\bigwedge_{i\in I}\mathcal T_i = \bigcap_{i\in I}\mathcal T_i  
$$

and

$$
\bigvee_{i\in I}\mathcal T_i 
=
\bigcap  
\left\{  
\mathcal S\in\operatorname{Top}(X):  
\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal T_i\subseteq\mathcal S  
\right\}.  
$$

for any family $\{\mathcal T_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $\operatorname{Top}(X)$. Its least element and greatest element are the indiscrete topology and the discrete topology on $X$, respectively.
