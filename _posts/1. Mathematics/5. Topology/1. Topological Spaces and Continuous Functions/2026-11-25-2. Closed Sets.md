**Closed sets.** The dual concept of openness is *closedness*.

**Definition 1.2.1.** Let $(X,\mathcal{T})$ be a topological space. A subset $F$ of $X$ is called a *closed set* if $F^c \in \mathcal{T}$.

**Proposition 1.2.2.** Let $(X,\mathcal T)$ be a topological space, and let $\mathcal C$ be the collection of all closed subsets of $X$. The complementation map

$$
c:\mathcal P(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal P(X),
\qquad S\longmapsto S^c
$$

is an order anti-isomorphism satisfying

$$
c(\mathcal T)=\mathcal C
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
c(\mathcal C)=\mathcal T.
$$

In particular, its restriction to $\mathcal T$ is an order anti-isomorphism between $\mathcal T$ and $\mathcal C$, both ordered by set inclusion.

*Proof.* Since $c\circ c=\operatorname{id}_{\mathcal P(X)}$, the map $c$ is a bijection. Moreover, for any $S,T\in\mathcal P(X)$,

$$
S\subseteq T
\iff T^c\subseteq S^c
\iff c(T)\subseteq c(S).
$$

Thus $c$ is an order anti-isomorphism. By Definition 1.2.1, $c(\mathcal T)=\mathcal C$; applying $c$ once more gives $c(\mathcal C)=\mathcal T$. The assertion concerning the restriction follows immediately. $\square$

**Theorem 1.2.3 (Principle of duality).** Let $(X,\mathcal T)$ be a topological space, and let $\varphi(S_1,\ldots,S_n)$ be a first-order formula in the language of posets augmented by unary predicates for openness and closedness, with all variables ranging over $\mathcal P(X)$. Define its dual formula $\varphi^\partial$ by reversing the order relation and interchanging the predicates of openness and closedness, while leaving equality, logical connectives, and quantifiers unchanged. Then, for any $S_1,\ldots,S_n\in\mathcal P(X)$,

$$
\varphi(S_1,\ldots,S_n)
\iff
\varphi^\partial(S_1^c,\ldots,S_n^c).
$$

In particular, a sentence is true if and only if its dual is true.

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal C$ be the collection of all closed subsets of $X$, and consider the structures

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathfrak X
&=(\mathcal P(X),\subseteq,\mathcal T,\mathcal C),\\
\mathfrak X^\partial
&=(\mathcal P(X),\supseteq,\mathcal C,\mathcal T),
\end{aligned}
$$

where the last two components interpret the predicates of openness and closedness, respectively. For every formula $\psi$ in this language and every variable assignment $s$, we have the semantic duality

$$
\mathfrak X\vDash\psi[s]
\iff
\mathfrak X^\partial\vDash\psi^\partial[s].
$$

This follows by structural induction on $\psi$. The atomic cases follow directly from the definitions of $\mathfrak X^\partial$ and syntactic dualization. The induction steps for logical connectives follow because dualization leaves those connectives unchanged, and the steps for quantifiers follow because both structures have the same domain.
By Proposition 1.2.2, the complementation map is an isomorphism

$$
c:\mathfrak X^\partial\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathfrak X.
$$

Consequently, semantic duality and invariance of truth under isomorphisms give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathfrak X\vDash\varphi[S_1,\ldots,S_n]
&\iff
\mathfrak X^\partial\vDash
\varphi^\partial[S_1,\ldots,S_n]\\
&\iff
\mathfrak X\vDash
\varphi^\partial[S_1^c,\ldots,S_n^c].
\end{aligned}
$$

The assertion concerning sentences follows because their truth is independent of the variable assignment. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Corollary 1.2.4.** Let $(X,\mathcal{T})$ be a topological space. Then the following hold:
1. $\varnothing, X \in \mathcal{C}$;
2. if $F_i \in \mathcal{C}$ for all $i \in I$, then $\bigcap_{i \in I} F_i \in \mathcal{C}$;
3. if $I$ is finite and $F_i \in \mathcal{C}$ for all $i \in I$, then $\bigcup_{i \in I} F_i \in \mathcal{C}$.


**The interior and closure of a set.** Interior of a set $S$ is the greatest admissible region contained in $S$.

**Definition 1.2.5.** Let $S$ be a subset of a topological space $X$. The *interior* of $S$ is the union of all open sets contained in $S$, denoted by $\operatorname{int}S$. Dually, the *closure* of $S$ is the intersection of all closed sets containing $S$, denoted by $\operatorname{cl}S$.

**Proposition 1.2.6.** Let $S$ be a subset of a topological space $X$, and let $x \in S$. Then $N \subseteq X$ is a neighborhood of $x$ if and only if $x \in \operatorname{int}N$.

*Proof.* If $N \in \mathcal{N}(x)$, then there exists $U \in \mathcal{T}$ such that $x \in U \subseteq N$. It follows that $U \subseteq \operatorname{int}N$, so $x \in U \subseteq \operatorname{int}N$. Conversely, suppose $x \in \operatorname{int}N$. Since $\operatorname{int}N$ is an open set contained in $N$, $N \in \mathcal{N}(x)$. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Proposition 1.2.7.** Let $S$ be a subset of a topological space $X$. Then, $x \in \operatorname{cl}S$ if and only if every open neighborhood of $x$ intersects $S$.

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal{C}_S$ be the collection of closed sets containing $S$, and let $\mathcal{T}_{S^c}$ be the collection of open sets contained in $S^c$. Put $F = \operatorname{cl}S$. Then, $F$ is the smallest element in $\mathcal{C}_S$. By semantic duality, $F$ is the greatest element in $C_S^{\mathrm{op}}$. Since $\mathcal{T}_{S^c}$ and $C_S^{\mathrm{op}}$ are order isomorphic, $F^c$ is the greatest element in $\mathcal{T}_{S^c}$. It follows that 

$$ 
\begin{align}
x \in F 
&\iff x \notin F^c\\
&\iff \text{for all } U \in \mathcal{T}_{S^c}, \text{ we have } x \notin U\\
&\iff \text{for all } U \in \mathcal{T}, \text{ if } x \in U, \text{ then } U \not\subseteq S^c.
\end{align}
$$

Therefore $x \in \operatorname{cl}S$ if and only if every open neighborhood of $x$ intersects $S$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>
