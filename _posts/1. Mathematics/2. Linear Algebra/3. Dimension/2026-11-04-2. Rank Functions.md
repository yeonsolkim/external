**The subset and subspace lattices.**

**Proposition 3.2.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space. Then, the partially ordered set $\mathcal P(V)$ is a lattice, with join and meet given by

$$
S\vee T=S\cup T
\quad \text{and} \quad
S\wedge T=S\cap T
$$

for all $S,T\in\mathcal P(V)$. Its least and greatest elements are $\varnothing$ and $V$, respectively.

**Proposition 3.2.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space. Then, the partially ordered set $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ is a lattice, with join and meet given by

$$
U\vee W=U+W
\quad \text{and} \quad
U\wedge W=U\cap W  
$$

for all $U,W\in\operatorname{Sub}(V)$. The least and greatest elements of $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ are $\{0_V\}$ and $V$, respectively.

**The span map.**

**Definition 3.2.3.** Let $V$ be a vector space. The *span map* is the function

$$\sigma \colon \mathcal{P}(V) \to \operatorname{Sub}(V), \qquad S \mapsto \operatorname{span}(S).$$

**Proposition 3.2.4.** The span map $\sigma$ satisfies the following properties:

1. $\sigma$ is monotone;
2. $\sigma$ preserves join, that is, $\sigma(S \cup T) = \sigma(S) + \sigma(T)$ for all $S, T \in \mathcal{P}(V)$;
3. $\sigma$ is surjective, that is, $\sigma(W) = W$ for all $W \in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$.


**Proposition 3.2.5 (Galois connection).** Let $\iota \colon \operatorname{Sub}(V) \hookrightarrow \mathcal{P}(V)$ be the inclusion map. Then, for every $S \in \mathcal{P}(V)$ and $W \in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$,

$$\sigma(S) \subseteq W \iff S \subseteq \iota(W).$$

In particular, $\sigma$ is left adjoint to $\iota$, and $\sigma \circ \iota = \operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Sub}(V)}$.

**Remark 3.2.6.** Note that the composite $\iota \circ \sigma \colon \mathcal{P}(V) \to \mathcal{P}(V)$ is precisely the span operator considered in section 2.4.

**Proposition 3.2.7.** For all $S, T \in \mathcal{P}(V)$, we have

$$\sigma(S \cap T) \subseteq \sigma(S) \cap \sigma(T).$$

In general, this inclusion can be strict.


The span map preserves joins but does not, in general, preserve meets. Consequently, it is not a lattice homomorphism. 

**Distributivity and modularity.** We now compare the distributive and modular properties of the two lattices. Although the power-set lattice is distributive, the subspace lattice is generally not. Instead, the subspace lattice satisfies the weaker modular law.

**Proposition 3.2.8.** The power-set lattice $\mathcal{P}(V)$ is distributive. 

**Proposition 3.2.9.** The subspace lattice $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ is modular.

The subspace lattice is therefore modular but, whenever $\dim V \ge 2$, not distributive. In contrast, the power-set lattice is always distributive and hence modular. Thus, the implication from distributivity to modularity is strict in general. If $\dim V \le 1$, the subspace lattice is a chain and is therefore distributive.

**Remark 3.2.10.** The distinction between the two lattices can also be understood through the span map. Recall that

$$\sigma(S \cup T) = \sigma(S) \vee \sigma(T),$$

whereas

$$\sigma(S \cap T) \subseteq \sigma(S) \wedge \sigma(T)$$

with strict inclusion possible. Hence, although the span map preserves joins, it does not generally preserve meets. The distributive identities of the power-set lattice therefore cannot simply be transferred through this map to the subspace lattice. The failure of distributivity in the latter reflects the fact that taking linear spans can introduce common vectors that do not belong to the original intersection.

**Dimension as a modular rank function.** We now assume that $V$ is finite-dimensional, with $\dim V = n$. We show how the dimension function on the subspace lattice gives rise to a modular rank function, and how the span map induces a submodular rank function on the power-set lattice.


**Theorem 3.2.11.** The function

$$\rho \colon \operatorname{Sub}(V) \to \{0,1,\ldots,n\}, \qquad \rho(W) = \dim W$$

is a modular rank function on the subspace lattice $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$.

**Corollary 3.2.12 (Grassmann's formula).** Let $V$ be a vector space. Then, for all $U,W\in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$, 

$$\dim(U + W) + \dim(U \cap W) = \dim U + \dim W.$$


**Definition 3.2.13.** The _linear rank function_ on the power set of a vector space $V$ is the function

$$r \colon \mathcal{P}(V) \to \{0,1,\ldots,n\}, \qquad r(S) = \dim\operatorname{span}(S).$$

**Proposition 3.2.14.** The linear rank function $r$ satisfies the following properties:

1. $r(\varnothing) = 0$;
2. $r$ is monotone;
3. $r$ is submodular.


**Remark 3.2.15.** The difference between the two sides of the submodular inequality can be expressed more precisely as

$$ r(S) + r(T) - r(S \cup T) - r(S \cap T) = \dim(\sigma(S) \cap \sigma(T)) - \dim (\sigma(S \cap T)). $$

Thus, equality holds if and only if

$$\operatorname{span}(S \cap T) = \operatorname{span}(S) \cap \operatorname{span}(T).$$

**Remark 3.2.16.** Note that the function $r$ is not the rank function of the power-set lattice in the sense of graded lattices. Rather, it measures the dimension of the subspace generated by a subset. If $E$ is a finite subset of $V$, the restriction of $r$ to $\mathcal{P}(E)$ is the rank function of the linear matroid represented by $E$. Thus, the submodularity of linear rank provides a connection between the lattice-theoretic properties developed here and matroid theory.
