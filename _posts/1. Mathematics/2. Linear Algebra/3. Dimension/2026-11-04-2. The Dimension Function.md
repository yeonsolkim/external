**The subset lattice and subspace lattice.**

**Proposition 3.2.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space. Then, the partially ordered set $\mathcal P(V)$ is a lattice, with join and meet given by

$$
S\vee T=S\cup T
\quad \text{and} \quad
S\wedge T=S\cap T
$$

for all $S,T\in\mathcal P(V)$. Its least and greatest elements are $\varnothing$ and $V$, respectively.

*Proof.* Since $S,T\subseteq V$, we have $S\cup T\subseteq V$ and $S\cap T\subseteq V$. Let $A\in\mathcal P(V)$. If $A\supseteq S$ and $A\supseteq T$, then $A\supseteq S\cup T$. Hence $S\cup T$ is the supremum for $S$ and $T$. On the other hand, if $A\subseteq S$ and $A\subseteq T$, then $A\subseteq S\cap T$. Hence $S\cap T$ is the infimum for $S$ and $T$. By Proposition 1.3.7, $(\mathcal P(V),\cup)$ and $(\mathcal P(V),\cap)$ are an upper semilattice and lower semilattice, respectively. Furthermore, since $S\cap T \subseteq S$ and $S\subseteq S\cup T$, we have

$$
\begin{align}
S\cap (S\cup T) &= (S\cap S) \cup (S\cap T) = S,\\
S\cup (S\cap T) &= (S\cup S) \cap (S\cup T) = S,
\end{align}
$$

for all $S,T\in \mathcal P(V)$. By Definition 1.3.9, $(\mathcal P(V),\cup,\cap)$ is a lattice.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


**Proposition 3.2.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space. Then, the partially ordered set $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ is a lattice, with join and meet given by

$$
U\vee W=U+W
\quad \text{and} \quad
U\wedge W=U\cap W  
$$

for all $U,W\in\operatorname{Sub}(V)$. The least and greatest elements of $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ are $\{0_V\}$ and $V$, respectively.

*Proof.* Since $U+W$ is a subspace of $V$ and is the supremum for $U$ and $W$ by Theorem 2.2.12, $(\operatorname{Sub}(V), +)$ is an upper semilattice. On the other hand, by Theorem 2.2.9, $U\cap W$ is a subspace of $W$ and is the infimum for $U$ and $W$. Hence $(\operatorname{Sub}(V),\cap)$ is a lower semilattice. Furthermore, since $U\subseteq U+W$ and $U\cap W\subseteq U$, we have 

$$ U\cap (U+W) = U\quad \text{and} \quad U + (U \cap W) = U$$

for all $U,V\in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$. By Definition 1.3.9, $(\operatorname{Sub}(V), +, \cap)$ is a lattice. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

We now compare the distributive and modular properties of the two lattices. Although the subset lattice is distributive and hence modular, the subspace lattice is generally not. Instead, the subspace lattice satisfies the weaker modular law.

**Theorem 3.2.3.** Let $V$ be a vector space.
1. The subset lattice $\mathcal{P}(V)$ is distributive. 
2. The subspace lattice $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ is modular.

*Proof.* (1) By the definitions of union and intersection, union is distributive over intersection and vice versa. Hence $\mathcal{P}(V)$ is distributive.
(2) Let $W_1, W_2, W_3 \in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$ and $W_1\subseteq W_3$. Suppose $w\in W_1 + (W_2 \cap W_3)$. Then there exist $w_1\in W_1$ and $w_2\in W_2 \cap W_3$ such that $w = w_1 + w_2$. Hence $w=w_1+w_2 \in W_1 + W_2$. Furthermore, since $W_1 \subseteq W_3$, we have $w_1 \in W_3$ and $w_2\in W_3$. Since $W_3\in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$, $w=w_1 + w_2 \in W_3$. Thus $W_1 + (W_2 \cap W_3) \subseteq (W_1 + W_2) \cap W_3$. 
We now suppose $w \in (W_1 + W_2) \cap W_3$. Then there exist $w_1 \in W_1$ and $w_2 \in W_2$ such that $w = w_1 + w_2$. Since $W_1 \subseteq W_3$ and $W_3 \in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$, $w_1 \in W_3$ so that $w_2 = w - w_1 \in W_3$. Thus $w = w_1 + w_2 \in W_1 + (W_2 \cap W_3)$, so $W_1 + (W_2 \cap W_3) \supseteq (W_1 + W_2) \cap W_3$. 
It is concluded that $W_1 + (W_2 \cap W_3) = (W_1 + W_2) \cap W_3$, and, by Definition 1.3.13, $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ is modular.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


**Galois connections and the span map.** Although the subset lattice and the subspace lattice are both ordered by inclusion, their join operations are different. Nevertheless, there is a natural relationship between them: every subset of $V$ generates a smallest subspace containing it, while every subspace can be regarded as a subset of $V$. These two constructions are related by a _Galois connection_, a general order-theoretic relationship between pairs of maps. We first establish the basic properties of Galois connections and then apply them to the span map and the inclusion map. In particular, their composite recovers the span operator studied earlier, providing a different perspective on its closure properties.

**Definition 3.2.4.** Let $(P,\le_P)$ and $(Q,\le_Q)$ be posets, and let $F \colon P\to Q$ and $G \colon Q\to P$ be maps. The pair $(F,G)$ is called a *Galois connection* between these posets if, for every $p\in P$ and $q\in Q$,

$$
F(p)\le_Q q\iff p\le_P G(q).
$$

In this case, $F$ is called the *left adjoint* of $G$, and $G$ is called the *right adjoint* of $F$. We write $F\dashv G$.

**Theorem 3.2.5.** Suppose that two lattices $L$ and $M$ have a Galois connection, and $F$ and $G$ are the left adjoint and the right adjoint, respectively. Then the following hold: 

1. $F$ and $G$ are order-preserving;
2. $F$ and $G$ preserve join and meet, respectively;
3. $G \circ F$ is a closure operator. 

*Proof.* (1) Suppose $l, l' \in L$ and $l \le l'$. Since $F(l) \le F(l)$,  $l' \le G(F(l'))$. It follows that $l \le l' \le G(F(l'))$, which is equivalent to $F(l) \le F(l')$. Hence the left adjoint $F$ is order-preserving. It follows from duality that the right adjoint is also order-preserving.
(2) Let $l, l' \in L$. Since $F$ is order-preserving, by Theorem 1.3.11, $F(l \vee l') \ge F(l) \vee F(l')$. We now show the reverse inequality. Firstly, we have $F(l) \le F(l) \vee F(l')$ and $F(l') \le F(l) \vee F(l')$. Since $G$ is monotone, $G(F(l)) \le G(F(l) \vee F(l'))$ and $G(F(l')) \le G(F(l) \vee F(l'))$. Since $x \le G(F(x))$ for all $x\in L$, it follows that $l \le G(F(l)) \le G(F(l) \vee F(l'))$ and $l' \le G(F(l')) \le G(F(l) \vee F(l'))$. Therefore $l \vee l' \le G(F(l) \vee F(l'))$, which is equivalent to $F(l \vee l') \le F(l) \vee F(l')$. Consequently, $F(l \vee l') = F(l) \vee F(l')$, so $F$ preserves join. It follows from duality that $G$ preserves meet.
(3) Put $\operatorname{cl} = G \circ F$. Since $F(l) \le F(l)$, $l \le G(F(l)) = \operatorname{cl}(l)$. On the other hand, we have $F(\operatorname{cl}(l)) \le F(l)$ from $G(F(l)) \le G(F(l))$. Hence, if $l \le G(m)$ then $F(\operatorname{cl}(l)) \le F(l) \le m$. By Theorem 2.4.4, $\operatorname{cl}$ is a closure operator.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

We now apply these general results to the subset and subspace lattices of a vector space. 

**Definition 3.2.6.** Let $V$ be a vector space. The *span map* is the function

$$\sigma \colon \mathcal{P}(V) \to \operatorname{Sub}(V), \qquad S \mapsto \operatorname{span}(S).$$

**Theorem 3.2.7.** Let $\sigma$ be the span map, and let $\iota \colon \operatorname{Sub}(V) \hookrightarrow \mathcal{P}(V)$ be the inclusion map. Then the pair $(\sigma, \iota)$ is a Galois connection between $\mathcal{P}(V)$ and $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$. Hence the composite $\iota \circ \sigma$ is a closure operator on $\mathcal{P}(V)$.

*Proof.* Let $S \in \mathcal{P}(V)$ and $W \in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$. Suppose $\sigma(S) \subseteq W$ and let $s\in S$. Since $s \in \operatorname{span}(S)$, $s \in \sigma(S) \subseteq W$. Hence $S \subseteq \iota(W)$. Conversely, suppose $S \subseteq \iota(W)$ and let $v \in \sigma(S)$. Then there exist a finite set $I$ and indexed families $\{s_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $S$ and $\{a_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $F$ such that $v=\sum_{i\in I}a_is_i$. Since $\{s_i : i\in I\} \subseteq S \subseteq \iota(W) = W$ and $W$ is a subspace, $v=\sum_{i\in I}a_is_i \in W$. Hence $\sigma(S) \subseteq W$. By Definition 3.2.4, $(\sigma, \iota)$ is a Galois connection. Furthermore, by Theorem 3.2.5, $\iota \circ \sigma$ is a closure operator on $\mathcal{P}(V)$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Remark 3.2.8.** 

**Theorem 3.2.9.** For all $S, T \in \mathcal{P}(V)$, we have

$$\sigma(S \cup T) = \sigma(S) + \sigma(T)
\quad \text{and} \quad 
\sigma(S \cap T) \subseteq \sigma(S) \cap \sigma(T),$$

where the inclusion can be strict.


Thus, the span map preserves joins but does not, in general, preserve meets. Consequently, the distributivity of the subset lattice is not automatically inherited by the subspace lattice through this map. This distinction will also play an important role in the next subsection, where the dimension function on subspaces is used to define a rank function on subsets. As we shall see, the failure of meet preservation accounts for the submodularity of the resulting rank function.


**Dimension as a rank function.** We now assume that $V$ is finite-dimensional, with $\dim V = n$. We show how the dimension function on the subspace lattice gives rise to a modular rank function, and how the span map induces a submodular rank function on the subset lattice.


**Theorem 3.2.10.** The function

$$\rho \colon \operatorname{Sub}(V) \to \{0,1,\ldots,n\}, \qquad \rho(W) = \dim W$$

is a modular rank function on the subspace lattice $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$.

**Corollary 3.2.11 (Grassmann's formula).** Let $V$ be a vector space. Then, for all $U,W\in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$, 

$$\dim(U + W) + \dim(U \cap W) = \dim U + \dim W.$$


**Definition 3.2.12.** The _linear rank function_ on the power set of a vector space $V$ is the function

$$r \colon \mathcal{P}(V) \to \{0,1,\ldots,n\}, \qquad r(S) = \dim\operatorname{span}(S).$$

**Proposition 3.2.13.** The linear rank function $r$ satisfies the following properties:

1. $r(\varnothing) = 0$;
2. $r$ is monotone;
3. $r$ is submodular.


**Remark 3.2.14.** The difference between the two sides of the submodular inequality can be expressed more precisely as

$$ r(S) + r(T) - r(S \cup T) - r(S \cap T) = \dim(\sigma(S) \cap \sigma(T)) - \dim (\sigma(S \cap T)). $$

Thus, equality holds if and only if

$$\operatorname{span}(S \cap T) = \operatorname{span}(S) \cap \operatorname{span}(T).$$

**Remark 3.2.15.** Note that the function $r$ is not the rank function of the power-set lattice in the sense of graded lattices. Rather, it measures the dimension of the subspace generated by a subset. If $E$ is a finite subset of $V$, the restriction of $r$ to $\mathcal{P}(E)$ is the rank function of the linear matroid represented by $E$. Thus, the submodularity of linear rank provides a connection between the lattice-theoretic properties developed here and matroid theory.
