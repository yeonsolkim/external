**The subset and subspace lattices.**

**Proposition 3.2.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space. Then, the partially ordered set $\mathcal P(V)$ is a lattice, with join and meet given by

$$
S\vee T=S\cup T
\quad \text{and} \quad
S\wedge T=S\cap T
$$

for all $S,T\in\mathcal P(V)$. Its least and greatest elements are $\varnothing$ and $V$, respectively.

*Proof.* Since $S,T\subseteq V$, we have $S\cup T\subseteq V$ and $S\cap T\subseteq V$. Let $A\in\mathcal P(V)$. If $A\supseteq S$ and $A\supseteq T$, then $A\supseteq S\cup T$. Hence $S\cup T$ is the supremum for $S$ and $T$. On the other hand, if $A\subseteq S$ and $A\subseteq T$, then $A\subseteq S\cap T$. Hence $S\cap T$ is the infimum for $S$ and $T$. By Proposition 1.3.7, $(\mathcal P(V),\cup)$ and $(\mathcal P(V),\cap)$ are an upper semilattice and lower semilattice, respectively. Furthermore, since $S\cap T \subseteq S$ and $S\subseteq S\cup T$, we have

$$
\begin{align}
S\cap (S\cup T) &= (S\cap S) \cup (S\cap T) = S,\\
S\cup (S\cap T) &= (S\cup S) \cap (S\cup T) = S,
\end{align}
$$

for all $S,T\in \mathcal P(V)$. By Definition 1.3.9, $(\mathcal P(V),\cup,\cap)$ is a lattice.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


**Proposition 3.2.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space. Then, the partially ordered set $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ is a lattice, with join and meet given by

$$
U\vee W=U+W
\quad \text{and} \quad
U\wedge W=U\cap W  
$$

for all $U,W\in\operatorname{Sub}(V)$. The least and greatest elements of $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ are $\{0_V\}$ and $V$, respectively.

*Proof.* Since $U+W$ is a subspace of $V$ and is the supremum for $U$ and $W$ by Theorem 2.2.12, $(\operatorname{Sub}(V), +)$ is an upper semilattice. On the other hand, by Theorem 2.2.9, $U\cap W$ is a subspace of $W$ and is the infimum for $U$ and $W$. Hence $(\operatorname{Sub}(V),\cap)$ is a lower semilattice. Furthermore, since $U\subseteq U+W$ and $U\cap W\subseteq U$, we have 

$$ U\cap (U+W) = U\quad \text{and} \quad U + (U \cap W) = U$$

for all $U,V\in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$. By Definition 1.3.9, $(\operatorname{Sub}(V), +, \cap)$ is a lattice. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

We now compare the distributive and modular properties of the two lattices. Although the subset lattice is distributive and hence modular, the subspace lattice is generally not. Instead, the subspace lattice satisfies the weaker modular law.

**Theorem 3.2.3.** Let $V$ be a vector space.
1. The subset lattice $\mathcal{P}(V)$ is distributive. 
2. The subspace lattice $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ is modular.

*Proof.* (1) Since union is distributive over intersection and vice versa, $\mathcal{P}(V)$ is distributive.
(2) Let $W_1, W_2, W_3 \in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$ and $W_1\subseteq W_3$. Suppose $w\in W_1 + (W_2 \cap W_3)$. Then there exist $w_1\in W_1$ and $w_2\in W_2 \cap W_3$ such that $w = w_1 + w_2$. Hence $w=w_1+w_2 \in W_1 + W_2$. Furthermore, since $W_1 \subseteq W_3$, we have $w_1 \in W_3$ and $w_2\in W_3$. Since $W_3\in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$, $w=w_1 + w_2 \in W_3$. Thus $W_1 + (W_2 \cap W_3) \subseteq (W_1 + W_2) \cap W_3$. 
We now suppose $w \in (W_1 + W_2) \cap W_3$. Then there exist $w_1 \in W_1$ and $w_2 \in W_2$ such that $w = w_1 + w_2$. Since $W_1 \subseteq W_3$ and $W_3 \in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$, $w_1 \in W_3$ so that $w_2 = w - w_1 \in W_3$. Thus $w = w_1 + w_2 \in W_1 + (W_2 \cap W_3)$, so $W_1 + (W_2 \cap W_3) \supseteq (W_1 + W_2) \cap W_3$. 
It is concluded that $W_1 + (W_2 \cap W_3) = (W_1 + W_2) \cap W_3$, and, by Definition 1.3.13, $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$ is modular.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


**Connection between $\mathcal{P}(V)$ and $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$.**

**Definition 3.2.4.** Let $V$ be a vector space. The *span map* is the function

$$\sigma \colon \mathcal{P}(V) \to \operatorname{Sub}(V), \qquad S \mapsto \operatorname{span}(S).$$

**Proposition 3.2.5.** The span map $\sigma$ satisfies the following properties:

1. $\sigma$ is monotone;
2. $\sigma$ preserves joins, that is, $\sigma(S \cup T) = \sigma(S) + \sigma(T)$ for all $S, T \in \mathcal{P}(V)$;
3. $\sigma$ is surjective, that is, $\sigma(W) = W$ for all $W \in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$.

*Proof.* Since the span operator is a closure operator, $\sigma$ is monotone. By Corollary 2.4.10, we have $\operatorname{span}(S \cup T) = \operatorname{span}(S) + \operatorname{span}(T)$ for all $S, T \in \mathcal{P}(V)$, so $\sigma$ preserves joins. Since every subspace of $V$ is closed under the span operator by Theorem 2.4.8, $\sigma(W) = W$ for all $W \in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$. Thus $\sigma$ is surjective.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


**Theorem 3.2.6 (Galois connection).** Let $\iota \colon \operatorname{Sub}(V) \hookrightarrow \mathcal{P}(V)$ be the inclusion map. Then, for every $S \in \mathcal{P}(V)$ and $W \in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$,

$$\sigma(S) \subseteq W \iff S \subseteq \iota(W).$$

In particular, $\sigma$ is left adjoint to $\iota$, and $\sigma \circ \iota = \operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Sub}(V)}$.

**Remark 3.2.7.** Note that the composite $\iota \circ \sigma \colon \mathcal{P}(V) \to \mathcal{P}(V)$ is precisely the span operator considered in section 2.4.

**Proposition 3.2.8.** For all $S, T \in \mathcal{P}(V)$, we have

$$\sigma(S \cap T) \subseteq \sigma(S) \cap \sigma(T).$$

In general, this inclusion can be strict.


The span map preserves joins but does not, in general, preserve meets. Consequently, it is not a lattice homomorphism. 

**Remark 3.2.9.** The distinction between the two lattices can also be understood through the span map. Recall that

$$\sigma(S \cup T) = \sigma(S) \vee \sigma(T),$$

whereas

$$\sigma(S \cap T) \subseteq \sigma(S) \wedge \sigma(T)$$

with strict inclusion possible. Hence, although the span map preserves joins, it does not generally preserve meets. The distributive identities of the power-set lattice therefore cannot simply be transferred through this map to the subspace lattice. The failure of distributivity in the latter reflects the fact that taking linear spans can introduce common vectors that do not belong to the original intersection.

**Dimension as a rank function.** We now assume that $V$ is finite-dimensional, with $\dim V = n$. We show how the dimension function on the subspace lattice gives rise to a modular rank function, and how the span map induces a submodular rank function on the power-set lattice.


**Theorem 3.2.10.** The function

$$\rho \colon \operatorname{Sub}(V) \to \{0,1,\ldots,n\}, \qquad \rho(W) = \dim W$$

is a modular rank function on the subspace lattice $\operatorname{Sub}(V)$.

**Corollary 3.2.11 (Grassmann's formula).** Let $V$ be a vector space. Then, for all $U,W\in \operatorname{Sub}(V)$, 

$$\dim(U + W) + \dim(U \cap W) = \dim U + \dim W.$$


**Definition 3.2.12.** The _linear rank function_ on the power set of a vector space $V$ is the function

$$r \colon \mathcal{P}(V) \to \{0,1,\ldots,n\}, \qquad r(S) = \dim\operatorname{span}(S).$$

**Proposition 3.2.13.** The linear rank function $r$ satisfies the following properties:

1. $r(\varnothing) = 0$;
2. $r$ is monotone;
3. $r$ is submodular.


**Remark 3.2.14.** The difference between the two sides of the submodular inequality can be expressed more precisely as

$$ r(S) + r(T) - r(S \cup T) - r(S \cap T) = \dim(\sigma(S) \cap \sigma(T)) - \dim (\sigma(S \cap T)). $$

Thus, equality holds if and only if

$$\operatorname{span}(S \cap T) = \operatorname{span}(S) \cap \operatorname{span}(T).$$

**Remark 3.2.15.** Note that the function $r$ is not the rank function of the power-set lattice in the sense of graded lattices. Rather, it measures the dimension of the subspace generated by a subset. If $E$ is a finite subset of $V$, the restriction of $r$ to $\mathcal{P}(E)$ is the rank function of the linear matroid represented by $E$. Thus, the submodularity of linear rank provides a connection between the lattice-theoretic properties developed here and matroid theory.
