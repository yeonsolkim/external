**Linearity and preservation.** We suppose that $V$ and $W$ are vector spaces over the same field $F$, and $\tau$ is a function from $V$ to $W$. The function $\tau$ is said to be *linear* if it preserves the vector-space structures of $V$ and $W$. 

**Definition 3.1.1.** Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over the same field $F$. A function $\tau\colon V\to W$ is called a *linear map* from $V$ to $W$ if for all $u,v\in V$ and $a\in F$ the following conditions hold: 
1. $\tau(u+v)=\tau(u) +\tau(v)$; 
2. $\tau(av) = a\tau(v)$.

The set of all linear maps from $V$ to $W$ is denoted by $\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$. 
A linear map from $V$ to $V$ is called a *linear operator* on $V$. The *identity operator* $\operatorname{id}$ on $V$ is a linear operator on $V$ such that $\operatorname{id}(v)=v$ for all $v\in V$. The set of all linear operators on $V$ is denoted by $\operatorname{End}(V)$. A linear operator on a real vector space is called a *real operator*.

In real analysis, differentiation and integration provide us with two of the most important examples of linear maps.

**Example 3.1.2.** 
1. Let $C^{\infty}$ denote the set of all real-valued functions defined on $\mathbb R$ that have derivatives of every order. Then $C^{\infty}$ is a vector space. Define $D\colon C^{\infty}\to C^{\infty}$ by $Df=f'$. Then, the operator $D$ is linear. 
2. Let $C([0,1])$ be a vector space of continuous real-valued functions defined on $[0,1]$. Define 
   
   $$  I:C([0,1])\to C^1([0,1]),\qquad f\mapsto If, $$
   
   where 
   
   $$ (If)(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt. $$
   
   Then the linearity of $I$ follows from that of integration.


A coordinate map, defined in Definition 2.6.6, is another good example of linear maps.

**Proposition 3.1.3.** Let $B$ be a basis for a vector space $V$ over a field $F$. Then the coordinate map $\phi_B\colon V\to F^{(B)}$ is linear.

*Proof.* Let $u,v\in V$, let $a\in F$, and let 

$$ S = \operatorname{supp}(c_{u+v}^B) \cup \operatorname{supp}(c_{u}^B) \cup \operatorname{supp}(c_{v}^B).$$

Then $S$ is finite and linearly independent. Since

$$ \sum_{b\in \operatorname{supp}(c_{u+v}^B)}c_{u+v}^B(b)b = \sum_{b\in \operatorname{supp}(c_{u}^B)}c_{u}^B(b)b + \sum_{b\in \operatorname{supp}(c_{v}^B)}c_{v}^B(b)b, $$

we have 

$$ \sum_{b\in S}(c_{u+v}^B(b)-c_{u}^B(b)-c_{v}^B(b))b = 0_V.$$

Since $S$ is linearly independent, Theorem 2.5.6 yields

$$c_{u+v}^B(b) = c_{u}^B(b) + c_{v}^B(b)$$

for all $b\in S$, and this equality holds for all $b\in B$. It follows that

$$ c_{u+v}^B = c_{u}^B + c_{v}^B.$$


Similarly, let 

$$ T = \operatorname{supp}(c_{av}^B) \cup \operatorname{supp}(c_{v}^B).$$ 

Then $T$ is finite and linearly independent. Since 

$$ \sum_{b\in \operatorname{supp}(c_{av}^B)}c_{av}^B(b)b = a\sum_{b\in \operatorname{supp}(c_{v}^B)}c_{v}^B(b)b,$$

we have

$$ \sum_{b\in T}(c_{av}^B(b)-ac_{v}^B(b))b = 0_V.$$

Since $T$ is linearly independent, Theorem 2.5.6 yields

$$c_{av}^B(b) = ac_{v}^B(b)$$

for all $b\in S$, and this equality holds for all $b\in B$. It follows that

$$ c_{av}^B = ac_{v}^B.$$


We have shown that $\phi_B(u+v)=\phi_B(u)+\phi_B(v)$ and $\phi_B(av)=a\phi_B(v)$. Therefore $\phi_B$ is linear.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>


**Proposition 3.1.4.** Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $F$, and let $\tau\colon V \to W$ be a function.
1. If $\tau$ is linear, then $\tau(0_V)=0_W$.
2. If $\tau$ is linear, then $\tau(-v)=-\tau(v)$ for all $v\in V$.
3. The function $\tau$ is linear if and only if, for all $u,v\in V$ and $a\in F$, we have 
   
   $$\tau(au+v)=a\tau(u)+\tau(v).$$ 


*Proof.* (1) We have $v+0_V=v$ for all $v\in V$. Since $\tau$ is linear, $\tau(v) + \tau(0_V) = \tau(v)$. Since the zero vector in $W$ is unique, $\tau(0_V)=0_W$.
(2) Since $v+(-v)=0_V$, we have $\tau(v)+\tau(-v)=0_W$ by (1). Since the inverse of a vector is unique, $\tau(-v)=-\tau(v)$.
(3) If $\tau$ is linear, then $\tau(au+v)=\tau(au) + \tau(v)=a\tau(u) + \tau(v)$.
We now prove the converse. Setting $a=1$, we obtain $\tau(u+v)=\tau(u)+\tau(v)$. Hence $\tau(0_V)=0_W$. Therefore, setting $v=0_V$ gives $\tau(au)=a\tau(u)$, so $\tau$ is linear.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 3.1.5.** A function $\tau\colon V\to W$ is linear if and only if for any finite set $I$ and indexed families $\{v_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $V$ and $\{a_i\}_{i\in I}$ in $F$, 

$$ 
\tau\lowparen{\sum_{i\in I}a_iv_i}
=  
\sum_{i\in I}a_i\tau(v_i)
$$

*Proof.* Suppose that $\tau$ is linear. We proceed by induction on $n=|I|$. If $n=0$, then 

$$ \tau\lowparen{\sum_{i\in I}a_iv_i} = \tau(0_V) = 0_W = \sum_{i\in I}a_i\tau(v_i).$$


We now suppose that the statements holds for $n=k$. Let $|I|=k+1$ and choose $i'\in I$. Define $I' = I\setminus\{i'\}$. Since $|I'|=k$, by induction hypothesis and linearity,

$$
\begin{align}
\tau\lowparen{\sum_{i\in I}a_iv_i} &= \tau\lowparen{a_{i'}v_{i'} +\sum_{i\in I'}a_iv_i}\\
&= \tau(a_{i'}v_{i'}) + \tau\lowparen{\sum_{i\in I'}a_iv_i}\\
&= a_{i'}\tau(v_{i'}) + \sum_{i\in I'}a_i\tau(v_i)\\
&= \sum_{i\in I}a_i\tau(v_i).
\end{align}
$$


The backward implication is trivial.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Proposition 3.1.6.** Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces, and let $\tau\in \operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$. 
1. If $U$ is a subspace of $W$, then $\tau^{-1}(U)$ is a subspace of $V$.
2. If $U$ is a subspace of $V$, then $\tau(U)$ is a subspace of $W$.

*Proof.* (1) Since $\tau(0_V)=0_W$ and $0_W\in U$, $0_V\in \tau^{-1}(U)$. Let $u,v\in \tau^{-1}(U)$. Then we have $\tau(u)\in U$ and $\tau(v)\in U$. Since $U$ is a subspace, $\tau(u)+\tau(v)\in U$. Since linearity gives $\tau(u+v)=\tau(u)+\tau(v)$, $u+v\in \tau^{-1}(U)$. Similarly, closedness of $U$ under scalar multiplication and linearity of $\tau$ gives $av\in \tau^{-1}(U)$, where $a\in F$. By Theorem 2.2.2, $\tau^{-1}(U)$ is a subspace of $V$.
(2) Since $0_V\in U$, $0_W\in \tau(U)$ by Proposition 3.1.4(1). Let $u,v\in \tau(U)$ and let $s\in \tau^{-1}(\{u\})$ and $t\in \tau^{-1}(\{v\})$. Since $s+t\in U$, linearity gives $u+v=\tau(s+t)\in \tau(U)$. Similarly, since $as\in U$, we have $au=\tau(as)\in \tau(U)$, where $a\in F$. By Theorem 2.2.2, $\tau(U)$ is a subspace of $W$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Proposition 3.1.7.** Let $B$ be a basis for $V$. For any function $f\colon B\to W$, there is a unique map $\tau\in \operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$ such that $\tau(b)=f(b)$ for all $b\in B$.

*Proof.* Let $v\in V$ and let $c_v^B\colon B\to F$ be the representation of $v$ in terms of $B$. Define 

$$ \tau\colon V\longrightarrow W,\qquad v\longmapsto \sum_{b\in \operatorname{supp}(c_v^B)}c_v^B(b)f(b).$$

Since $B$ is linearly independent, $v\in B$ has the unique representation, such that $c_v^B(v) = 1$ and $\operatorname{supp}(c_v^B) = \{v\}$. Hence $\tau(b)=f(b)$ for all $b\in B$. 
We now show the linearity of $\tau$. Let

$$S=\operatorname{supp}(c_u^B)\cup\operatorname{supp}(c_v^B). $$

Since $c_{au+v}^B  = ac_u^B + c_v^B$ by Proposition 3.1.3, we have $\operatorname{supp}(c_{au+v}^B)\subseteq S$. Therefore 

$$ \begin{aligned} \tau(au+v) &=\sum_{b\in\operatorname{supp}(c_{au+v}^B)} c_{au+v}^B(b)f(b)\\ &=\sum_{b\in S}c_{au+v}^B(b)f(b)\\ &=\sum_{b\in S}\left(ac_u^B(b)+c_v^B(b)\right)f(b)\\ &=a\sum_{b\in S}c_u^B(b)f(b) +\sum_{b\in S}c_v^B(b)f(b)\\ &=a\sum_{b\in\operatorname{supp}(c_u^B)}c_u^B(b)f(b) +\sum_{b\in\operatorname{supp}(c_v^B)}c_v^B(b)f(b)\\ &=a\tau(u)+\tau(v). \end{aligned}
$$

Hence $\tau$ is linear.
Suppose that $\tau$ is a linear map from $V$ to $W$ such that $\tau(b)=f(b)$ for all $b\in B$. Let $v\in V$ and let $c_v^B\colon B\to F$ be the representation of $v$ in terms of $B$. It follows from Theorem 3.1.5 that

$$ 
\begin{align}
\tau(v) &= \tau\lowparen{\sum_{b\in \operatorname{supp}(c_v^B)}c_v^B(b)b}\\
&= \sum_{b\in \operatorname{supp}(c_v^B)}c_v^B(b)\tau(b)\\
&= \sum_{b\in \operatorname{supp}(c_v^B)}c_v^B(b)f(b).
\end{align}
$$

Therefore, the condition $\tau(b)=f(b)$ for all $b\in B$ determines the unique map $\tau\in \operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 3.1.8.** The set $\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$ is a vector space under ordinary addition of functions and scalar multiplication of functions by elements of $F$.

*Proof.* Let $u,v\in V$ and $a,b\in F$. Since 

$$ \begin{align}
(\tau + \sigma)(au+v) &= \tau(au + v) + \sigma(au + v)\\
&= (a\tau(u) + \tau(v)) + (a\sigma(u) + \sigma(v))\\
&= a(\tau(u)+\sigma(v)) + (\tau(u) + \sigma(v))\\
&= a(\tau + \sigma)(u) + (\tau + \sigma)(v)
\end{align}
$$

and 

$$ \begin{align}
(a\tau)(bu + v) &= a\tau(bu + v)\\
&= a(b\tau(u) + \tau(v))\\
&= b(a\tau(u)) + a\tau(v)\\
&= b(a\tau)(u) + (a\tau)(v),
\end{align}
$$

the two operations are closed.
Associativity and commutativity of addition follow from those in $W$. The zero map is the additive identity and $-\tau\in \operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$ defined by $(-\tau)(v) = -\tau(v)$ for all $v\in V$ is the additive inverse of $\tau\in \operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$. Hence $\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$ is an abelian group. Since scalar multiplication axioms follows from the corresponding axioms in $W$, $\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$ is a vector space.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Proposition 3.1.9.** If $\sigma \in \operatorname{Hom}(U,V)$ and $\tau\in \operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$, then the composition $\tau\sigma$ is in $\operatorname{Hom}(U,W)$.

*Proof.* Let $u,v\in U$ and $a\in F$. Then we have

$$ 
\begin{align}
(\tau\sigma)(au+v) &= \tau(\sigma(au+v))\\
&= \tau(a\sigma(u) + \sigma(v))\\
&= a\tau(\sigma(u)) + \tau(\sigma(v))\\
&= a(\tau\sigma)(u) + (\tau\sigma)(v).
\end{align}
$$

Therefore, $\tau\sigma\in \operatorname{Hom}(U,W)$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 3.1.10.** The vector space $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is an algebra, where multiplication is composition of functions. The identity operator $\operatorname{id}_V$ is the multiplicative identity.

*Proof.* The closedness of $\operatorname{End}(V)$ under multiplication follows from Proposition 3.1.9. Since composition of functions is associative, multiplication is associative. We now let $\rho,\sigma,\tau\in \operatorname{End}(V)$ and $v\in V$. Since $\rho$ is linear, 

$$ \begin{align}
(\rho(\sigma+\tau))(v) &= \rho(\sigma(v)+\tau(v))\\
&= \rho(\sigma(v)) + \rho(\tau(v))\\
&= (\rho\sigma)(v) + (\rho\tau)(v)\\
&= (\rho\sigma + \rho\tau)(v).
\end{align}
$$

Therefore $\rho(\sigma+ \tau) = \rho\sigma + \rho\tau$. Similarly, we have $(\rho + \sigma)\tau = \rho\tau + \sigma\tau$. Therefore, multiplication is distributive. By definition 1.2.1, $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is a ring under the addition and multiplication.
Furthermore, we have 

$$
\begin{align}
((a\sigma)\tau)(v) &= (a\sigma)(\tau(v))\\
&= a\sigma(\tau(v))\\
&= a(\sigma\tau)(v)\\
&= (a(\sigma\tau))(v).
\end{align}
$$

Therefore $(a\sigma)\tau = a(\sigma\tau)$. In the same way, we obtain $\sigma(a\tau) = a(\sigma\tau)$. Hence multiplication is compatible with scalar multiplication.
Consequently, $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is an algebra by Definition 2.1.7. 
Since $(\operatorname{id}\tau)(v) = \operatorname{id}(\tau(v)) = \tau(v)$ and $(\tau\operatorname{id})(v) = \tau(\operatorname{id}(v)) = \tau(v)$, the identity operator $\operatorname{id}$ is the multiplicative identity of $\operatorname{End}(V)$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>
