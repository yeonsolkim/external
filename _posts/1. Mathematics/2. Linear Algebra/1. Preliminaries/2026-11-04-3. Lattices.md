**Definition 1.3.1.** A *semilattice* is an ordered pair $(S,\vee)$ where $S$ is a set, $\vee$ is an associative, commutative, and idempotent binary operation on $S$. 

**Proposition 1.3.2.** Given a semilattice $(S,\vee)$, there is a unique order $\le$ on $S$ which makes $x\vee y$ the supremum for $\{x,y\}$ for all $x,y\in S$. 
Conversely, suppose that $S$ is a poset and any pair of two elements of $S$ has a supremum. If we write the supremum for $\{x,y\}$ as $x\vee y$, and regard $\vee$ as a binary operation on $S$, then $(S,\vee)$ is a semilattice.

*Proof.* We define a relation $\le$ on $S$ by 

$$ x\le y \iff y=x\vee y$$

for all $x,y\in S$. Then $\le$ is a order on $S$ and makes $x\vee y$ the supremum for $\{x,y\}$ for all $x,y\in S$. 
Conversely, suppose that $S$ is partially ordered by $\le$ and any pair of two elements $x$ and $y$ of $S$ has a supremum $x\vee y$. Let $(x\vee y)\vee z = \alpha$. Then $x\vee y \le \alpha$ and $z\le \alpha$, so $\alpha$ is an upper bound for $\{x,y,z\}$. Suppose $\beta$ is an upper bound for $\{x,y,z\}$. It follows that $x\vee y \le \beta$ and $z\le \beta$. Since $(x\vee y)\vee z = \alpha$, we have $\alpha\le \beta$. Therefore $\alpha$ is the supremum for $\{x,y,z\}$. Similarly, $x\vee(y\vee z)$ is the supremum for $\{x,y,z\}$, so that $x\vee(y\vee z) = \alpha$. Hence $\vee$ is associative. By definition, $\vee$ is commutative. Furthermore, since $x$ is the supremum for $\{x\}$, $\vee$ is idempotent. Therefore $(S,\vee)$ is a semilattice.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Definition 1.3.3.** A semilattice $(S,\vee)$ is called an *upper semilattice* if $x\vee y$ is interpreted as the supremum for $\{x,y\}$ for all $x,y\in S$. In this case, the operation $\vee$ is called *join*.
Replacing "supremum" with "infimum", we obtain the dual concept of a *lower semilattice*. To distinguish from the previous situation, we shall denote a lower semilattice by $(S,\wedge)$ and call $\wedge$ *meet*.

**Definition 1.3.4.** A *lattice* is an ordered pair $(L,\vee,\wedge)$ where $L$ is a set and $\vee$ and $\wedge$ are binary operations on $L$ satisfying the following: 
1. the ordered pair $(L,\vee)$ is an upper semilattice;
2. the ordered pair $(L,\wedge)$ is a lower semilattice;
3. we have for all $x,y\in L$
   
   $$ x\wedge(x\vee y) = x\quad \text{and} \quad x\vee(x\wedge y) = x.$$


**Example 1.3.5.** Given a set $S$, the ordered pair $(\mathcal{P}(S),\cup, \cap)$ is a lattice.

**Definition 1.3.6.** Let $P$ be a nonempty poset. For $x,y\in P$, we say that $y$ *covers* $x$, written 

$$x\lessdot y,$$

if $x<y$ and there is no $z\in P$ such that $x<z<y$. In this case, $x$ is called the *immediate predecessor* of $y$, and $y$ is called the *immediate successor* of $x$.
We say that $x$ and $y$ are in *covering relation* if $x\lessdot y$ or $y\lessdot x$.


**Definition 1.3.7.** Let $P$ be a nonempty poset. A *chain* $C$ in $P$ is a nonempty totally ordered subset of $P$.
If the chain $C$ is finite, then it is called a *finite chain*. The *length* of a finite chain $C$, denoted by $\ell(C)$, is the number of the pairs in the covering relation defined on $C$.

**Proposition 1.3.8.** Let $C$ be a finite chain in a poset $P$. Then we have

$$ \ell(C) = |C|-1.$$


**Definition 1.3.9.** The *length* of an interval $[x,y]$ in a poset, denoted by $\ell([x,y])$, is the supremum of the lengths of all finite chains in $[x,y]$.

**Definition 1.3.10.** A nonempty poset $P$ is said to have *finite length* if there exists a nonnegative integer $n$ such that every chain in $P$ is finite and has length at most $n$. The least such $n$ is called the *length* of $P$, denoted by $\ell(P)$.

**Example 1.3.11.** Consider the power set $\mathcal P$ of $\{x,y,z\}$, partially ordered by set inclusion. We can graphically express the covering structure of $\mathcal P$ as follows:

$$
\begin{tikzpicture}[hasse]
  \node (xyz) at (0,3) {$\{x,y,z\}$};
  \node (xy) at (-2,2) {$\{x,y\}$};
  \node (xz) at (0,2) {$\{x,z\}$};
  \node (yz) at (2,2) {$\{y,z\}$};
  \node (x) at (-2,1) {$\{x\}$};
  \node (y) at (0,1) {$\{y\}$};
  \node (z) at (2,1) {$\{z\}$};
  \node (e) at (0,0) {$\varnothing$};
  \draw (xyz) -- (xy) (xyz) -- (xz) (xyz) -- (yz)
        (xy) -- (x) (xy) -- (y) (xz) -- (x) (xz) -- (z) (yz) -- (y) (yz) -- (z)
        (x) -- (e) (y) -- (e) (z) -- (e);
\end{tikzpicture}
$$

This type of diagram is called a *Hasse diagram*.
By means of this diagram, we can easily identify the interval $I = [\varnothing, \{x,z\}]$: there are six finite chains and the length of $I$ is $2$. Furthermore, it is straightforward to see that the length of $\mathcal P$ is $3$.


Throughout the remainder of this section, we only consider lattices of finite length with the least element.

**Definition 1.3.12.** Let $L$ be a lattice with the least element $\hat 0$. A function

$$ \rho\colon L\longrightarrow\mathbb Z_{\geq 0} $$

is called a *rank function* on $L$ if it satisfies the following:

1. we have $\rho(\hat{0})=0$;
    
2. we have $\rho(y)=\rho(x)+1$ whenever $x\lessdot y$.
    
The lattice $L$ is called *graded* if it admits a rank function.

**Proposition 1.3.13.** A lattice $L$ is graded if and only if, for every $x,y\in L$ with $x\leq y$, all maximal chains in $[x,y]$ have the same length. In this case, the rank function is unique and satisfies

$$ \rho(x)=\ell([\hat0,x]) \quad \text{and} \quad \ell([x,y])=\rho(y)-\rho(x).$$

**Definition 1.3.14.** Let $L$ be a lattice. We say that $L$ is *modular* if, for all $x,y,z\in L$ with $x\leq z$,

$$ x\vee(y\wedge z)=(x\vee y)\wedge z. $$

This identity is called the *modular law*.



**Definition 1.3.15.** Let $L$ be a lattice. We say that $L$ is *upper semimodular* if, for all $x,y\in L$,

$$ x\wedge y\lessdot x \implies y\lessdot x\vee y. $$

Dually, $L$ is *lower semimodular* if

$$ y\lessdot x\vee y \implies x\wedge y\lessdot x. $$

Unless otherwise specified, a semimodular lattice means an upper semimodular lattice.

**Definition 1.3.16.** Let $L$ be a lattice and let $f\colon L\to\mathbb R$ be a function. We say that $f$ is *submodular* if

$$ f(x\vee y)+f(x\wedge y) \leq f(x)+f(y) $$

for all $x,y\in L$. We say that $f$ is *modular* if equality holds for all $x,y\in L$.

**Theorem 1.3.17.** A lattice $L$ is upper semimodular if and only if $L$ is graded and its rank function $\rho$ is submodular.

**Theorem 1.3.18.** Let $L$ be a modular lattice, and let $x,y\in L$. Then the intervals

$$ [x\wedge y,x] \quad\text{and}\quad [y,x\vee y] $$

are isomorphic as lattices.
