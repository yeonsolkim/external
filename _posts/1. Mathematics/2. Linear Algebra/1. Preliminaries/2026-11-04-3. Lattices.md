**Semilattices and lattices.** In a partially ordered set, suprema and infima, when they exist, provide natural ways of combining elements. These constructions can also be studied algebraically: the corresponding binary operations determine, and are determined by, the underlying order. We begin by considering these operations separately and then examine structures in which both are present.

**Definition 1.3.1.** A *semilattice* is an ordered pair $(S,\vee)$ where $S$ is a set, $\vee$ is an associative, commutative, and idempotent binary operation on $S$. 

**Proposition 1.3.2.** Given a semilattice $(S,\vee)$, there is a unique order $\le$ on $S$ which makes $x\vee y$ the supremum for $\{x,y\}$ for all $x,y\in S$. 
Conversely, suppose that $S$ is a poset and any pair of two elements of $S$ has a supremum. If we write the supremum for $\{x,y\}$ as $x\vee y$, and regard $\vee$ as a binary operation on $S$, then $(S,\vee)$ is a semilattice.

*Proof.* We define a relation $\le$ on $S$ by 

$$ x\le y \iff y=x\vee y$$

for all $x,y\in S$. Then $\le$ is an order on $S$ and makes $x\vee y$ the supremum for $\{x,y\}$ for all $x,y\in S$. 
Conversely, suppose that $S$ is partially ordered by $\le$ and any pair of two elements $x$ and $y$ of $S$ has a supremum $x\vee y$. Let $(x\vee y)\vee z = \alpha$. Then $x\vee y \le \alpha$ and $z\le \alpha$, so $\alpha$ is an upper bound for $\{x,y,z\}$. Suppose $\beta$ is an upper bound for $\{x,y,z\}$. It follows that $x\vee y \le \beta$ and $z\le \beta$. Since $(x\vee y)\vee z = \alpha$, we have $\alpha\le \beta$. Therefore $\alpha$ is the supremum for $\{x,y,z\}$. Similarly, $x\vee(y\vee z)$ is the supremum for $\{x,y,z\}$, so that $x\vee(y\vee z) = \alpha$. Hence $\vee$ is associative. By definition, $\vee$ is commutative. Furthermore, since $x$ is the supremum for $\{x\}$, $\vee$ is idempotent. Therefore $(S,\vee)$ is a semilattice.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Definition 1.3.3.** A semilattice $(S,\vee)$ is called an *upper semilattice* if $x\vee y$ is interpreted as the supremum for $\{x,y\}$ for all $x,y\in S$. In this case, the operation $\vee$ is called *join*.
Replacing "supremum" with "infimum", we obtain the dual concept of a *lower semilattice*. To distinguish from the previous situation, we shall denote a lower semilattice by $(S,\wedge)$ and call $\wedge$ *meet*.

**Definition 1.3.4.** A *lattice* is an ordered triple $(L,\vee,\wedge)$ where $L$ is a set and $\vee$ and $\wedge$ are binary operations on $L$ satisfying the following: 
1. the pair $(L,\vee)$ is an upper semilattice;
2. the pair $(L,\wedge)$ is a lower semilattice;
3. we have for all $x,y\in L$
   
   $$ x\wedge(x\vee y) = x\quad \text{and} \quad x\vee(x\wedge y) = x.$$


**Example 1.3.5.** Given a set $S$, the pair $(\mathcal{P}(S),\cup, \cap)$ is a lattice.

In a lattice, the join and meet operations each determine an order relation. The third axiom in Definition 1.3.4 ensures that these two descriptions agree, as the following proposition shows.

**Proposition 1.3.6.** Let $L$ be a lattice. Then, for all $x,y\in L$,

$$ x\vee y=y \iff x\le y\iff x\wedge y=x.$$



An order-preserving map between lattices need not preserve joins or meets. Nevertheless, the extremal properties of these operations impose certain inequalities that every such map must satisfy.

**Theorem 1.3.7.** Let $L$ and $M$ be lattices, and let $f\colon L\to M$ be a order-preserving map. Then, for all $x,y\in L$,

$$ f(x\wedge y) \le f(x)\wedge f(y)\quad \text{and} \quad f(x\vee y)\ge f(x)\vee f(y).$$

*Proof.* Since $x\wedge y\le x$ and $x\wedge y\le y$, monotonicity yields $f(x\wedge y)\le f(x)$ and $f(x\wedge y)\le f(y)$. Hence $f(x\wedge y)$ is a lower bound for $f(x)$ and $f(y)$. It follows from the definition of infimum that $f(x\wedge y) \le f(x)\wedge f(y)$. The second inequality follows from duality.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

The *distributive laws* need not hold in an arbitrary lattice. Nevertheless, one direction of each distributive identity always holds as an inequality, without any additional assumptions. 

**Corollary 1.3.8.** Let $L$ be a lattice. For all $x,y,z\in L$,
   
   $$ x\vee(y\wedge z) \leq (x\vee y)\wedge(x\vee z) \quad \text{and} \quad x\wedge(y\vee z)\ge (x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z). $$
   
*Proof.* Fix $x\in L$ and define 

$$ f_x\colon L\to L,\qquad f_x(t) = x\vee t.$$

Suppose $y,z\in L$ and $y\le z$. Since $x\vee z\ge x$ and $x\vee z\ge z \ge y$, $x\vee z$ is an upper bound for $x$ and $y$. By the definition of supremum, $x\vee y\le x\vee z$, so that $f_x(y)\le f_x(z)$. Thus $f_x$ is order-preserving. Theorem 1.3.7 now gives the first inequality. The second inequality follows from duality.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

In a distributive lattice, the inequalities in Corollary 1.3.8 become equalities. A weaker requirement is to ask for equality in the first inequality whenever $x\le z$, in which case $x\vee z=z$. This conditional form of distributivity leads to the notion of a modular lattice.

**Definition 1.3.9.** A lattice $L$ is called *modular* if, for all $x,y,z\in L$

$$ x\le z\implies x\vee(y\wedge z)=(x\vee y)\wedge z. $$

This identity is called the *modular law*.

**Covering relation.** One way to examine the structure of lattices is to consider the immediate steps between comparable elements, with no intermediate elements. This leads to the covering relation, which provides the basis for the notions of chain, length, and rank developed below.

**Definition 1.3.10.** Let $P$ be a poset. For $x,y\in P$, we say that $y$ *covers* $x$, written 

$$x\lessdot y,$$

if $x<y$ and there is no $z\in P$ such that $x<z<y$. In this case, $x$ is called the *immediate predecessor* of $y$, and $y$ is called the *immediate successor* of $x$.
We say that $x$ and $y$ are in *covering relation* if $x\lessdot y$ or $y\lessdot x$.


**Definition 1.3.11.** A *chain* $C$ in a poset $P$ is a nonempty totally ordered subset of $P$.
If $C$ is finite, then it is called a *finite chain*. The *length* of the finite chain $C$, denoted by $\ell(C)$, is the number of the pairs in the covering relation defined on $C$.

**Proposition 1.3.12.** Let $C$ be a finite chain in a poset $P$. Then we have

$$ \ell(C) = |C|-1.$$


**Definition 1.3.13.** The *length* of an interval $[x,y]$ in a poset, denoted by $\ell([x,y])$, is the supremum of the lengths of all finite chains in $[x,y]$.

**Definition 1.3.14.** A nonempty poset $P$ is said to have *finite length* if there exists a nonnegative integer $n$ such that every chain in $P$ is finite and has length at most $n$. The least such $n$ is called the *length* of $P$, denoted by $\ell(P)$. 
In particular, the length of a finite-length poset $P$ is the maximum of lengths of all chains in $P$.

**Example 1.3.15.** The power set $\mathcal P$ of $\{x,y,z\}$ partially ordered by set inclusion is a lattice of length $3$. We can graphically express the covering structure of $\mathcal P$ as follows:

$$
\begin{tikzpicture}[hasse]
  \node (xyz) at (0,3) {$\{x,y,z\}$};
  \node (xy) at (-2,2) {$\{x,y\}$};
  \node (xz) at (0,2) {$\{x,z\}$};
  \node (yz) at (2,2) {$\{y,z\}$};
  \node (x) at (-2,1) {$\{x\}$};
  \node (y) at (0,1) {$\{y\}$};
  \node (z) at (2,1) {$\{z\}$};
  \node (e) at (0,0) {$\varnothing$};
  \draw (xyz) -- (xy) (xyz) -- (xz) (xyz) -- (yz)
        (xy) -- (x) (xy) -- (y) (xz) -- (x) (xz) -- (z) (yz) -- (y) (yz) -- (z)
        (x) -- (e) (y) -- (e) (z) -- (e);
\end{tikzpicture}
$$

This type of diagram is called a *Hasse diagram*.


Throughout the remainder of this section, we only consider finite-length lattices with the least element.

**Definition 1.3.16.** Let $L$ be a lattice with the least element $\hat 0$. A function

$$ \rho\colon L\longrightarrow\mathbb Z_{\geq 0} $$

is called a *rank function* on $L$ if it satisfies the following:

1. we have $\rho(\hat{0})=0$;
2. we have $\rho(y)=\rho(x)+1$ whenever $x\lessdot y$.
    
A lattice is called *graded* if it admits a rank function.

**Theorem 1.3.17.** A lattice $L$ is graded if and only if all maximal chains in any interval in $L$ have the same length. In this case, the rank function is unique and satisfies

$$ \rho(x)=\ell([\hat0,x]) \quad \text{and} \quad \ell([x,y])=\rho(y)-\rho(x)$$

for every $x,y\in L$ with $x\leq y$.

*Proof.* Suppose $L$ is graded and $\rho\colon L\to Z_{\ge 0}$ is a rank function on $L$. Let $I=[a,b]$ be any interval in $L$ and $C$ be a maximal chain in $I$. Then $a$ and $b$ are the minimum and maximum of $C$, respectively. Furthermore, since $C$ has the form 

$$ a = x_0\lessdot x_1\lessdot\cdots\lessdot x_{\ell(C)}=b,$$

we have $\ell(C) = \rho(b)-\rho(a)$. It follows that

$$ \ell([a,b]) = \ell(C) = \rho(b)-\rho(a).$$

Hence all maximal chains in the interval $I$ have the same length. Furthermore, we have 

$$ \rho(x) = \ell([\hat0,x]) + \rho(\hat0) = \ell([\hat0,x])$$

for all $x\in L$, which implies that a rank function is unique.
Conversely, suppose that all maximal chains in any interval in $L$ have the same length. Define $\rho(\hat0) = 0$. For each interval $[\hat0,x]$ in $L$, define $\rho(x) = \ell([\hat0, x])$. Let $x,y\in L$ with $x\lessdot y$ and let $C$ be a maximal chain in $[\hat0,x]$. Then, $C'=C\mathbin{\dot{\cup}} \{y\}$ is a maximal chain in $[\hat0,y]$. It follows from Proposition 1.3.12 that 

$$ 
\begin{align}
\ell([\hat0,y]) &= \ell(C')\\
&= |C'|-1\\
&= |C|\\
&= \ell(C) + 1 \\
&= \ell([\hat0, x]) + 1.
\end{align}
$$

Therefore 

$$
\rho(y) = \rho(x) + (\ell([\hat0,y]) -\ell([\hat0,x])) = \rho(x) + 1.
$$

Hence $\rho$ is the rank function and $L$ is graded. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Semimodularity and submodularity.** 

**Definition 1.3.18.** A lattice $L$ is called *upper semimodular* if, for all $x,y\in L$,

$$ x\wedge y\lessdot x \implies y\lessdot x\vee y. $$

Dually, $L$ is called *lower semimodular* if, for all $x,y\in L$,

$$ y\lessdot x\vee y \implies x\wedge y\lessdot x. $$


**Definition 1.3.19.** Let $L$ be a lattice and let $f\colon L\to\mathbb R$ be a function. We say that $f$ is *submodular* if, for all $x,y\in L$,

$$ f(x\vee y)+f(x\wedge y) \leq f(x)+f(y) $$

Dually, $f$ is called *supermodular* if, for all $x,y\in L$,

$$ f(x\vee y)+f(x\wedge y) \ge f(x)+f(y) $$

We say that $f$ is *modular* if it is both submodular and supermodular.

**Theorem 1.3.20.** A lattice $L$ is upper semimodular if and only if $L$ is graded and its rank function is submodular.

*Proof.* Suppose that $L$ is upper semimodular. We first prove that $L$ is graded. Let $I=[a,b]$ be an interval in $L$. We show, by induction on $\ell(I)$, that all maximal chains in $I$ have the same length. The assertion is immediate if $\ell(I)\le 1$. 
Suppose that $\ell(I)\ge 2$ and that the assertion holds for every interval of smaller length. Let $C$ and $D$ be maximal chains in $I$, and let $u$ and $v$ be their respective immediate successors of $a$. Thus $a\lessdot u$ and $a\lessdot v$. If $u=v$, the induction hypothesis applied to $[u,b]$ gives $\ell(C)=\ell(D)$. Suppose instead that $u\ne v$. Since both $u$ and $v$ cover $a$, we have $u\wedge v=a$. By upper semimodularity, $u\lessdot u\vee v$ and $v\lessdot u\vee v$. Put $w=u\vee v$. Since $w\le b$, the intervals $[u,b]$, $[v,b]$, and $[w,b]$ all have length smaller than $\ell(I)$. By the induction hypothesis, all maximal chains in each of these intervals have the same length. Consequently,

$$ \ell(C)=1+\ell([u,b]) =2+\ell([w,b]) $$

and, similarly,

$$ \ell(D)=1+\ell([v,b]) =2+\ell([w,b]). $$

Hence $\ell(C)=\ell(D)$. By induction, all maximal chains in every interval of $L$ have the same length. It follows from Theorem 1.3.17 that $L$ is graded.
We now prove that its rank function $\rho$ is submodular. First, observe that if $u\lessdot v$, then, for any $z\in L$,

$$ u\vee z=v\vee z \quad\text{or}\quad u\vee z\lessdot v\vee z. $$

Indeed, since

$$ u\le v\wedge(u\vee z)\le v, $$

we have either $v\wedge(u\vee z)=v$ or $v\wedge(u\vee z)=u$. In the first case, $v\le u\vee z$, so that $u\vee z=v\vee z$. In the second case, upper semimodularity gives

$$ u\vee z\lessdot v\vee(u\vee z)=v\vee z. $$

Now let $x,y\in L$, and choose a maximal chain in $[x\wedge y,x]$, say

$$ x\wedge y=x_0\lessdot x_1 \lessdot\cdots\lessdot x_k=x. $$

Since $L$ is graded, we have

$$ k=\rho(x)-\rho(x\wedge y). $$

For each $i=0,\ldots,k$, put $y_i=x_i\vee y$. The preceding observation shows that, for every $i=1,\ldots,k$, either $y_{i-1}=y_i$ or $y_{i-1}\lessdot y_i$. Consequently,

$$ \rho(y_i)-\rho(y_{i-1})\le 1. $$

Since $y_0=y$ and $y_k=x\vee y$, it follows that

$$ \begin{aligned} \rho(x\vee y)-\rho(y) &=\sum_{i=1}^{k} \bigl(\rho(y_i)-\rho(y_{i-1})\bigr)\\ &\le k\\ &=\rho(x)-\rho(x\wedge y). \end{aligned} $$

Therefore,

$$ \rho(x\vee y)+\rho(x\wedge y) \le \rho(x)+\rho(y). $$

Hence $\rho$ is submodular.
Conversely, suppose that $L$ is graded and its rank function $\rho$ is submodular. Let $x,y\in L$ satisfy $x\wedge y\lessdot x$. Then $\rho(x)=\rho(x\wedge y)+1$. By submodularity,

$$ \begin{aligned} \rho(x\vee y) &\le \rho(x)+\rho(y)-\rho(x\wedge y)\\ &=\rho(y)+1. \end{aligned} $$

Furthermore, $x\not\le y$, since otherwise $x\wedge y=x$, contradicting $x\wedge y\lessdot x$. Thus $y<x\vee y$, and Theorem 1.3.17 gives

$$ \rho(y)<\rho(x\vee y)\le\rho(y)+1. $$

Since the rank function takes integer values, we conclude that $\rho(x\vee y)=\rho(y)+1$. By Theorem 1.3.17, the interval $[y,x\vee y]$ has length $1$. Therefore $y\lessdot x\vee y$, proving that $L$ is upper semimodular. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 1.3.21.** Let $L$ be a modular lattice, and let $x,y\in L$. Then the intervals

$$ [x\wedge y,x] \quad\text{and}\quad [y,x\vee y] $$

are isomorphic as lattices.

**Theorem 1.3.22.** Let $L$ be a lattice. Then the following are equivalent:

1. the lattice $L$ is modular;
2. the lattice $L$ is both upper semimodular and lower semimodular;
3. the lattice $L$ is graded, and its rank function $\rho$ is modular.
