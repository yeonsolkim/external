**Definition 1.2.1.** A *ring* is an ordered pair $(R,+,\times)$ where $R$ is a set, $+$ is a binary operation called *addition*, and $\times$ is a binary operation called *multiplication* satisfying the following axioms:
1. the ordered pair $(R,+)$ is an abelian group;
2. multiplication is associative;
3. multiplication is *distributive* with respect to addition, that is, for all $x,y,z\in R$, the following hold:
   - $x\times (y+z) = (x\times y) + (x\times z)$ (*left-distributive*);
   - $(x+y)\times z = (x\times z) + (y\times z)$ (*right-distributive*).

We shall denote multiplication by juxtaposition, the additive identity of $R$ by $0$, and the additive inverse of a ring element $x$ by $-x$.

The distributive laws express that multiplication is *biadditive*: left multiplication and right multiplication are both additive. More precisely, for each fixed $a\in R$, the maps $x\mapsto ax$ and $x\mapsto xa$ are homomorphisms of the additive group $(R,+)$ to itself.

**Definition 1.2.2.** A ring $R$ is said to be *commutative* if multiplication is commutative. A ring $R$ is said to have a *multiplicative identity* if there is an element $1\in R$ such that $1x = x1 = x$ for all $x\in R$.

**Proposition 1.2.3.** Let $R$ be a ring.
1. We have $0x=x0=0$ for all $x\in R$.
2. We have $(-x)y=x(-y)=-(xy)$ for all $x,y\in R$.
3. We have $(-x)(-y) = xy$ for all $x,y\in R$.
4. If $R$ has a multiplicative identity $1$, then the identity is unique and $-x = (-1)x$ for all $x\in R$.

*Proof.* (1) Since $x+0 = x$, right distributivity gives $xy = 0y +xy$ for all $y\in R$. Hence $0y=0$ for all $y\in R$. Similarly, left distributivity gives $yx = y0 + yx$, so that $y0=0$ for all $y\in R$.
(2) Since we have $x+(-x)=0$, right distributivity gives $xy + (-x)y = 0y$. Since $0y=0$ by (1), $xy + (-x)y = 0$. Therefore $(-x)y = -(xy)$. Similarly, left distributivity gives and (1) gives $yx + y(-x) = 0$. Thus $y(-x) = -(yx)$.
(3) Since $(-x)(-y) =  -(x(-y))$ and $x(-y) = -(xy)$ by (2), we have $(-x)(-y) = -(-(xy))$. Since $-(-x) = x$, $(-x)(-y) = xy$.
(4) Let $1$ and $1'$ be multiplicative identities. Then $1 = 1\times 1' = 1'$. Hence a multiplicative identity is unique. Since

$$x + (-1)x = 1x + (-1)x =(1+(-1))x=0x=0,$$

we have $(-1)x = -x$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Definition 1.2.4.** A ring $R$ with multiplicative identity $1\ne 0$ is called a *division ring* if every nonzero element $x\in R$ has a *multiplicative inverse*, that is, there exists $y\in R$ such that $xy = yx = 1$. A commutative division ring is called a *field*.

**Definition 1.2.5.** A *subring* of a ring $R$ is a subgroup of $R$ that is closed under multiplication. 

**Proposition 1.2.6.** A subset $S$ of a ring $R$ is a subring of $R$ if and only if 
1. the subset $S$ is nonempty;
2. the subset $S$ is closed under subtraction;
3. the subset $S$ is closed under multiplication.

*Proof.* Suppose that $S$ is a subring of $R$. By Proposition 1.1.11, $S$ is nonempty and closed under subtraction. By Definition 1.2.5, $S$ is closed under multiplication.
We now prove the converse. Since $S$ is nonempty and is closed under subtraction, $S$ is a subgroup of $R$ by Proposition 1.1.11. Since $S$ is closed under multiplication, $S$ is a subring of $R$ by Definition 1.2.5.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>
