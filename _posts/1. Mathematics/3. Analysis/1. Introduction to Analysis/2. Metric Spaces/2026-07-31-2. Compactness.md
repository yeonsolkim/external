We now consider how a collection of open sets can cover a set $A$ as a whole. Such a collection may contain infinitely many sets, and this raises a natural question. Can finitely many members of the collection already cover $A$? Compactness formalizes the sets for which the answer is always yes.

**Definition 2.2.1.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M.$ A collection $\lbrace U_{i}\rbrace_{i \in I}$ of subsets of $M$ is a *cover* of $A$ if

$$
A\subseteq\bigcup_{i \in I}U_{i}.
$$

A cover of $A$ whose elements are open sets is called an *open cover* of $A$.
A subcollection of a cover of $A$ that still covers $A$ is called a *subcover* of $A$, and a subcover of $A$ whose index set is finite is called a *finite subcover* of $A$.

&emsp; Since openness depends on the ambient space, it is natural to ask whether compactness does as well. We therefore keep track of the ambient space in the initial definition and then show that this apparent dependence disappears.

**Definition 2.2.2.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq E\subseteq M.$ The set $A$ is *compact relative to* $E$ if every cover of $A$ whose elements are open relative to $E$ has a finite subcover of $A$.

**Theorem 2.2.3.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq E\subseteq M.$ Then, $A$ is compact relative to $M$ if and only if $A$ is compact relative to $E.$

*Proof.* Suppose that $A$ is compact relative to $M.$ Let $\lbrace U_{i}\rbrace_{i \in I}$ be a cover of $A$ whose elements are open relative to $E$. By Theorem 2.1.20, there is an open set $V_i$ such that $V_i \cap E=U_i$ for each $i\in I$; and since $A$ is compact relative to $M,$ we have

$$ A\subseteq \bigcup_{k=1}^{n}V_{i_k}\tag{$\ast$}$$

for some finitely many indices $i_1,\dots,i_n\in I.$ Since $A\subseteq E,$ $(\ast)$ implies

$$ A\subseteq \bigcup_{k=1}^{n}U_{i_k}.\tag{$\ast\ast$}$$

Therefore $A$ is compact relative to $E.$
Conversely, suppose that $A$ is compact relative to $E.$ Let $\lbrace V_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ be a collection of open sets which covers $A,$ and let $U_i=V_i\cap E$ for each $i\in I.$ Then $U_i$ is open relative to $E$ for each $i\in I,$ and $\lbrace U_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ covers $A.$ Since $A$ is compact relative to $E,$ $(\ast\ast)$ holds for some finitely many indices $i_1,\dots,i_n\in I.$ Since $U_i\subseteq V_i$ for each $i\in I,$ $(\ast\ast)$ implies $(\ast).$ Therefore $A$ is compact relative to $M.$<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

&emsp; Thus compactness is intrinsic to the space $A$: it does not depend on whether openness is measured in $M$ or in an intermediate subspace $E$. We may therefore drop the reference to the ambient space.

**Definition 2.2.4.** A metric space $M$ is *compact* if it is compact relative to $M$. Equivalently, $M$ is compact if every relative open cover of $M$ has a finite subcover.

We now look into some relationship between compactness and closedness.

**Theorem 2.2.5.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M.$ If $A$ is compact then $A$ is closed.

*Proof.* Suppose $x\in A^c$. For $y\in A$, since $x\ne y$, we have $d(x,y)>0$. Let

$$0< r_y < \frac 1 2 d(x,y).$$

Then the collection $\lbrace B_{r_y} : y\in A\rbrace$ is an open cover of $A$. Since $A$ is compact, there are finitely many points $y_1,\dots,y_n\in A$ such that

$$ A \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^n B_{r_{y_i}}(y_i) = E.$$

Since $r_{y}<\frac 1 2 d(x,y)$, we have $B_{r_{y_i}}(y_i) \cap B_{r_{y_i}}(x) = \varnothing$ for each $i=1,\dots,n$. Therefore, if

$$r = \min \lbrace r_{y_1},\dots,r_{y_n}\rbrace,$$

then $B_r(x)$ does not intersect $E$. Since $A\subseteq E$, we have $A \cap B_r(x)=\varnothing.$ It follows that $B_r(x)\subseteq A^c$, and thus $A^c$ is open. By Theorem 2.1.14, $A$ is closed.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Remark 2.2.6.** The usefulness of compactness was clearly demonstrated in the proof above. For each point $x\in A$, we chose an open neighborhood $U_x$ on which a desired property holds. The resulting neighborhoods covered $A$, but there might be infinitely many of them, and the corresponding local choices need not combine into a single global conclusion of $A$. It was compactness that removed this difficulty. Once only finitely many local choices have remained, we could take their minimum, maximum, or a common bound and thereby pass from local to global control of the set.

**Theorem 2.2.7.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M.$ If $A$ is compact, then every closed subset of $A$ is compact.

*Proof.* Let $E$ be a closed subset of $A$, and $\lbrace U_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ be an open cover of $E$. Let $\mathcal U = \lbrace U_i\rbrace_{i\in I} \cup \lbrace E^c\rbrace$. Since each $U_i$ and $E^c$ are open, $\mathcal U$ is an open cover of $A$. Since $A$ is compact, there is a finite subcollection $\mathcal V$ of $\mathcal U$ which covers $A$. Then $\mathcal V$ covers $E$. If $E^c$ is a member of $\mathcal V$, we may just need to remove it from $\mathcal V$, and $\mathcal V \setminus \lbrace E^c\rbrace$ still an open cover of $E$. Hence $E$ is compact.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Corollary 2.2.8.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A,E\subseteq M.$ If $A$ is compact and $E$ is closed, then $A\cap E$ is compact.

*Proof.* Since $A$ is closed by Theorem 2.2.5 and $E$ is closed, $A\cap E$ is closed by (2) of Theorem 2.1.15. Since $A\cap E$ is a closed subset of $A$, Theorem 2.2.7 shows that $A\cap E$ is compact.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

The finite-subcover condition has a dual formulation in terms of closed sets. To state it explicitly, we introduce the finite intersection property.

**Definition 2.2.9.** A collection $\{A_i\}_{i\in I}$ is said to have the *finite intersection property* if every finite subcollection of $\{A_i\}_{i\in I}$ has nonempty intersection. 

**Theorem 2.2.10.** A metric space $M$ is compact if and only if every collection of relative closed subsets of $M$ satisfying the FIP has nonempty intersection.

*Proof.* Suppose that $M$ is compact, and let $\lbrace A_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ be a collection of relative closed subsets of $M$ satisfying the FIP. Let $E_i$ be the relative complement of $A_i$ in $M$. If $\bigcap_{i\in I} A_i = \varnothing,$ then $\lbrace E_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ is an relative open cover of $M$. Since $M$ is compact, $M = \bigcup_{k=1}^n E_{i_k}$ for some finitely many indices $i_1,\dots,i_n$. It follows that $\bigcap_{k=1}^n A_{i_k} = \varnothing$, which contradicts the FIP. Hence $\bigcap_{i\in I} A_i \ne \varnothing$.
Conversely, suppose that every collection of relative closed subsets of $M$ satisfying the FIP has nonempty intersection. Let $\lbrace U_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ be an open cover of $M$, and put $E_i = M\setminus U_i$. Then every $E_i$ is closed relative to $M$, and $\bigcap_{i\in I} E_i =\varnothing$. Consequently, $\lbrace E_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ cannot have the finite intersection property. Hence there exists a finite subcollection $\lbrace E_{i_k}\rbrace_{k=1}^{n}$ whose intersection is empty. Taking complements yields $M \subseteq \bigcup_{k=1}^n U_{i_k}$. Therefore $M$ is compact.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

&emsp; The preceding theorem states that a collection of closed subsets of a compact space has a common point whenever it satisfies the FIP. The compactness of the entire ambient space, however, is stronger than necessary: it suffices for one member of the collection to be compact.

**Corollary 2.2.11.** Let $M$ be a metric space. If $\{A_i\}_{i\in I}$ is a collection of closed subsets of $M$ satisfying the FIP, and $A_{i_0}$ is compact for some $i_0\in I$, then

$$
\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i\neq\varnothing.
$$

*Proof.* Define $E_i=A_i\cap A_{i_0}$ for each $i\in I$. Then, we have 

$$ \bigcap_{k=1}^nE_{i_k} = A_{i_1}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_n} \cap A_{i_0}$$ 

for any finitely many indices $i_1,\dots,i_n\in I$. Since $\{A_i\}_{i\in I}$ has the FIP, the right-hand side is nonempty. Therefore $\{E_i\}_{i\in I}$ has the FIP. Since $A_{i_0}$ is compact and $E_i$ is closed in $A_{i_0}$, Theorem 2.2.10 now gives $\bigcap_{i\in I}E_i\neq\varnothing$. Since

$$ \bigcap_{i\in I}E_i = \lowparen{\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i}\cap A_{i_0} =\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i,$$

we finally have $\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i \ne \varnothing$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Corollary 2.2.12.** Let $M$ be a metric space. If $\lbrace a_n\rbrace$ is a sequence of nonempty compact subsets of $M$ such that $a_{n+1}\subseteq a_n$ for $n=1,2,3,\dots$ , then

$$ \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \ne \varnothing.$$

*Proof.* Let $\lbrace a_n\rbrace_{n\in I}$ be a finite subcollection of $\lbrace a_n\rbrace$, where $n_1,\dots,n_k\in I$. Since $a_{n+1}\subseteq a_n$, we have 

$$ \bigcap_{n\in I} a_n = a_{n_m}$$

where $n_m$ is the greatest element of $I$. Since $a_m$ is nonempty, $\bigcap_{n\in I}$ is also nonempty. Consequently, $\lbrace a_n\rbrace$ has the FIP. Then, Corollary 2.2.12 immediately shows that $\lbrace a_n\rbrace$ has nonempty intersection.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Compactness prevents an infinite subset from remaining locally discrete: infinitely many points confined to a compact set must accumulate somewhere in that set.

**Theorem 2.2.13.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A$ be a compact subset of $M$. If $E$ is an infinite subset of $A$, then $E$ has a limit point in $A$.

*Proof.* Suppose that no point of $A$ is a limit point of $E$. For each $x\in A$, we have $B_r^{\ast}(x)\cap E = \varnothing$ for some $r>0$. Then $B_r(x)$ contains at most one point of $E$. Thus no finite subcollection of $\lbrace B_{r_x}(x) : x\in A\rbrace$ can cover $E$; the same is true of $A$, since $E\subseteq A$. However, since $\lbrace B_{r_x}(x) : x\in A\rbrace$ is an open cover of $A$, the compactness of $A$ guarantees the existence of a finite subcover of $A$; this is a contradiction.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>
