We now consider how a collection of open sets can describe a set $A$ as a whole: namely, by covering every point of $A$. Such a collection may contain infinitely many sets, and this raises a natural question. Can finitely many members of the collection already cover $A$? Compactness formalizes the sets for which the answer is always yes.

**Definition 2.2.1.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M.$ A collection $\lbrace U_{i}\rbrace_{i \in I}$ of subsets of $M$ is a *cover* of $A$ if

$$
A\subseteq\bigcup_{i \in I}U_{i}.
$$

A cover of $A$ whose elements are open sets is called an *open cover* of $A$.
A subcollection of a cover of $A$ that still covers $A$ is called a *subcover* of $A$, and a subcover of $A$ whose index set is finite is called a *finite subcover* of $A$.

&emsp; Since openness depends on the ambient space, it is natural to ask whether compactness does as well. We therefore keep track of the ambient space in the initial definition and then show that this apparent dependence disappears.

**Definition 2.2.2.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq E\subseteq M.$ The set $A$ is *compact relative to* $E$ if every cover of $A$ whose elements are open relative to $E$ has a finite subcover of $A$.

**Theorem 2.2.3.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq E\subseteq M.$ Then, $A$ is compact relative to $M$ if and only if $A$ is compact relative to $E.$

*Proof.* Suppose that $A$ is compact relative to $M.$ Let $\lbrace U_{i}\rbrace_{i \in I}$ be a cover of $A$ whose elements are open relative to $E$. By Theorem 2.1.20, there is an open set $V_i$ such that $V_i \cap E=U_i$ for each $i\in I$; and since $A$ is compact relative to $M,$ we have

$$ A\subseteq \bigcup_{k=1}^{n}V_{i_k}\tag{$\ast$}$$

for some finitely many indices $i_1,\dots,i_n\in I.$ Since $A\subseteq E,$ $(\ast)$ implies

$$ A\subseteq \bigcup_{k=1}^{n}U_{i_k}.\tag{$\ast\ast$}$$

Therefore $A$ is compact relative to $E.$
Conversely, suppose that $A$ is compact relative to $E.$ Let $\lbrace V_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ be a collection of open sets which covers $A,$ and let $U_i=V_i\cap E$ for each $i\in I.$ Then $U_i$ is open relative to $E$ for each $i\in I,$ and $\lbrace U_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ covers $A.$ Since $A$ is compact relative to $E,$ $(\ast\ast)$ holds for some finitely many indices $i_1,\dots,i_n\in I.$ Since $U_i\subseteq V_i$ for each $i\in I,$ $(\ast\ast)$ implies $(\ast).$ Therefore $A$ is compact relative to $M.$<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

&emsp; Thus compactness is intrinsic to the set $A$: it does not depend on whether openness is measured in $M$ or in an intermediate subspace $E$. We may therefore drop the reference to the ambient space.

**Definition 2.2.4.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M.$ The set $A$ is *compact* if it is compact relative to $M.$ Equivalently, $A$ is compact if every open cover of $A$ has a finite subcover of $A$.

We now look into some relationship between compactness and closedness.

**Theorem 2.2.5.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M.$ If $A$ is compact then $A$ is closed.

*Proof.* Suppose $x\in A^c$. For $y\in A$, since $x\ne y$, we have $d(x,y)>0$. Let

$$0< r_y < \frac 1 2 d(x,y).$$

Then the collection $\lbrace B_{r_y} : y\in A\rbrace$ is an open cover of $A$. Since $A$ is compact, there are finitely many points $y_1,\dots,y_n\in A$ such that

$$ A \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^n B_{r_{y_i}}(y_i) = E.$$

Since $r_{y}<\frac 1 2 d(x,y)$, we have $B_{r_{y_i}}(y_i) \cap B_{r_{y_i}}(x) = \varnothing$ for each $i=1,\dots,n$. Therefore, if

$$r = \min \lbrace r_{y_1},\dots,r_{y_n}\rbrace,$$

then $B_r(x)$ does not intersect $E$. Since $A\subseteq E$, we have $A \cap B_r(x)=\varnothing.$ It follows that $B_r(x)\subseteq A^c$, and thus $A^c$ is open. By Theorem 2.1.14, $A$ is closed.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Remark 2.2.6.** The usefulness of compactness is clearly demonstrated in the proof above. For each point $x\in A$, we choose an open neighborhood $U_x$ on which a desired property holds. The resulting neighborhoods cover $A$, but there may be infinitely many of them, and the corresponding local choices need not combine into a single global conclusion. It is compactness that removes this difficulty. Once only finitely many local choices remain, we can take their minimum, maximum, or a common bound and thereby pass from local control to global control.

**Theorem 2.2.7.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M.$ If $A$ is compact, then every closed subset of $A$ is compact.

*Proof.* Let $E$ be a closed subset of $A$, and $\lbrace U_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ be an open cover of $E$. Let $\mathcal U = \lbrace U_i\rbrace_{i\in I} \cup \lbrace E^c\rbrace$. Since each $U_i$ and $E^c$ are open, $\mathcal U$ is an open cover of $A$. Since $A$ is compact, there is a finite subcollection $\mathcal V$ of $\mathcal U$ which covers $A$. Then $\mathcal V$ covers $E$. If $E^c$ is a member of $\mathcal V$, we may just need to remove it from $\mathcal V$, and $\mathcal V \setminus \lbrace E^c\rbrace$ still an open cover of $E$. Hence $E$ is compact.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Corollary 2.2.8.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A,E\subseteq M.$ If $A$ is compact and $E$ is closed, then $A\cap E$ is compact.

*Proof.* Since $A$ is closed by Theorem 2.2.5 and $E$ is closed, $A\cap E$ is closed by (2) of Theorem 2.1.15. Since $A\cap E$ is a closed subset of $A$, Theorem 2.2.7 shows that $A\cap E$ is compact.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 2.2.9.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space. If $\lbrace A_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ is a collection of compact subsets of $M$ and each intersection of every finite subcollection of $\lbrace A_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ is nonempty, then the intersection of $\lbrace A_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ is nonempty.

*Proof.* Let $A_1\in \lbrace A_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$, and suppose, for contradiction, that the intersection of $\lbrace A_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ is empty. Then, there are no points of $A_1$ that belongs to every $A_i$. Thus there exists $i\in I$ such that $x\in A^{c}_{i}$. Hence the union of the sets $A^{c}_{i}$ is an open cover of $A_1$. Since $A_1$ is compact, there are finitely many indices $i_1,\dots,i_n\in I$ such that

$$ A_1\subseteq A^{c}_{i_1} \cup \cdots \cup A^{c}_{i_n}.$$

It follows that

$$ A_1\cap A^{c}_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap A^{c}_{i_n} = \varnothing,$$

which contradicts our premise.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Corollary 2.2.10.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space. If $\lbrace a_n\rbrace$ is a sequence of nonempty compact subsets of $M$ such that $a_{n+1}\subseteq a_n$ for $n=1,2,3,\dots$ , then

$$ \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \ne \varnothing.$$

**Theorem 2.2.11.** Let $(M,d)$ be a metric space and let $A$ be a compact subset of $M$. If $E$ is an infinite subset of $A$, then $E$ has a limit point in $A$.

*Proof.* Suppose that no point of $A$ is a limit point of $E$. For each $x\in A$, we have $B_r^{\ast}(x)\cap E = \varnothing$ for some $r>0$. Then $B_r(x)$ contains at most one point of $E$. Thus no finite subcollection of $\lbrace B_{r_x}(x) : x\in A\rbrace$ can cover $E$; the same is true of $A$, since $E\subseteq A$. However, since $\lbrace B_{r_x}(x) : x\in A\rbrace$ is an open cover of $A$, the compactness of $A$ guarantees the existence of a finite subcover of $A$; this is a contradiction.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 2.2.12.** If $\lbrace I_n \rbrace$ is a sequence of intervals in $\mathbb R$, such that $I_{n+1}\subseteq I_n$ for $n=1,2,3,\dots$ , then

$$ \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} I_n \ne \varnothing.$$
