A great deal of analysis on $\mathbb R$, $\mathbb C$, and $\mathbb R^k$ depends not on their full algebraic structure, but only on the notion of distance between points. This suggests isolating the notion of distance from the particular nature of the points themselves.
Accordingly, let $M$ be a nonempty set. We temporarily forget what its elements are and retain only a function $d:M\times M\to\mathbb R,$ where $d(x,y)$ is intended to represent the distance between $x$ and $y$.
Not every such function can reasonably be interpreted as a distance. A distance should be nonnegative; distinct points should have positive distance from one another; the distance from $x$ to $y$ should be the same as the distance from $y$ to $x$; and traveling directly from $x$ to $z$ should be no longer than traveling from $x$ to $z$ through an intermediate point $y$. These requirements are abstracted into the following axioms.

**Definition 2.1.1.** Suppose that $M$ is a nonempty set. A function $d:M^2\to \mathbb R$ is called a *metric* on $M$ if, for all $x,y,z\in M$,
1. $0\le d(x,y),$
2. $d(x,y)=0$ if and only if $x=y,$
3. $d(x,y)=d(y,x),$
4. $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z).$
{:marker-prefix="M" }

Then, the set $M$ with $d$ is called a *metric space*.

**Example 2.1.2.** The fundamental example is Euclidean space. For $k\in\mathbb N_{>0}$, define $d:(\mathbb R^k)^2\to\mathbb R$ by

$$d(x,y)=\|x-y\|.$$

The usual properties of the Euclidean norm imply that $d$ satisfies the metric axioms, so $(\mathbb R^k,d)$ is a metric space.
Moreover, if $S\subseteq\mathbb R^k$, then the restriction $d\vert{}_{S\times S}:S\times S\to\mathbb R$ is again a metric. Hence every subset of Euclidean space becomes a metric space when equipped with the restricted Euclidean metric. 

It will be useful to think of a metric space as being mainly about two basic relations: the membership relation and the distance relation. From this perspective, the most fundamental objects in a metric space are balls. 

**Definition 2.1.3.** Let $M$ be a metric space, let $a\in M$, and let $r>0$. The *open ball* of radius $r$ centered at $a$ is the set $B_r(a)$ defined by

$$B_r(a) = \lbrace x\in M :  d(x,a)<r\rbrace.$$

The *closed ball* of radius $r$ centered at $a$ is the set $\overline B_r(a)$ defined by

$$\overline B_r(a) = \lbrace x\in M :  d(x,a)\le r\rbrace.$$

The *punctured open ball* of radius $r$ centered at $a$ is the set $B^{\ast}_r(a)$ defined by

$$B^{\ast}_r(a) = \lbrace x\in M :  0<d(x,a)<r\rbrace.$$


In metric spaces, the notion of distance induces several local relations between a point $x$ and a set $A$. Firstly, with respect to how sufficiently small balls around $x$ lie entirely on one side of $A$ and $A^c$, there are three mutually exclusive possibilities: $x$ may be an interior point, an exterior point, or a boundary point of $A$.

**Definition 2.1.4.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M$. Interior and exterior points are the cases where there is some sufficiently small region around $x$ that lies completely on one side of $A$. $x$ is an *interior point* of $A$ if $B_r(x)\subseteq A$ for some $r>0$. Conversely, $x$ is an *exterior point* of $A$ if $B_r(x)\subseteq A^c$ for some $r>0$.
The remaining possibility is that no ball centered at $x$ lies entirely in $A$ or entirely in $A^c$. Equivalently, $B_r(x)\cap A \ne \varnothing$ and $B_r(x) \cap A^c\ne \varnothing$ for all $r>0$. Such a point $x$ is called a *boundary point* of $A$.
$\operatorname{int} A$ denotes the *interior* of $A$, the set of all interior points of $A$, $\operatorname{ext} A$ denotes the *exterior* of $A$, the set of all exterior points of $A$, and $\partial A$ denotes the *boundary* of $A$, the set of all boundary points of $A$.

**Theorem 2.1.5.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M.$ Every point of $M$ is either an interior point of $A,$ an exterior point of $A,$ or a boundary point of $A,$ and hence

$$M = \operatorname{int}A \ \dot{\cup} \ \partial A\  \dot{\cup} \ \operatorname{ext}A.$$

*Proof.* If $x\in \operatorname {int} A,$ then $B_r(x)\subseteq A$ for some $r>0.$ Since $x\in \operatorname{int} A$ implies $x\in A,$ $B_r(x)$ does not meet $A^{c}.$ Hence $\operatorname{int} A \cap \partial A = \varnothing.$ If $0<s<r$ then $B_s(x)\subseteq B_r(x)\subseteq A.$ If $s>r$ then $B_r(x)\subseteq B_s(x)$ so that $B_s(x)\cap A\ne \varnothing.$ Hence $\operatorname{int} A \cap \operatorname{ext} A = \varnothing.$ Lastly, since $x\in \partial A$ implies $x\notin \operatorname{ext} A,$ $\partial A \cap \operatorname{ext} A = \varnothing.$ Therefore $\operatorname{int} A, \partial A,$ and $\operatorname{ext} A$ are mutually disjoint.
We claim that $\partial A \cup \operatorname{ext} A = \left(\operatorname{int} A\right)^{c}.$ Indeed, if $x\in \partial A$ or $x\in \operatorname{ext} A$ then $x\notin \operatorname{int} A$, because $\operatorname{int} A, \partial A,$ and $\operatorname{ext} A$ are mutually disjoint. Therefore $\partial A \cup \operatorname{ext} A \subseteq \left( \operatorname{int} A\right) ^{c}.$ Conversely, suppose $x\notin \operatorname{int} A,$ which means that every ball around $x$ meets $A^{c}.$ There may exist some $r>0$ such that $B_r(x)\subseteq A^c,$ which means $x\in \operatorname{ext} A.$ Otherwise, no such $r$ exists, which means that every ball around $x$ meets $A.$ Thus $x\in \partial A.$ Therefore $\left(\operatorname{int} A\right)^{c}\subseteq \partial A \cup \operatorname{ext} A.$ It is concluded that $\partial A \cup \operatorname{ext} A = \left(\operatorname{int} A\right)^{c}.$ <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Closure point and limit point answer a somewhat different question: can $A$ be completely avoided by restricting our attention to a sufficiently small distance around $x$?

**Definition 2.1.6.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M$, and let $x\in M$. $x$ is a *closure point* of $A$ if $B_r(x)\cap A\neq\varnothing$ for all $r>0$. In words, no matter how tightly we localize around $x$, $x$ can never get completely away from $A$. The *closure* of $A$ is the set of all closure points of $A$, denoted by $\overline A$. This includes every point $x\in A$ automatically. 
A limit point deliberately removes this trivial possibility by puncturing the ball: $x$ is a *limit point* of $A$ if $B_r^{\ast}(x)\cap A\neq\varnothing$ for all $r>0$. The *derived set* of $A$ is the set of all limit points of $A$, denoted by $A'$.

**Remark 2.1.7.** Since every ball $B_r(x)$ contains $x$ and hence nonempty, no $x\in M$ can be an interior point, boundary point, limit point, or closure point of $\varnothing$. On the other hand, every $x\in M$ is an exterior point of $\varnothing$. 
In the case of $M$, every $x\in M$ is an interior point and closure point of $M$, while no $x\in M$ can be a boundary point or an exterior point of $M$.

**Proposition 2.1.8.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M.$ Then the following hold.
1. $\overline A = M \setminus \operatorname{ext} A$.
2. $\overline A = A\cup A'$.
3. $\operatorname{int} A \subseteq A \subseteq \overline A$.

*Proof.* (1) If $x\in \overline A,$ then every ball meets $A.$ Hence no balls lie entirely in $A^{c},$ so that $x\notin \operatorname{ext} A.$ If $x\notin \operatorname{ext} A,$ then every ball meets $A$ so that $x\in \overline A.$ Hence $\overline A = M \setminus \operatorname{ext} A.$
(2) Since $B_r(x) = B_r^{\ast}(x) \cup \lbrace x\rbrace,$ every ball around $x$ meets $A$ if and only if every punctured ball meets $A$ or $\lbrace x\rbrace$ meets $A.$ Hence $\overline A = A\cup A'.$
(3) If $x\in \operatorname{int} A,$ then $B_r(x)\subseteq A$ for some $r>0.$ Since $x\in B_r(x),$ $x\in A,$ so that $\operatorname{int} A \subseteq A.$ (2) immediately yields $A \subseteq \overline A.$ Therefore $\operatorname{int} A \subseteq A \subseteq \overline A.$<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

&emsp; As a simple application of closure, consider a bounded subset of $\mathbb R$. Its supremum need not belong to the set, but it can never be separated from it.

**Theorem 2.1.9.** Let $A$ be a nonempty subset of $\mathbb R$ which is bounded above, and let $\alpha = \sup A.$ Then $\alpha \in \overline A$. 

*Proof.* Since $\alpha$ is the supremum for $A$, there exists $x\in A$ such that $\alpha - r<x\le \alpha$ for every $r>0$. Hence $B_r(\alpha)$ has a point of $A$ for every $r>0$. Therefore $\alpha$ is a closure point of $A$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 2.1.10.** Let $M$ be a metric space, and let $A\subseteq M$. If $a$ is a limit point of $A$, then every open ball of $a$ has infinitely many points of $A$.

*Proof.* Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists an punctured open ball of $a$ which contains only a finite number of points of $A$. Since $a$ is a limit point of $A$, we may let $p_1,\ldots,p_n$ be those points, which are distinct from $a$. We now define

$$
r=\min_{1\leq i\leq n} d(a,p_i),  
$$

and it is clear that $r>0$. Then, $B_r^{\ast}(a)$ contains no point of $A$. Thus $a$ is not a limit point of $A$, which contradicts our premise.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Corollary 2.1.11.** Let $M$ be a metric space, and let $A\subseteq M$. If $A$ is finite, then $A$ has no limit points.

So far, our notions have described the "local" relations of an individual point to a set. We now turn these pointwise relations into "global" properties of a set as a whole. 
Recall that 

$$ \operatorname{int} A \subseteq A \subseteq \overline A. $$

The set $A$ is open when the first inclusion is an equality, and closed when the second inclusion is an equality.

**Definition 2.1.12.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq M$. $A$ is *open* if every point of $A$ is an interior point of $A$. Equivalently, 

$$
\mathmakebox[9em][r]{A \text{ is open}}
\mathmakebox[3em][c]{\iff}
\mathmakebox[9em][l]{\operatorname{int} A = A.}
$$

$A$ is *closed* if it contains all of its limit points. Equivalently,

$$
\mathmakebox[9em][r]{A \text{ is closed}}
\mathmakebox[3em][c]{\iff}
\mathmakebox[9em][l]{\overline A = A.}
$$

**Theorem 2.1.13.** Every open ball is open.

*Proof.* Let $M$ be a metric space. Then let $a\in M$, $r>0$, and define $A=B_r(a).$ If $x\in A$ then $d(x,a)<r.$ We choose $s>0$ so that $s<r-d(x,a).$ Since, for any $y\in M,$

$$ d(y,x)<s \quad \Longrightarrow \quad d(y,a)\le d(y,x)+d(x,a)<s+d(x,a)<r,$$

we have $B_s(x)\subseteq A.$ Hence $A$ is open. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 2.1.14.** Let $M$ be a metric space, and let $A\subseteq M$. $A$ is open if and only if $A^c$ is closed.

*Proof.* Suppose that $A$ is open and $x\in A.$ Then we can choose $r>0$ so that $B_r(x)\subseteq A.$ It follows that $B^{\ast}_r(x)\cap A^c = \varnothing,$ so $x$ is not a limit point of $A^c.$ Hence every limit point of $A^c$ is a member of $A^c,$ and thus $A^c$ is closed.
Conversely, suppose that $A^c$ is closed and let $x\in A.$ Then $x$ is not a limit point of $A^c.$ Hence we can choose $r>0$ so that $B^{\ast}_r(x)\cap A^c=\varnothing.$ It follows that $B_r(x)\subseteq A.$ Therefore $A$ is open.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 2.1.15.** Let $M$ be a metric space.
1. If $\lbrace A_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ is a collection of open sets, then $\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i$ is open.
2. If $\lbrace A_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ is a collection of closed sets, then $\bigcap_{i\in I } A_i$ is closed.
3. If $\lbrace A_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ is a finite collection of open sets, then $\bigcap_{i\in I } A_i$ is open.
4. If $\lbrace A_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ is a finite collection of closed sets, then $\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i$ is closed.

*Proof.* (1) Let $x\in \bigcup_{i\in I } A_i.$ Then we may let $x\in A_i$ for some $i\in I.$ Since $x$ is an interior point of $A_i$, $x$ is also an interior point of $\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i$. Therefore $\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i$ is open.
(2) By Theorem 2.1.14, $A_i^c$ is open for every $i\in I.$ Then $\bigcup_{i\in I } A^c_i$ is open by (1). Since

$$\bigcup_{i\in I } A^c_i = \lowparen{\bigcap_{i\in I } A_i}^{\!\!c},$$

$\bigcap_{i\in I } A_i$ is closed by Theorem 2.1.14.
(3) With the standard convention $\bigcap_{\varnothing}A_i = M$, if $I=\varnothing$ then $\bigcap_{\varnothing}A_i$ is open. Suppose $I\ne \varnothing$ and $x\in \bigcap_{i\in I } A_i.$ Then we have $x\in A_i$ for every $i\in I$. Since $A_i$ is open, there exists $r_i>0$ such that $B_{r_i}(x)\subseteq A_i.$ Since $I$ is finite, we may let

$$ r = \min_{i\in I}r_i.$$

Then $B_r(x)\subseteq \bigcap_{i\in I } A_i$. Hence $\bigcap_{i\in I } A_i$ is open.
(4) By Theorem 2.1.14, $A_i^c$ is open for every $i\in I.$ Then $\bigcap_{i\in I } A^c_i$ is open by (1). Since

$$\bigcap_{i\in I } A^c_i = \lowparen{\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i}^{\!\!c},$$

$\bigcup_{i\in I } A_i$ is closed by Theorem 2.1.14.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 2.1.16.** Let $M$ be a metric space, and let $A\subseteq M$. Then, the closure of $A$ is closed. Furthermore, the closure is the smallest closed superset of $A$.

*Proof.* We show that $x\notin \overline A$ is not a limit point of $\overline A$. If $x$ is not a closure point of $A$, then there exists $r>0$ such that $B_r(x)\cap A = \varnothing.$ If $s=r/2$, then every $y\in B_s(x)$ is not a limit point of $A$. Hence $B_s(x) \cap \overline A = \varnothing$. Consequently, every limit point of $\overline A$ is in $\overline A,$ and hence $\overline A$ is closed. 
We now prove that $\overline A$ is the smallest closed superset of $A$. Let $X$ be a closed superset of $A$, and let $x\in \overline A$. Then every ball of $x$ meets $A$, so it meets $X$. Hence $x$ is a closure point of $X$. Since $X$ is closed, $x\in X$. Consequently, every closed superset of $A$ contains $\overline A$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Until now, openness of a set has always been measured in the ambient space $M$. However, when a subset $E\subseteq M$ is regarded as a metric space in its own right, only points that remain in $E$ should be taken into account.

**Definition 2.1.17.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq E \subseteq  M.$ $A$ is *open relative to* $E$ if, for all $x\in A$, there exists $r>0$ such that

$$ B_r(x)\cap E \subseteq A.$$

**Theorem 2.1.18.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $A\subseteq E\subseteq M.$ Then, $A$ is open relative to $E$ if and only if there exists an open set $X$ such that

$$ X \cap E = A.$$

*Proof.* Suppose that $A$ is open relative to $E$ and $x\in A$. Define $B_{r_x}(x)$ to be an open ball whose intersection with $E$ is contained in $A$, then define

$$ X = \bigcup_{x\in A}B_{r_x}(x).$$

Since every open ball is open, by Theorem 2.1.15, $X$ is open. It is clear that $X\cap E\subseteq A$ by the definition of $X$. Since $x\in B_{r_x}(x)$ and $x\in E$, we also have $A\subseteq X \cap E.$ Therefore $X\cap E=A$.
Conversely, suppose that a set $X$ is open and $X \cap E=A.$ If $x\in A$ then $x\in X$ so that $x$ has an open ball $B_{r_x}(x)$ contained in $X$. Since the intersection of $X$ with $E$ is $A$, the intersection of a subset of $X$ with $E$ is contained in $A$. Therefore $B_{r_x}(x)\cap E \subseteq A$, so that $A$ is open relative to $E$. <span class="qed">$\square$</span>
