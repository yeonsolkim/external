**Definition 3.4.1.** Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $\mathbb R$. If for every $y\in \mathbb R$ there is $n_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$ such that 

$$n\ge n_0\ \Longrightarrow \ x_n\ge y,$$ 

then we write $x_n\to +\infty$. Similarly, if for every $y\in \mathbb R$ there is $n_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$ such that 

$$n\ge n_0 \ \Longrightarrow \  x_n\le y,$$ 

then we write $x_n\to -\infty$.

**Definition 3.4.2.** Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $\mathbb R$. For each $n$, define $s_k$ and $t_k$ to be the supremum and infimum for $\{x_n : n\ge k\}$ taken in $\overline{\mathbb R}$, respectively. The *upper limit* and *lower limit* of $(x_n)$ are defined, respectively, by

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}{x_n}
=
\inf_{\overline{\mathbb R}}{\{s_k:k\ge1\}}
\quad \text{and} \quad 
\liminf_{n\to\infty}x_n
=
\sup_{\overline{\mathbb R}}{\{t_k:k\ge1\}}.
$$

**Theorem 3.4.3.** Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $\mathbb R$. Define $A$ to be the set of numbers $y\in \overline {\mathbb R}$ such that $x_{n_k}\to y$ for some subsequence $(x_{n_k})$. Then 

$$\limsup_{n\to\infty}x_n=\max A\quad \text{and} \quad \liminf_{n\to\infty}x_n=\min A.$$

*Proof.* Let $s_k = \sup {\{x_n : n\ge k\}}$ and $L = \limsup_{n\to\infty}x_n$. We first suppose $L\in \mathbb R$. We show that no subsequential limit can be greater than $L$. Suppose $x_{n_j}\to y$ and fix $k$. Since $n_j\to \infty$, there is $j_0$ such that $j\ge j_0$ implies $n_j\ge k$. It follows that $x_{n_j}\le s_k$.
If $y\in \mathbb R$, then for any $\varepsilon>0$, there is $j_0'$ such that $j\ge j_0'$ implies $|x_{n_j}-y|<\varepsilon$. Taking maximum of $j_0$ and $j_0'$, eventually $y-\varepsilon < s_k$. Since $y$ is the supremum for $\{y-\varepsilon : \varepsilon > 0\}$ and $s_k$ is an upper bound, $y\le s_k$.
If $y = +\infty$, then for any $N>0$, there exists $j_0'$ such that $j\ge j_0'$ implies $x_{n_j}\ge N$. Taking maximum, eventually $N\le x_{n_j}\le s_k$, which implies $s_k=+\infty$. Hence $y\le s_k$.
If $y=-\infty$, then automatically $y\le s_k$.
Consequently, $y\le s_k$. Since $k$ was arbitrary, $y\le \inf_{k\ge 1} {s_k} = L$.
Furthermore, $L$ is itself a subsequential limit. Indeed, since $L$ is the infimum for $\{s_k:k\ge 1\}$, for each $j\ge 1$, we can choose $k_j$ such that $s_{k_j}<L+\frac1j$. Since $s_{k_j}$ is the supremum for $\{x_n:n\ge k_j\}$, there exists $n_j\ge k_j$ such that $x_{n_j}>s_{k_j}-\frac 1 j$. Then, $s_{k_j}\ge L$ implies $x_{n_j}>L-\frac 1 j$. It follows that

$$ L-\frac1j<x_{n_j}\le s_{k_j}<L+\frac1j. $$

Rearranging these indices so that $n_1<n_2<\cdots$, we have $x_{n_j}\to L$. 
Consequently, $L$ is an upper bound for $A$ and $L\in A$. Hence $L = \max A$.
We now check the infinite cases. If $L=+\infty$, every $\{x_n : n\ge k\}$ is unbounded above. We can choose $n_j>n_{j-1}$ such that $x_{n_j}>j$, giving $x_{n_j}\to+\infty$. If $L=-\infty$, the suprema of $\{x_n : n\ge k\}$ satisfy $s_k\to-\infty$, which means the entire sequence tends to $-\infty$.
Therefore, in all cases, $L=\max A$.
In the case of the lower limit, the proof is analogous.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>
