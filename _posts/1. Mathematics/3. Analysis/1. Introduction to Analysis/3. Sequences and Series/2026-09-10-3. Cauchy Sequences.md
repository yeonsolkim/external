Convergence describes the asymptotic behavior of a sequence by relating its terms to a point of the ambient space. This raises a natural question. How much of this asymptotic behavior can be recognized from the terms of the sequence themselves, without first specifying or establishing the existence of such a point?
The Cauchy condition isolates precisely this internal aspect of convergence. Instead of comparing the terms $x_n$ with a separate point $x$, it compares sufficiently late terms with one another and asks whether they eventually become arbitrarily close.

**Definition 3.3.1.** Let $M$ be a metric space and let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $M$. The sequence $(x_n)$ is called a *Cauchy sequence* if for any $\varepsilon >0$, there exists $n_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$ such that 

$$ m,n\ge n_0\ \Longrightarrow \ d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon.$$



This condition can be viewed geometrically as saying that the tails of the sequence eventually become arbitrarily small as sets. To make this precise, we introduce the diameter of a set.

**Definition 3.3.2.** Let $A$ be a nonempty subset of a metric space $M$, and let $S$ be the set of all reals of the form $d(x,y)$ where $x,y\in A$. The supremum of $S$ is called the *diameter* of $A$, denoted by $\operatorname {diam}{A}$.

**Theorem 3.3.3.** Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in a metric space $M$, and let $A_k = \{x_n\}_{n\ge k}.$ Then, $(x_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence if and only if 

$$ \lim\limits_{k\to \infty} {\operatorname {diam}{A_k}} = 0.$$

*Proof.* Let $a_k = \operatorname {diam}{A_k}$. Suppose $(x_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence, and let $\varepsilon>0$ be given. Then, there is $n_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$ such that $m,n\ge n_0$ implies $d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon /2$. Hence the set of the reals of the form $d(x_m,x_n)$ is bounded above, and the supremum is $a_{n_0}$. Therefore, we have $a_{n_0} <\varepsilon$, which implies $a_k\to 0$.
Conversely, suppose $a_k\to 0$, and let $\varepsilon>0$ be given. Then, there is $k_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$ such that $a_{k_0}<\varepsilon$. That is, if $m,n\ge k_0$ then 

$$d(x_m,x_n)<a_{k_0}\le \varepsilon.$$

Thus $(x_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 3.3.4.** Let $M$ be a metric space. Then the following hold.
1. Let $A$ be a subset of $M$. Then, $\operatorname {diam} {\overline A} = \operatorname {diam} {A}$.
2. Let $(K_n)$ be a sequence of nonempty compact sets in $M$ such that $K_{n+1}\subseteq K_n$ for $n=1,2,3\ldots$. If 
   
   $$\lim\limits_{n\to \infty} {\operatorname {diam} {K_n}} = 0,$$
   
   then $\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} K_n$ consists of one point.

*Proof.* (1) Let $\varepsilon>0$ be given, and let $x,y\in \overline A$. Then there exist points $x',y'\in A$ such that $d(x,x')<\varepsilon$ and $d(y,y')<\varepsilon$. Hence, we have 

$$ \begin{aligned}
d(x,y) &\le d(x,x') + d(x',y') + d(y',y)\\
&< d(x',y') + 2\varepsilon\\
&\le \operatorname {diam}{A} + 2\varepsilon.
\end{aligned} 
$$

It follows that $d(x,y)<\operatorname {diam} {A}$. Therefore $\operatorname {diam}{\overline A} \le \operatorname {diam} {A}$. Since $A\subseteq \overline A$, it is clear that $\operatorname {diam} {A} \le \operatorname {diam} {\overline A}$. Consequently, $\operatorname {diam} {\overline A} = \operatorname {diam} {A}$.
(2) By Corollary 2.2.12, $\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} K_n$ is nonempty. If $x,y \in \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} K_n$ with $x\ne y$, then $\operatorname {diam}{K_n}>d(x,y)>0$ for every $n\in \mathbb N^{+}$. This contradicts $\lim\limits_{n\to \infty} {\operatorname {diam} {K_n}} = 0$. Therefore $\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} K_n$ consists of exactly one point.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

The preceding results describe the Cauchy condition entirely through the tails of the sequence: their diameters tend to zero. We now ask whether this internal asymptotic behavior is enough to produce convergence. Every convergent sequence is necessarily Cauchy, but the converse requires something from the ambient space. Compactness provides one condition under which this can be guaranteed.

**Theorem 3.3.5.** Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in a metric space $M$.
1. If $(x_n)$ converges in $M$, then $(x_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence.
2. If $M$ is compact and $(x_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence, then $(x_n)$ converges in $M$.

*Proof.* (1) Suppose $(x_n)$ converges to $x\in M$ and let $\varepsilon>0$ be given. Then, there is $n_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$ such that $n\ge n_0$ implies $d(x_n,x)<\varepsilon /2$. By the triangle inequality, it follows that 

$$ m,n\ge n_0 \ \Longrightarrow \ d(x_m,x_n)< \varepsilon.$$

Hence $(x_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence.
(2) Let $A_k = \{x_n\}_{n\ge k}$. Then Theorem 3.3.3 and (1) of Theorem 3.3.4 gives

$$ \lim\limits_{k\to \infty} {\operatorname {diam}{\overline {A_k}}} = 0.$$

Since $M$ is compact, by Theorem 2.2.7, $\overline {A_k}$ is compact. Since $A_{k+1}\subseteq A_k$, we also have $\overline {A_{k+1}} \subseteq \overline {A_k}$. (2) of Theorem 3.3.4 now shows that there is a unique point $y\in \bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}{\overline{A_k}}$. 
Let $\varepsilon >0$ be given. Then, there is $k_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$ such that $k\ge k_0$ implies $\operatorname {diam}{\overline {A_k}}<\varepsilon$. Since $y\in \overline {A_k}$, if $z\in \overline {A_k}$ then $d(z,y)<\varepsilon$; hence $z\in A_k$ implies $d(z,y)<\varepsilon$. Since $x_n\in A_k$ if and only if $n\ge k$, it is concluded that $n\ge k_0$ implies $d(x_n,y)<\varepsilon$; this says that $x_n\to y$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Euclidean space is not compact, so Theorem 3.3.5 cannot be applied to it directly. However, every Cauchy sequence is bounded and is therefore contained in a compact $k$-cell. 

**Corollary 3.3.6.** Let $(\mathbf x_n)$ be a sequence in $\mathbb R^k$. Then, $(\mathbf x_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence  if and only if $(\mathbf x_n)$ converges in $\mathbb R^k$.

*Proof.* Let $A_m = \{\mathbf x_n\}_{n\ge m}$. Then we have $\operatorname {diam}{A_{m_0}}<1$ for some $m_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$. Since the image $\{\mathbf x_n\}_{n\in \mathbb N^{+}}$  is the union of $A_{m_0}$ and the finite set $\{\mathbf x_1,\ldots,\mathbf x_{m_0-1}\}$, $\{\mathbf x_n\}_{n\in \mathbb N^{+}}$ is bounded. Thus $\{\mathbf x_n\}_{n\in \mathbb N^{+}}\subseteq I$ for some $k$-cell $I$. Since $I$ is compact, Theorem 3.3.5 completes the proof.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

The argument in $\mathbb R^k$ reveals a distinction that is more fundamental than compactness itself. The Cauchy condition concerns the asymptotic relation among the terms of a sequence, whereas the assertion that every such sequence has a limit is a property of the ambient metric space. This motivates separating these two roles: a Cauchy sequence has the internal behavior required of a convergent sequence, and a complete space is one in which this behavior is *always* realized by an actual limit.

**Definition 3.3.7.** Let $M$ be a metric space. If every Cauchy sequence in $M$ converges in $M$, then $M$ is said to be *complete*.
