**Definition 3.1.1.** Let $M$ be a metric space. A sequence $(x_n)$ of points in $M$ is said to *converge* in $M$ if there is a point $x\in M$ with the following property: For any $r>0$, there exists $n_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$ such that

$$n\ge n_0\ \Longrightarrow\  x_n\in B_r(x).$$ 

In this case, we also say that $(x_n)$ *converges to* $x$, and we write $x_n\to x$ or $\lim\limits_{n\to \infty} x_n = x$.
If $(x_n)$ does not converge, it is said to *diverge*.

**Definition 3.1.2.** Let $M$ be a metric space. A sequence $(x_n)$ in $M$ is said to be *bounded* if the image of $(x_n)$ is bounded.

&emsp; Note that the boundedness of a subset of a metric space is not relative to a subspace when the subspace just carries the restricted metric. It therefore follows that the boundedness of a sequence is also not relative to subspace.
We now look into some important properties of convergent sequences in metric spaces.

**Theorem 3.1.3.** Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in a metric space $M$.

1. $(x_n)$ converges to $x\in M$ if and only if every open ball centered at $x$ contains $x_n$ for all but finitely many $n$.
2. For $x,y\in M$, if $(x_n)$ converges to $x$ and to $y$, then $x=y$.
3. If $(x_n)$ converges, then $(x_n)$ is bounded.
4. If $x$ is a limit point of $A\subseteq M$, then there exists a sequence $(x_n)$ in $A$ such that $x_n \to x$.

*Proof.* (1) Suppose $(x_n)\to x$ and let $B_r(x)$ be an open ball of $x$. Corresponding to this $r$, there exists $n_0\in \mathbb N^+$ such that $n\ge n_0$ implies $x_n\in B_r(x)$. Conversely, suppose that every open ball of $x$ contains $x_n$ for all but finitely many $n$. Fix $r>0$ and let

$$ \max {\{ n\in \mathbb N^+ : x_n\notin B_r(x)\}} < n_0.$$

Then $n\ge n_0$ implies $x_n\in B_r(x)$. Thus $x_n\to x$.
(2) Let $r>0$ be given, and define $n_{0_x}$ to satisfy

$$ n\ge n_{0_x}\ \Longrightarrow\  x_n\in B_r(x).$$

Similarly, define $n_{0_y}$ and let

$$ n_0 \ge \max {\{n_{0_x}, n_{0_y}\}}.$$

Then, we have

$$ n\ge n_0 \ \Longrightarrow\  x_n\in B_r(x)\cap B_r(y).$$

It follows that

$$ d(x,y) \le d(x_n,x) + d(x_n,y) < 2r. $$

Since $r$ was arbitrary, we conclude that $d(x,y)=0$; hence $x=y$.
(3) Suppose $x_n\to x$. There exists $n_0\in \mathbb N^+$ such that $n>n_0$ implies $x_n\in B_1(x)$. Put

$$ r = \max {\{1,d(x_1,x),\ldots,d(x_{n_0},x)\}}.$$

Then we have $x_n\in B_r(x)$ for all $n\in \mathbb N^+$; hence $(x_n)$ is bounded.
(4) For each $n\in \mathbb N^+$, there is a point $x_n\in A$ such that $x_n\in B_{1/n}(x)$. Given $r>0$, choose $n_0$ so that $1/n_0\le r$. Then, $n\ge n_0$ implies $x_n\in B_r(x)$; hence $x_n\to x$.

For sequences in $\mathbb R^k$, we can study the relation between convergence and the algebraic operations. We first consider sequences of complex numbers.

**Theorem 3.1.4.** Suppose $(x_n)$ and $(y_n)$ are sequences in $\mathbb C$, and suppose $\lim\limits_{n\to \infty} x_n = x$ and $\lim\limits_{n\to \infty} y_n = y.$ Then, the following hold:

1. $\lim\limits_{n\to \infty} (x_n + y_n) = x + y$.
2. $\lim\limits_{n\to \infty} cx_n = cx$.
3. $\lim\limits_{n\to \infty} x_ny_n = xy$.
4. $\lim\limits_{n\to \infty} (1/x_n) = 1/x,$ provided $x_n\ne 0$ and $x\ne 0$.
