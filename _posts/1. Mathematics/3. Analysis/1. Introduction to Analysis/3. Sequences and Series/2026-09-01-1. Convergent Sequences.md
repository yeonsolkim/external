**Definition 3.1.1.** Let $M$ be a metric space. A sequence $\{x_n\}$ of points in $M$ is said to *converge* in $M$ if there is a point $x\in M$ with the following property: For any $r>0$, there exists $n_0\in \mathbb N_{>0}$ such that 

$$n\ge n_0\ \Longrightarrow\  x_n\in B_r(x).$$ 

In this case, we also say that $\{x_n\}$ *converges to* $x$, and we write $x_n\to x$ or $\lim_{n\to \infty} x_n = x$.
If $\{x_n\}$ does not converge, it is said to *diverge*.

**Definition 3.1.2.** Let $M$ be a metric space, and let $A\subseteq M$. The set $A$ is said to be *bounded* if there exists $x\in M$ and $r>0$ such that 

$$ A\subseteq B_r(x).$$

&emsp; A sequence $\{x_n\}$ in $M$ is said to be *bounded* if the image of $\{x_n\}$ is bounded.

&emsp; Note that the boundedness of a subset of a metric space is not relative to a subspace when the subspace just carries the restricted metric. It therefore follows that the boundedness of a sequence is also not relative to subspace.
We now look into some important properties of convergent sequences in metric spaces.

**Theorem 3.1.3.** Let $\{x_n\}$ be a sequence in a metric space $M$. 

1. $\{x_n\}$ converges to $x\in M$ if and only if every open ball of $x$ contains $x_n$ all but finitely many $n$.
2. For $x,y\in M$, if $\{x_n\}$ converges to $x$ and to $y$, then $x=y$.
3. If $\{x_n\}$ converges, then $\{x_n\}$ is bounded.
4. If $x$ is a limit point of $E\subseteq M$, then there exists a sequence $\{x_n\}$ in $E$ such that $x_n \to x$.

*Proof.*
