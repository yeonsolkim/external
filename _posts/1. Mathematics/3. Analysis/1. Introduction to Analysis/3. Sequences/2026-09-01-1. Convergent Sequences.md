We now use the metric structure to describe the asymptotic behavior of an *ordered* family of points. To say that a sequence $(x_n)$ of points in $M$ converges to $x$ means that its terms eventually lie as close to $x$ as we wish.

**Definition 3.1.1.** Let $M$ be a metric space. A sequence $(x_n)$ of points in $M$ is said to *converge* in $M$ if there is a point $x\in M$ with the following property: For any $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $n_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$ such that

$$n\ge n_0\ \Longrightarrow\  d(x_n,x)<\varepsilon.$$ 

In this case, we also say that $(x_n)$ *converges to* $x$, or that $x$ is the *limit* of $(x_n)$, and we write $x_n\to x$ or $\lim_{n\to \infty} x_n = x$.
If $(x_n)$ does not converge, it is said to *diverge* in $M$.

As can be seen from the definition, convergence depends only on the *tail* of a sequence: changing finitely many initial terms does not affect either convergence or its limit.

**Definition 3.1.2.** Let $M$ be a metric space. A sequence $(x_n)$ in $M$ is said to be *bounded* if the range of $(x_n)$ is bounded.

Note that the boundedness of a subset of a metric space is not relative to a subspace when the subspace just carries the restricted metric. Thus the boundedness of a sequence is also not relative to a subspace.
We now look into some important properties of convergent sequences in metric spaces.

**Theorem 3.1.3.** Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in a metric space $M$.

1. $(x_n)$ converges to $x\in M$ if and only if every open ball centered at $x$ contains $x_n$ for all but finitely many $n$.
2. For $x,y\in M$, if $(x_n)$ converges to $x$ and to $y$, then $x=y$.
3. If $(x_n)$ converges in $M$, then $(x_n)$ is bounded.
4. If $x\in M$ is a limit point of $A\subseteq M$, then there exists a sequence $(x_n)$ in $A$ that converges to $x$.

*Proof.* (1) Suppose $(x_n)\to x$ and let $B_r(x)$ be an open ball of $x$. Corresponding to this $r$, there exists $n_0\in \mathbb N^+$ such that $n\ge n_0$ implies $x_n\in B_r(x)$. Conversely, suppose that every open ball of $x$ contains $x_n$ for all but finitely many $n$. Fix $r>0$ and let

$$ n_0 > \max {\{ n\in \mathbb N^+ : x_n\notin B_r(x)\}}.$$

Then $n\ge n_0$ implies $x_n\in B_r(x)$. Thus $x_n\to x$.
(2) Given $\varepsilon>0$, there exist $n_{0_x},n_{0_y} \in \mathbb N^+$ such that 

$$ n\ge n_{0_x} \Rightarrow d(x_n,x)<\frac \varepsilon 2 \quad \text{and} \quad n\ge n_{0_y} \Rightarrow d(x_n,y)<\frac \varepsilon 2.$$

If $n_0 = \max {\{n_{0_x}, n_{0_y}\}}$, then we have

$$ d(x,y) \le d(x_n,x) + d(x_n,y) < \varepsilon. $$

Since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, we conclude that $d(x,y)=0$; hence $x=y$.
(3) Suppose $x_n\to x$. There exists $n_0\in \mathbb N^+$ such that $n>n_0$ implies $x_n\in B_1(x)$. Put

$$ \varepsilon = \max {\{d(x_1,x),\ldots,d(x_{n_0},x)\}} + 1.$$

Then we have $d(x_n,x)<\varepsilon$ for all $n\in \mathbb N^+$; hence $(x_n)$ is bounded.
(4) For each $n\in \mathbb N^+$, there is a point $x_n\in A$ such that $d(x_n,x)<1/n$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $n_0$ so that $1/n_0\le \varepsilon$. Then, $n\ge n_0$ implies $d(x_n,x)<\varepsilon$; hence $x_n\to x$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Definition 3.1.4.** A sequence $(x_n)$ in $\mathbb R$ is said to be *monotonically increasing* if $x_n\le x_{n+1}$ for $n=1,2,3,\ldots$, and is said to be *monotonically decreasing* if $x_n\ge x_{n+1}$ for $n=1,2,3,\ldots$.
The sequence $(x_n)$ is said to be *monotonic* if it is monotonically increasing or monotonically decreasing.
We often write $x_n\uparrow x$ if $(x_n)$ is monotonically increasing and $(x_n)$ converges to $x$. Similarly, we write $x_n\downarrow x$ if $(x_n)$ is monotonically decreasing and $(x_n)$ converges to $x$.

**Theorem 3.1.5.** Suppose $(x_n)$ is a monotonic sequence in $\mathbb R$. Then, $(x_n)$ converges in $\mathbb R$ if and only if it is bounded.

*Proof.* Suppose $x_n\le x_{n+1}$ for $n=1,2,3,\ldots$ and let $\alpha$ be the supremum for $\{x_n\}_{n\in \mathbb N^{+}}$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, by lemma 1.1.10, there is $n_0\in \mathbb N^{+}$ such that $x_{n_0}\in (\alpha-\varepsilon,\alpha]$. Then, $n\ge n_0$ implies $|\alpha-x_n|<\varepsilon$; hence $(x_n) \to \alpha$.
The converse follows from (3) of Theorem 3.1.3.
In the other case, where $(x_n)$ is monotonically decreasing, the proof is analogous.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

For $\mathbb C$ with the usual metric $d(x,y)=|x-y|$, taking limits is compatible with its field operations, as the following theorem shows.

**Theorem 3.1.6.** Suppose $(x_n)$ and $(y_n)$ are sequences in $\mathbb C$. If $\lim{n\to \infty} x_n = x$ and $\lim{n\to \infty} y_n = y$, then the following hold:

1. $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to \infty} (x_n + y_n) = x + y$.
2. $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to \infty} cx_n = cx$ for any $c\in \mathbb C$.
3. $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to \infty} x_ny_n = xy$.
4. If $x_n\ne 0$ and $x\ne 0$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to \infty}{\frac{1}{x_n}} = \frac 1 x$.

*Proof.* (1) Given $\varepsilon>0$, there exist $n_{0_x},n_{0_y} \in \mathbb N^+$ such that 

$$ n\ge n_{0_x} \Rightarrow |x_n-x|<\frac \varepsilon 2 \quad \text{and} \quad n\ge n_{0_y} \Rightarrow |y_n-y|<\frac \varepsilon 2.$$

If $n_0 = \max {\{n_{0_x},n_{0_y}\}}$, then $n\ge n_0$ implies 

$$ | (x_n + y_n) - (x+y)| \le |x_n-x| + |y_n-y| < \varepsilon.$$

Since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, (1) holds.
(2) Given $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $n_0\in \mathbb N^+$ such that 

$$ n\ge n_0 \ \Longrightarrow \ |x_n-x|<\frac{\varepsilon}{|c|+1}.$$

Then, $n\ge n_0$ implies 

$$ |cx_n - cx| = |c||x_n-x|<\frac{|c|}{|c|+1}\varepsilon <\varepsilon.$$

Since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, (2) holds.
(3) Given $\varepsilon>0$, there exist $n_{0_x},n_{0_y}\in \mathbb N^+$ such that 

$$ n\ge n_{0_x} \Rightarrow |x_n-x|<\sqrt \varepsilon\quad \text{and} \quad n\ge n_{0_y} \Rightarrow |y_n-y|<\sqrt \varepsilon.$$

If $n_0 = \max {\{n_{0_x},n_{0_y}\}}$, then $n\ge n_0$ implies 

$$ |(x_n-x)(y_n-y)| = |x_n-x||y_n-y| < \varepsilon.$$

Thus we have $\lim{n\to \infty} (x_n-x)(y_n-y) = 0$. It follows that

$$ \lim\limits_{n\to \infty} (x_ny_n - xy) = \lim\limits_{n\to \infty} ((x_n-x)(y_n-y) + x(y_n-y) + y(x_n-x))=0.$$

Therefore

$$ \lim\limits_{n\to \infty} x_ny_n = \lim\limits_{n\to \infty} ((x_ny_n - xy)+xy) = xy.$$


(4) Since

$$\left|\frac {1}{x_n} - \frac{1}{x}\right| = \left|\frac{x_n-x}{x_nx}\right|,$$

what we have to show is to ensure that the denominators $x_n$ stay uniformly away from zero. To do that, we first prove the following lemma, so-called the reverse triangle inequality.

**Lemma 3.1.7.** In a metric space $(M,d)$, the following inequality holds:

$$ d(x,y)\ge |d(x,z) -d(y,z)|.$$

*Subproof.* If $d(x,z)\ge d(y,z)$, let $\delta=d(x,z)-d(y,z)$. In order that 

$$ d(x,y) + d(y,z) \ge d(x,z) = d(y,z) + \delta ,$$ 

$d(x,y)$ must be greater than or equal to $\delta$. That is, $d(x,y)\ge d(x,z)-d(y,z)$. If $d(y,z)\ge d(x,z)$, in the same way, we have $d(x,y)\ge d(y,z)-d(x,z)$. Therefore $d(x,y)\ge |d(x,z) -d(y,z)|$.<span class="qed">$\blacksquare$</span> 

Since $x\ne 0$, we have $d(x,0)>0$. Then, there exists $m\in \mathbb N^+$ such that $n\ge m$ implies $d(x_n,x)<\frac 1 2 d(x,0)$. By Lemma 3.1.7, we obtain

$$ d(x_n,0)\ge d(0,x) - d(x_n,x)>\frac 1 2 d(0,x).$$

whenever $n\ge m$. Equivalently, $|x_n| >\frac 1 2 |x|$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, there is $n_0>m$ such that $n\ge n_0$ implies 

$$ |x_n-x|<\frac 1 2 |x|^2 \varepsilon.$$

Hence, if $n\ge n_0$, then 

$$ \left|\frac {1}{x_n} - \frac{1}{x}\right| = \left|\frac{x_n-x}{x_nx}\right|<\frac{2}{|x|^2}|x_n-x|<\varepsilon.\tag*{$\square$}$$


Having established the limit laws in $\mathbb C$, we now take Euclidean space into consideration. Because the Euclidean norm is determined by finitely many coordinates, convergence in $\mathbb R^k$ can be tested one coordinate at a time. The next theorem makes this reduction precise.

**Theorem 3.1.8.** Suppose $(\mathbf x_n)$ is a sequence in $\mathbb R^k$ and $\mathbf x_n = (x_{1,n},\ldots,x_{k,n})$. Then, $(\mathbf x_n)$ converges to $\mathbf x = (x_1,\ldots,x_k)$ if and only if

$$ \lim\limits_{n\to \infty} x_{i,n} = x_i$$

for $i=1,2,\dots,k$.

*Proof.* Suppose $\mathbf x_n\to \mathbf x$. From the definition of the norm in $\mathbb R^k$, we have

$$ |x_{i,n}-x_i|\le \|\mathbf x_n - \mathbf x\|,$$

for each $i=1,2,\ldots,k$ and $n\in \mathbb N^+$. Hence, given $\varepsilon >0$, there exists $n_0\in \mathbb N^+$ such that $n\ge n_0$ implies $|x_{i,n}-x_i|<\varepsilon$. Therefore $x_{i,n}\to x_i$ for each $i=1,2,\ldots,k$.
Conversely, suppose $x_{i,n}\to x_i$ for each $i=1,2,\ldots,k$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $n_{0_i}\in \mathbb N^+$ such that $n\ge n_{0_i}$ implies 

$$|x_{i,n}-x_i| < \frac {\varepsilon}{\sqrt k}.$$

for each $i=1,2,\ldots,k$. Put $n_0 = \max {\{n_{0_1},\ldots,n_{0_k}\}}$. Then $n\ge n_0$ implies 

$$ \|\mathbf x_n - \mathbf x\| = \left\{\sum_{i=1}^k |x_{i,n}-x_i|^2\right\}^{\!1/2}<\varepsilon.$$

Since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $\mathbf x_n\to \mathbf x$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

We consider several limits that will be used repeatedly. The following results concern particular sequences in $\mathbb R$. Their proofs also illustrate how the order properties of $\mathbb R$, the binomial theorem, and comparison arguments can be used to establish convergence.

**Lemma 3.1.9.** Let $(x_n)$ and $(y_n)$ be sequences in $\mathbb R$. If $0\le x_n\le y_n$ eventually and $y_n\to 0$, then $x_n\to 0$.

*Proof.* Let $\varepsilon>0$ be given. Then eventually $y_n<\varepsilon$. Since $x_n\le y_n$, we have $0\le x_n <\varepsilon$ eventually. Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $x_n\to 0$.<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

**Theorem 3.1.10.**  
1. If $x>0$ then $\displaystyle \lim_{n\to \infty}{\frac {1}{n^x}} = 0$.
2. If $x>0$ then $\displaystyle \lim_{n\to \infty}{\sqrt[n]{x}} = 1$.
3. $\displaystyle \lim_{n\to \infty}{\sqrt[n]{n}} = 1$.
4. If $x>0$ and $y\in \mathbb R$ then $\displaystyle \lim_{n\to \infty}{\frac {n^y}{(1+x)^n}}=0.$
5. If $|x|<1$ then $\displaystyle \lim_{n\to \infty}{x^n} = 0$.

*Proof.* (1) Let $\varepsilon > 0$ be given. The Archimedean property of $\mathbb R$ guarantees the existence of $n_0\in \mathbb N^+$ such that $n_0 > \left(1/ {\varepsilon}\right)^{1/x}$. Then, $n\ge n_0$ implies $1/{n^x}<\varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $1/{n^x}\to 0$.
(2) If $x=1$ then it is trivial. Suppose $x>1$ and let $\sqrt[n]x = 1+\delta_n$. Then we have 

$$ (1 + \delta_n)^n = x.$$

Since

$$ 1 + n\delta_n < (1 + \delta_n)^n$$

by the binomial theorem, it follows that 

$$ 1 + n\delta_n < x.$$

Therefore 

$$ \delta_n < \frac {x-1}{n},$$

and thus $\delta_n\to 0$ by Lemma 3.1.9. This proves $\lim_{n\to \infty}{\sqrt[n]{x}} = 1$.
Since $\sqrt[n]x \sqrt[n]{1/x} = 1$, we have

$$\lim_{n\to \infty}{\sqrt[n]{\frac 1 x}} = \lim_{n\to \infty}{\frac{1}{\sqrt[n]x}} = \frac{1}{\lim_{n\to \infty}{\sqrt[n]{x}}} = 1.$$

This shows that (2) holds as well when $x<1$.
(3) Let $\sqrt[n]n = 1 + \delta_n$ where $\delta_n>0$. Then we have 

$$(1+\delta_n)^n = n.$$ 

If $n\ge 2$, then 

$$\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\delta_n^2<(1+\delta)^n$$

by the binomial theorem. It follows that 

$$ \frac{n(n-1)}{2}\delta_n^2< n.$$

Thus we have

$$ 0<\delta_n < \sqrt{\frac {2}{n-1}}.$$

Hence $\delta_n \to 0$, by Lemma 3.1.9.  Consequently, $\sqrt[n]{n} \to 1$.
(4) Let $k$ be an integer such that $k>y$ and $k>0$. If $n>2k$, then, by the binomial theorem,  

$$ (1+x)^n > \binom n k x^k = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!}x^k > \frac{n^k}{2^k k!}x^k.$$

It follows that 

$$ 0< \frac {n^y}{(1+x)^n} < \frac{2^k k!}{x^k n^{k-y}} $$

Since we have

$$ \frac{2^k k!}{x^k n^{k-y}} \to 0$$

by (1), Lemma 3.1.9 now gives

$$ \frac {n^y}{(1+x)^n} \to 0.$$


(5) If $x=0$ it is trivial. If $0<|x|<1$, then taking $y=0$ in (4) shows (5).<span class="qed">$\square$</span>

Taken together, these assertions describe several basic relations among powers, roots, and exponential sequences. Assertion (1) says that the reciprocal of every fixed positive power of $n$ tends to zero. Assertion (2) says that taking successively higher roots removes the scale of any fixed positive number: regardless of its original size, its $n$-th root approaches $1$. Assertion (3) shows that the same conclusion remains true even when the quantity under the root varies with $n$. Thus, although $n$ itself tends to infinity, its growth is not fast enough to produce an $n$-th root bounded away from $1$.
Assertions (4) and (5) describe two sides of exponential behavior. In (4), $n^y$ is a fixed power of $n$, whereas $(1+x)^n$ is an exponential sequence with fixed base greater than $1$. Both $x$ and $y$ are held fixed as $n\to\infty$. Therefore, no matter how close $1+x$ is to $1$, or how large $y$ is, the exponential term eventually dominates the power term. Assertion (5) gives the corresponding statement for exponential decay: repeated multiplication by a fixed number whose absolute value is less than $1$ forces the resulting sequence to zero.
