Derivatives. Derivatives derive their values from the values of the underlying instruments. Since sellers create derivatives when they first sell them, derivatives are in zero net supply. All derivatives have futurity: their values depend on future events. Almost all derivatives have an expiration date, on which traders make the final settlement and the contract expires. Derivatives include the following: First, Forward contracts: Forward contracts are contracts for the future sale of a commodity. Second, Futures: Futures are forward contracts guaranteed by an exchange clearinghouse. The clearinghouse acts as the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. Futures traders therefore need to consider only the creditworthiness of the clearinghouse. Third, Options: An option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to do something. Option contracts give their holders the right to buy or sell an underlying instrument at a fixed price. The trader who sells an option contract is called the writer, and the option is said to be written on the underlying instrument. A writer may write a call option, which gives the holder the right to buy at a fixed strike price, or a put option, which gives the holder the right to sell at a fixed strike price. In an option contract, the buyer, or holder, pays a premium to acquire this right. The writer receives the premium in exchange for assuming the contractual obligation if the buyer exercises the option. A call writer must sell the underlying instrument at the specified price, while a put writer must buy it at the specified price. American-style option: The holder can exercise the option at any time before the expiration date. European-style option: The holder can exercise the option only on the expiration date.
