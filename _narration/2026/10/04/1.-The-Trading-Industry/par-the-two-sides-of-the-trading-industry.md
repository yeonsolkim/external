The two sides of the trading industry. In a completely frictionless market where two people exchange goods directly, separate sell-side traders may be unnecessary. In real markets, however, traders face tasks such as finding counterparties, routing orders, quoting prices, making immediate execution possible, handling settlement, and providing financing. Specialized traders and institutions emerge to meet these needs. Exchange services provide the liquidity and immediacy that make trading possible. To understand today’s trading industry, it is useful to distinguish the trades themselves from the services that make them possible. For example, client A may place an order with a broker, who then finds another trader willing to take the other side and arranges the trade: A to broker to B.

In this case, the actual economic counterparties—the buyer and the seller—are A and B. The broker earns a commission from A. When a dealer is involved, on the other hand, the transaction may take the following form: A to dealer.

Here, the dealer acts as a principal and may take the assets into its own inventory. The dealer earns profits from its own trading. Many sell-side firms employ both brokers and dealers and are known as broker-dealers or wirehouses. Goldman Sachs is one example.
