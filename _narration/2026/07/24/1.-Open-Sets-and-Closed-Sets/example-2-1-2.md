Example 2.1.2. The fundamental example is Euclidean space. For k in the positive integers, define d from R k squared to R by

d of x comma y equals the norm of x minus y.

The usual properties of the Euclidean norm imply that d satisfies the metric axioms, so R k, d is a metric space. Moreover, if A is contained in R k, then the restricted metric from A times A to R is again a metric. Hence every subset of Euclidean space becomes a metric space when equipped with the restricted Euclidean metric. More generally, if M, d is any metric space and N is contained in M, then the restricted metric is a metric on N, called the restricted metric. With the restricted metric, N thus can be regarded as a metric space on its own right.
