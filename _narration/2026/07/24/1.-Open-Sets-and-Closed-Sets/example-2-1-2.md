Example 2.1.2. The fundamental example is Euclidean space. For k in the positive integers, define d from R k squared to R by d of x and y equals the norm of x minus y.

The usual properties of the Euclidean norm imply that d satisfies the metric axioms, so R k, d, is a metric space. Moreover, if A is contained in R k is nonempty, then the restriction d to A times A, from A times A to R, is again a metric. Hence every nonempty subset of Euclidean space becomes a metric space when equipped with the restricted Euclidean metric. More generally, if M, d, is any metric space and N is contained in M is nonempty, then d restricted to N times N is a metric on N, called the restricted metric. With the restricted metric, N thus can be regarded as a metric space on its own right.
